Musical Features

The music editor of the radio station wants upbeat songs at about 120 beats per minute, preferably in a minor key, for a new playlist. Features such as the short-time energy and the spectral centroid say how loud or how bright a piece is, but not which notes it plays, in which key, or how fast. Listeners hear these properties instantly, and music retrieval needs them for playlists, for finding cover versions of a song, and for searching by melody. This section covers the three most important musical features: pitch, chroma, and tempo. The extended methods, such as estimating several simultaneous pitches and tracking beats with dynamic programming, are in Extended Audio and Music Features.

Pitch, Harmonics, and Notes¶

An instrument produces a mixture of sine waves, its partials. In most instruments, the partials lie close to integer multiples of the lowest one, the fundamental f0f_0f0​: a note at f0=220f_0 = 220f0​=220 Hz has partials at 440, 660, 880 Hz, and so on. These multiples are the harmonics, and all partials above the fundamental are the overtones. The fundamental determines the pitch we hear. The relative strength of the overtones, and how it changes over the duration of a note, determines the timbre: a violin and a flute at the same pitch differ in their overtones.

Doubling the frequency raises a note by one octave, and notes an octave apart sound so similar that music gives them the same name. Western music divides the octave into 12 semitones with the pitch classes C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, and B. In equal temperament, each semitone multiplies the frequency by 21/12≈1.05952^{1/12} \approx 1.059521/12≈1.0595. The reference is the standard pitch A4 = 440 Hz, the A above middle C. Numbering all notes consecutively, as the MIDI standard for electronic instruments does, gives a single formula for every note.

Chroma¶

A chroma vector describes which pitch classes sound in a frame, regardless of the octave. Each bin kkk of the spectrum is assigned to the pitch class p(k)p(k)p(k) of its frequency f(k)f(k)f(k), and the energy of all bins with the same pitch class is summed:

h(t,p)=∑k : p(k)=p∣X(t,k)∣2∑k∣X(t,k)∣2,0≤p≤11h(t, p) = \frac{\sum_{k \,:\, p(k) = p} |X(t,k)|^2}{\sum_{k} |X(t,k)|^2}, \qquad 0 \le p \le 11h(t,p)=∑k​∣X(t,k)∣2∑k:p(k)=p​∣X(t,k)∣2​,0≤p≤11
(2)

The 12 values sum to 1. Since low notes lie only a few hertz apart, chroma needs long frames: the 40 ms frames of perceptual features resolve 25 Hz, which cannot separate neighboring notes below about 420 Hz, as computed in From Sound to Spectrogram. Chroma frames therefore typically last several hundred milliseconds, and the sum covers only bins in the range of musical notes. Chroma captures harmony, the chords and the key, and it is largely independent of the instrumentation, because a chord played by an orchestra and the same chord on a piano share their pitch classes. This makes chroma sequences the classic feature for chord recognition and for finding cover versions of a song.

Example: the key of the Brahms clip

The running music clip, 45.8 seconds of Brahms’s Hungarian Dance No. 5 played by a string orchestra, is analyzed with frames of 8192 samples (372 ms, a bin spacing of 2.7 Hz) over the note range from 55 Hz to 4000 Hz. Figure 2 shows the chroma averaged over the whole clip on one octave of a piano keyboard: the darker a key, the larger its share of the energy. D (0.20) and G (0.15) dominate, followed by A, C, and B♭ (0.10 each) and F♯ (0.08), while C♯, E, and G♯ get almost nothing.

A simple key estimator correlates this vector with a key profile, a template of 12 values that says how prominent each pitch class is in a key. The best match is G minor with a correlation of 0.76, ahead of G major (0.67) and D major (0.66). The filled markers show why: G minor has G as its tonic, B♭ as its minor third, D as its fifth, and F♯ as its leading tone, and all four are among the six darkest keys. Harmonics contribute as well: the third harmonic of every G is a D, which helps D rank even above G.

The title of the recording says “in F♯ minor”, the key of Brahms’s original version for piano. The hollow markers show that the fifth of F♯ minor, C♯, is one of the palest keys, and F♯ minor correlates with -0.04, rank 13 of the 24 major and minor keys. The string orchestra plays the dance in G minor, the key of Albert Parlow’s orchestration of 1876. The content contradicts the metadata: a title typed into a catalogue can be wrong, while the chroma is measured on the recording itself.

Fundamental Frequency¶

Chroma records all pitch classes in a frame, including those of the harmonics, but not the melody. For the melody, a system estimates the fundamental f0f_0f0​ of each frame. The idea of the classic frequency-domain method is a harmonic sum: for every candidate f0f_0f0​, add up the magnitudes of the spectrum at f0,2f0,3f0,…f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0, \dotsf0​,2f0​,3f0​,…, and choose the candidate that collects the most energy. Weighting the harmonics, with less weight for the higher ones, prevents the estimate from jumping to half or double the true pitch. A time-domain alternative uses the autocorrelation of the signal: a periodic signal resembles itself when shifted by one period, so the shift with the highest correlation gives 1/f01/f_01/f0​. Polyphonic music with several simultaneous notes requires estimating one fundamental, subtracting its harmonics from the spectrum, and repeating. Both methods and this iteration are described in Extended Audio and Music Features.

Tempo and Beats¶

The tempo of music is measured in beats per minute (bpm). Classical tempo markings range from Largo (40 to 60 bpm) over Andante (76 to 108 bpm) and Allegro (120 to 168 bpm) to Presto (168 to 200 bpm), and the tempo can change within a piece. Pop music usually keeps a constant tempo between 60 and 160 bpm, with 120 bpm the most common. At 100 bpm, a beat falls every 600 ms.

Beats show up as note onsets: sudden increases of energy in some frequency bands. The onset strength measures them with the positive part of the spectral flux on the log mel spectrum M(t,b)M(t,b)M(t,b) of Perceptual Features:

(3)

Example

The onset strength of the Brahms clip is computed with a hop of H=512H = 512H=512 samples at fs=22,050f_s = 22{,}050fs​=22,050 Hz, so one onset frame lasts 23.2 ms. Its autocorrelation peaks at l0=17l_0 = 17l0​=17, which gives 60⋅22,050/(512⋅17)=152.060 \cdot 22{,}050 / (512 \cdot 17) = 152.060⋅22,050/(512⋅17)=152.0 bpm, a beat every 395 ms. A second public recording, a 5.3-second jazz trumpet loop, was recorded at 90 bpm. Its strongest peak lies at lag 14, which gives 184.6 bpm, about twice the true tempo. A weaker peak at lag 27 gives 95.7 bpm, close to the recorded tempo. Such octave errors, at double, half, or a third of the true tempo, are the typical failure of tempo estimation, and listeners themselves often disagree whether a piece moves at 90 or at 180 bpm.

Beat tracking goes one step further and places the individual beats in time, the moments at which a listener would tap along. The beats should coincide with strong onsets and follow each other at intervals close to the estimated period, with small deviations allowed. Ellis (2007) formulated this trade-off as an objective that rewards onset strength at the chosen beat times and penalizes deviations from the period, and solved it with dynamic programming. Figure 3 shows the result for a ten-second excerpt: the onsets often fall on the beats, but also between them and with varying strength. The tempo prior and the dynamic program are described in Extended Audio and Music Features.
