The Web Search Landscape

The chapter opener named four problems: too many equal matches, diverse intents, no editor, and adversarial ranking. This section looks at them more closely and then at the two ends of a web search engine: the result page, which the engine composes for the intent of each query, and the crawler, which collects the pages and the evidence about them.

How Web Search Differs from Classical Retrieval¶

Five differences shape every design decision in web search:

aspectclassical retrievalweb search
collectioncontrolled and quality-assured, such as a product catalogue or a libraryno quality control; ranges from reference works to errors, propaganda, and spam
documentsshared structure, rich metadata, structured or faceted searchheterogeneous formats (HTML, PDF, scripts); beyond title, URL, and text, little reliable structure
queriesnarrow context, such as artists, albums, and songs in a music databaseany topic, two or three words, no explicit context; the engine must infer the intent
resultsmanageable result sizes, filters, facets, sortingbillions of matching pages for a short query; ranking decides everything
relationshipscurated relations with little extra informationa huge link graph whose topology reveals which pages people consider important

Classical retrieval assumes that every document in the collection deserves to be found and that its text describes it honestly. On the web, neither assumption holds.

Much low-quality content is not malicious. Pages that were correct when written become outdated, blogs are abandoned, and errors are never corrected. Other content is deliberately misleading: false information, scams that imitate banks or shops, and clickbait whose content does not deliver what its title promises. On top of this, much of the web exists in many copies. The manuals of popular products, the documentation of popular frameworks, and successful blog posts are mirrored and republished on many sites, and the same article is often reachable under several URLs. A ranking that only matches terms scores all copies equally, so the result list may show the same content ten times. In a library, a librarian filters all of this before a document enters the collection. On the web, the search engine must do it at ranking time, with the quality and spam signals of Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts.

Ranking on the web is also adversarial, because visibility is worth money. In a backlinko.com analysis of 4 million Google search results, the first organic result received an average click-through rate of 27.6%, and only 0.63% of searchers clicked on anything on the second page. A page that is not on the first page is practically invisible. Search engine optimization (SEO) is the discipline of improving a page’s position in the results. Much of it is legitimate and welcome to search engines, which publish guidelines for it: descriptive titles, a clean HTML structure, fast pages, and good content that others link to. Its other side manipulates the ranking signals directly, with keyword stuffing, hidden text, link farms, and cloaking, which serves the crawler different content than the user. No central authority decides which page deserves the top position. The ranking itself is the only arbiter, and many of the signals in this chapter were designed to be hard to fake.

Together, these differences shift the weight of the ranking away from the page text. A two-word query matches millions of pages that all look equally similar to the query, and the page text is exactly what an SEO controls. Signals that do not come from the page text, such as links, anchors, and user behaviour, therefore carry much of the ranking.

Parts of the web are invisible to a search engine. Pages behind logins, results of database queries, and content that single-page applications assemble in the browser with JavaScript are publicly reachable but hard or impossible to crawl. This deep web is many times larger than the indexed web. A crawler can reach a deep page only if some other page links to it directly, a so-called deep link.

What Users Search For¶

Andrei Broder classified web queries by the user’s goal into three types:

  • Navigational queries aim at one specific website or a section of it: “youtube”, “amazon prime”, “facebook login”. Bookmarks were designed for this need, but many users prefer to type the name into a search engine. There is usually exactly one correct result.

  • Transactional queries express the intent to do something, typically to buy, book, or download: “new coffee machine”, “trip to Hawaii”. The transactional intent is often implicit.

  • Informational queries seek knowledge about a topic: news, events, people, how-to instructions. Some are phrased as questions: “who won the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics”, “how to make mac and cheese”, “where to swim near Basel”.

The intent changes what a good result looks like. A navigational query is answered by one link at rank 1. An informational question may be answered best by a sentence on the result page itself, and a transactional query by a list of products to compare. Chapter 3 shows how a system detects the intent of a query and routes it to a backend. Broder’s three types are a coarse version of the routing intents there. A navigational query stays in the web index, with the strong expectation of a single result. Informational and transactional queries are often routed further, for example “Who was Albert Einstein?” to a people search, “where to swim near Basel” to a map search, and “new coffee machine” to a product search.

Most queries do not state their intent, and many are ambiguous. “apple” can mean the company, the fruit, or the record label; “jaguar” can mean the car or the animal. The engine cannot ask back, so it ranks for the interpretation that most users mean, which it learns from the clicks of earlier users. For “apple”, the home page of Apple comes first. Rarer interpretations receive a few positions further down, so that a user with a less common intent still finds something on the first page. This mix of interpretations is called result diversification.

Frequent navigational queries need no ranking computation at all. The correct target of “instagram” or “uni basel” is known and stable, and the engine can serve it from a curated or learned table of query-target pairs, much like the entries of an FAQ service. Here, a single wrong result is a failure: if the official site is not at rank 1, the quality of the remaining ranks does not matter.

Web queries are short. Figure 1 shows the share of search volume by query length: most search volume comes from queries of one to three words and around ten to twenty characters. Longer queries are more frequent among the distinct query strings than in the volume, because each long query is typed by few users.

Query frequencies follow Zipf’s law. A small set of queries, mostly navigational ones such as “amazon” or “netflix”, is repeated millions of times per month and forms the fat head of the search demand curve in Figure 2. In the chunky middle, queries reach thousands of searches per month. The long tail contains all the rest, each typed fewer than about a hundred times per month. In a 2020 analysis of 306 million keywords, backlinko.com found that 91.8% of all queries are long-tail queries with at most 100 searches per month, and that the median query is searched only 10 times per month. The head nevertheless carries a large share of the total volume: the 500 most popular queries alone account for 8.4% of all searches.

The long tail is where search engines compete. Head queries are easy: any engine can learn the right answer for “youtube” from the clicks of millions of users, or simply store it. Tail queries have little or no click history, and many are entirely new. Google reports that about 15% of the queries it sees every day have never been seen before, driven by news, new products, and the endless variety of how people phrase a question. About 14% of queries are phrased as questions (backlinko.com); “how” is the most common question word, followed by “what” and “where” (Figure 2, left). Many queries without a question word are implicit questions too: a person’s name usually means “who is ...”, and a product name often means “what does it cost”.

From Intent to Result Page¶

Early search engines treated every query the same way. A single retriever over an inverted index returned a list of links with ads in between, the “ten blue links” (Figure 3). For a question such as “who is the US president”, the user had to open a result, find the answer on the page, and leave the search engine, which could then no longer show them ads.

A modern engine produces the result page in three steps. It detects the intent of the query, sends the query to one or several specialized searches, and generates the elements of the page from their results. Each step keeps more of the user’s journey on the result page.

Intent detection and specialized searches. Besides the web index, a search engine runs vertical searches over collections of its own: images, videos, news, maps with local businesses, products with prices, and flights. The intent classifier decides which verticals to query and how to mix their results into the web results. Some intents need no search at all: weather, calculations, unit conversions, and translations are answered directly, like the one-shot vertical intents of Chapter 3.

Ranking and curated knowledge. The web results themselves are ordered by a learned rank model that combines hundreds of signals (Learning to Rank). For entities, the engine adds curated content. In 2012, Google launched its Knowledge Graph, a curated database of entities and facts from reliable sources. When a query names a known entity, a card next to the results shows the key facts directly (Figure 4). At launch, the Knowledge Graph held about 3.5 billion facts on 500 million entities; by 2020, Google reported 500 billion facts on 5 billion entities. Product and brand queries receive similar cards with prices and offers. This works only for facts and products in the provider’s knowledge base.

Snippets and highlighted passages. Snippets, the short text excerpts below each link, have existed since the early days. In January 2014, Google added featured snippets: for question-like queries, a passage extracted from one result is shown above all links. With BERT-based passage ranking, launched in 2021, the engine scores individual passages within long pages. This is the retriever-reader architecture of Chapter 7: a reader model marks the answer span in a retrieved document (Figure 5). Most browsers now support text fragments (“scroll to text”), so a click on the result jumps directly to the highlighted passage.

Generated answers. In February 2023, Microsoft added a chat assistant based on OpenAI’s GPT-4 to Bing (renamed Copilot later that year), the first major search engine to answer with retrieval-augmented generation (Chapter 7): the engine retrieves top results and a language model writes an answer with links to its sources (Figure 6). Google followed with AI Overviews, generated summaries above the results, in 2024 and with a conversational AI Mode in 2025. Generated answers are expensive, so search engines show them selectively, mainly for informational queries.

Related searches. Many searches are one step in a longer task. Related searches and related questions (“People also ask”) suggest the next step, derived from the query logs of other users with the same need.

Most of these components appeared within the last fifteen years, on top of a ranking core that dates from the late 1990s.

How web search engines evolved (optional reading)

In the 1990s, the web was small enough to be organized by hand. Yahoo started as a human-edited directory, a hierarchical catalogue of websites sorted into topics, and added keyword search later. AltaVista, launched in 1995, was one of the first engines to index the full text of a large part of the web. Its ranking relied on classical term weighting with some tweaks such as boosts for terms in the title or domain name. With a few million pages, this was good enough: queries matched few pages, and the best ones usually contained the query terms prominently.

Google grew out of a research project by Larry Page and Sergey Brin at Stanford and launched as a company in 1998. Three ideas set it apart. It ranked pages with link analysis (PageRank) and anchor text, which were much harder to manipulate than the page text. It optimized its ranking for the most common types of web queries instead of generic relevance. And it ran on a distributed architecture of cheap commodity machines, which let it crawl more pages and answer more queries than its competitors. Within a few years, Google dominated web search.

The 2000s added machine learning to the ranking (learning to rank, Learning to Rank), universal search that mixes images, news, videos, and maps into the result list, and the Knowledge Graph in 2012. The late 2010s brought transformer models: BERT for understanding queries and ranking results in 2019, and the ranking of individual passages within pages in 2021.

The 2020s brought generated answers. Microsoft’s integration of GPT-4 into Bing in 2023, Google’s AI Overviews in 2024, and chat-first assistants such as ChatGPT search and Perplexity changed what users expect from a search engine: an answer, not a list. Classical ranking remains at the core of these systems, because the generated answer is only as good as the retrieved results it is based on.

The Assembled Result Page¶

The result page (SERP, search engine results page) combines the outputs of all these components. Figure 7 shows a schematic for “uni basel”. An AI overview with source links sits at the top. Ads follow, marked as sponsored and ranked by bid and predicted relevance. The organic results are the ranked list of web pages with title, URL, and snippet; this is what the ranking signals of this chapter decide. Related questions expand to a featured snippet each, a carousel shows images or videos, and the knowledge panel on the side shows facts about the university.

The search engine must decide for each query which elements to show and in which order. This is itself a ranking problem, with the additional constraint that the page must load in a fraction of a second.

Answers on the result page change the economics of the web. When the answer is on the result page, the user has no reason to click. A 2024 clickstream study by SparkToro found that 58.5% of Google searches in the US and 59.7% in the EU end without a click on any result, a share that has grown with every new answer feature. Publishers, who depend on search traffic, lose visitors, while the search engine keeps users on its own page. A new optimization industry has emerged as well: generative engine optimization (GEO) tries to make a page more likely to be cited in generated answers, for example by adding statistics, quotations, and authoritative sources. Aggarwal et al. reported that such changes can raise a page’s visibility in generated answers by up to 40%.

Crawling the Web¶

A search engine can only rank pages it has fetched. Collecting the pages, however, is the smaller part of what a crawl provides. Sites can already announce their content themselves: a sitemap lists the URLs of a site, and some engines accept notifications of changed pages through protocols such as IndexNow. But everything a site submits is the site’s own claim about itself, and the web has no editor and strong incentives to cheat. The crawler gives the engine an independent view of the web, and this view is what most ranking signals of this chapter are built on:

  • The view from outside. By following links, the crawler sees each page through the rest of the web: which pages link to it, and with which anchor texts. This evidence is written by others and is therefore much harder to fake than the page’s own text. It feeds the anchor index (Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts) and the link graph (Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS). Global measures such as PageRank need the whole graph, which no single site can provide.

  • The content as delivered. The crawler fetches a page the way a browser does, so the engine judges the content that users actually receive, not the keywords and descriptions that a site declares about itself in its metadata.

  • Duplicates and change. Only a crawl across many sites reveals that a page is one of a hundred copies, and only repeated visits reveal how often a page changes, which the engine needs to keep its results fresh.

  • The basis for answers. Featured snippets, knowledge panels, and generated answers are only as good as the crawled pages behind them. The crawl is the corpus that the generated answers of Chapter 7 are grounded on.

The crawler (also called spider or bot) works in the loop shown in Figure 8:

  1. The crawl starts from a set of seed URLs, for example well-known sites and previously crawled pages.

  2. The URL frontier holds all URLs waiting to be fetched, ordered by priority. Important pages (high PageRank, frequent changes) are fetched first.

  3. The fetcher downloads a page. Before fetching from a site, it reads the site’s robots.txt file, which lists the paths the site owner does not want crawled.

  4. The parser extracts the text, the outgoing links, and their anchor texts. New URLs go back into the frontier; each link and its anchor text is stored under the target URL for the anchor index (Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts) and the link graph (Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS).

  5. Duplicate detection discards pages already seen. Exact duplicates are found by hashing the URL and the content; near-duplicates, such as the same article with different ads, by comparing compact fingerprints of the content such as SimHash.

Three requirements make crawling at web scale hard. Politeness: a crawler must not overload a site, so it limits the request rate per host, typically by keeping one queue per host in the frontier and waiting between requests. Freshness: pages change at very different rates. A news front page changes every few minutes, a professor’s home page every few months. The crawler estimates the change rate of each page and schedules recrawls accordingly; the index is never a snapshot of the web, but a mix of pages crawled at different times. Robustness: the web contains spider traps, such as calendars with an endless sequence of “next month” links, and servers that respond slowly or with garbage.

The robots.txt convention dates from 1994 and was standardized only in 2022 (RFC 9309). It is a request, not an access control: well-behaved crawlers follow it, but nothing enforces it. It has gained new attention with language models. AI companies crawl the web to collect training data, and site owners increasingly use robots.txt to block crawlers such as OpenAI’s GPTBot while still allowing search engine crawlers.
