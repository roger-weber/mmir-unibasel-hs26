Losses and Performance Measures

Where this is used

Evaluating Text Classifiers and Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves measure classifiers once they make hard decisions; this section covers the soft assignments with which they are trained. The softmax and the cross-entropy loss train the word embeddings of Word Embeddings, and with a temperature they form the contrastive loss of Multimodal Transformers. puts a softmax on top of frozen image embeddings. Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search trades search quality against speed and memory, the trade-off by which dimensionality reduction is measured. C - Neural Networks builds on the gradient of the cross-entropy loss derived below.

A performance measure, the PPP in Mitchell’s definition, states how well a system performs its task, and each task has its own idea of what “good” means. Measures serve two purposes. During training, a measure called the loss is what the learning algorithm minimizes. After training, a measure compares systems with each other. The two need not be the same: we usually train a classifier with the cross-entropy loss below and report its accuracy or F1 score. Measures fall into three groups: supervised learning compares the outputs with known targets, unsupervised learning has no targets, and reinforcement learning receives rewards.

Regression and the Mean Squared Error¶

A regression model is a function fw:RM→Rf_{\mathbf{w}}: \mathbb{R}^M \to \mathbb{R}fw​:RM→R with parameters w∈RD\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^Dw∈RD. Classification: Hard and Soft Assignments¶

Classification tasks differ along two dimensions. The first is the number of classes: binary classification decides between two classes, multi-class classification between many, often hundreds. Once a classifier makes hard decisions, precision, recall, accuracy, and the other values of the confusion matrix measure its quality. Evaluating Text Classifiers covers them for two and for many classes, and Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves shows how a score becomes a decision. This subsection is about the second dimension, which matters for training.

The second dimension is the form of the output. A hard assignment names one class. A soft assignment gives a probability for each class. Figure 1 shows both for an image classifier with four classes: dogs, cats, horses, and sharks. The network does not output probabilities directly. It outputs four raw scores, the logits, here 1.3, 4.6, 0.4, and 2.1. The soft assignment turns them into probabilities: 3% for dogs, 88% for cats, 1% for horses, and 7% for sharks. The photo shows a cat in a knitted shark costume, which explains the 7% for sharks. The hard assignment keeps only the most likely class, sets its output to 1 and all others to 0, and says “it is a cat”.

Soft assignments carry information that hard assignments lose. A second system that gives the cat only 79% makes the same hard assignment, so accuracy rates both systems equally, yet the first is more confident in the correct class. For training, there is a second reason to prefer soft assignments. Accuracy changes only when a prediction flips from one class to another, so a small change of the parameters usually leaves it unchanged, and its gradient is zero almost everywhere. Gradient descent needs a measure that changes smoothly with the parameters. This requires two steps: a function that maps logits to probabilities, and a loss that measures how well these probabilities match the 0/1 targets of the training set.

Softmax¶

The logits ok∈Ro_k \in \mathbb{R}ok​∈R, one for each of the KKK classes, can take any value, and their range depends on the model and its activation functions. We need probabilities y^k\hat{y}_ky^​k​ that are positive, sum to 1, and keep the order of the logits. Many functions satisfy these conditions; the softmax is the standard choice.

Key Formula: Softmax
y^k=exp⁡(ok)∑j=1Kexp⁡(oj)\hat{y}_k = \frac{\exp(o_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(o_j)}y^​k​=∑j=1K​exp(oj​)exp(ok​)​
(6)

The exponential makes every value positive and preserves the order of the logits; dividing by the sum makes the values add up to 1. A difference of one between two logits becomes a factor of e≈2.7e \approx 2.7e≈2.7 between their probabilities.

Unless stated otherwise, exp⁡\expexp and log⁡\loglog use the natural base eee.

A temperature below 1 sharpens it toward the largest logit, and a temperature above 1 flattens it. For the four logits above, τ=0.5\tau = 0.5τ=0.5 gives the cat 99% and τ=2\tau = 2τ=2 only 62%. The contrastive loss of Multimodal Transformers uses a small temperature.

The model distribution is the softmax output y^\hat{\mathbf{y}}y^​.

The softmax belongs in the loss, not twice in the model

Deep learning libraries often combine the softmax and the cross-entropy into one function for numerical stability. torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss in PyTorch, for example, expects logits, not probabilities. A model that already ends with a softmax layer then applies the softmax twice. Training still runs, but the inputs to the second softmax lie between 0 and 1, so the correct class can never receive more than e/(e+K−1)e / (e + K - 1)e/(e+K−1), which is 48% for four classes, and the loss cannot fall below 0.74.

Measures Without Ground Truth¶

Unsupervised tasks have no targets, so the measures above do not apply. Instead, we judge whether the model found “good” clusters or “true” outliers.

For clustering, the central difficulty is the number of clusters and their shape. Too many clusters split coherent regions; too few merge groups that should stay apart. Internal measures need no labels: the elbow method, the silhouette score, and the Davies-Bouldin index rate how compact the clusters are and how well they are separated. Clustering describes the first two. When a reference partition exists, for example when we test a clustering algorithm on labeled data, external measures such as the adjusted Rand index compare the clusters with it.

Dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis or autoencoders compress the data. Their measure is how well the original data can be reconstructed from the reduced representation. Often, the reduced vectors serve another task, such as vector search, and the measure becomes a trade-off: faster search and less memory against the loss of quality compared with search on the uncompressed vectors (Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search).

Rewards in Reinforcement Learning¶

A reinforcement learning agent acts in an environment in discrete time steps. In each step, it observes the state, chooses an action, and receives a reward. Its goal is to maximize the cumulative reward. There are no known pairs of inputs and correct outputs, so the agent must balance the exploration of unknown situations against the exploitation of what it already knows. The field draws on game theory, control theory, simulation, and genetic algorithms. The reward function is the performance measure, and designing it well is often the hardest part:

  • Autonomous driving: positive rewards for following traffic rules, staying on the road, and avoiding collisions; negative rewards for breaking rules, driving too fast or erratically, and causing accidents.

  • Game playing: rewards for the strategic advantages that lead to a win. In chess, the reward can be the final win or loss alone, or it can add intermediate rewards for capturing pieces and controlling the center. Intermediate rewards speed up learning but can teach the agent to value material more than the win.

  • Financial trading: a reward that balances profit against risk. An agent that chases highly profitable trades leaves the risk profile of the portfolio owner, while an overly cautious one misses opportunities. The strategy must also withstand unexpected changes of the market.
