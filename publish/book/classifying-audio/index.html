Classifying Audio

A radio archivist wants every hour of broadcast marked with its stretches of talk and music. The labels “speech” and “music” become facets and filters: a journalist searches only the talk, a music editor only the music (Filtering, Facets, and Compression). Speech versus music is the classic case for a classifier on hand-crafted features, because the two differ in a way that simple statistics can measure: speech alternates between syllables and short pauses, so its energy drops often, while music tends to keep a continuous level. This section builds such a classifier from segment statistics of the energy and the zero-crossing rate, applies it to a continuous stream, and then compares it with what replaced hand-crafted features: pretrained audio networks that recognize hundreds of sound classes, and CLAP, which assigns labels it was never trained on and answers text queries.

A Decision Tree for Speech and Music¶

A decision tree classifies an item with a sequence of threshold tests, such as “Is LSTER above 0.41?”, each of which sends the item down one of two branches until a leaf assigns the label. Learning algorithms such as C4.5 build the tree from labeled examples: they choose, at each node, the feature and threshold that best separate the classes, measured by how much the split reduces the uncertainty about the label, and repeat the choice in each branch. Trees are fast, need little training data, and their rules can be read and checked by a human. Gradient-boosted ensembles of many small trees, such as XGBoost, are the modern variant and often more accurate, but harder to interpret. The split criteria of C4.5, pruning, and boosting are described in Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting.

Castán et al. (2010) built a speech/music classifier along these lines. Figure 1 shows the pipeline: the signal is cut into frames of 40 ms and segments of 4 s, the frame features are summarized per segment, and C4.5 learns a rule set from segments labeled as speech or music. The features are six segment statistics:

Example: a tree on two features

Figure 2 shows LSTER and HZCRR for all 4-second segments, with a hop of 1 second, of six public recordings: three LibriVox readings by two men and a woman (34 segments), among them the reading of The Ashiel Mystery, and the first 45 seconds of three music pieces, Brahms, Tchaikovsky’s Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy, and the jazz piece Vibe Ace (126 segments). A tree of depth 2, learned with class weights that balance the two classes, finds two rules:

  1. If LSTER ≤0.41\le 0.41≤0.41, the segment is music.

  2. Otherwise, if HZCRR ≤0.123\le 0.123≤0.123, the segment is music; else it is speech.

The tree labels 80.0% of the segments correctly. All speech segments are correct, but only 74.6% of the music segments: 88.1% for Brahms, 81.0% for Tchaikovsky, and 54.8% for Vibe Ace. The drum kit and the decaying vibraphone notes give Vibe Ace frames with many zero crossings and gaps of low energy, so many of its segments fall into the speech region.

Evaluate on held-out recordings, not held-out segments

Neighboring segments overlap and come from the same recording, so they are near duplicates. A random split into training and test segments puts parts of the same recording on both sides and reports an accuracy that new recordings will not reach. Holding out whole recordings is the honest test. For the tree above, leaving out one clip at a time, learning on the other five, and testing on the held-out clip gives 74.4% instead of 80.0%. How to measure classifiers is covered in Evaluating Text Classifiers.

From Segments to Streams and Files¶

The tree labels segments, but the archive needs labels for longer stretches. Figure 3 shows the two cases. For a continuous stream such as a radio broadcast, the predictions of consecutive segments are smoothed, and a new stretch starts only when the smoothed value changes its side. For a single file, the predictions of its segments are counted, and the most frequent label wins.

Voting over files is simpler. Of the 42 segments of the Brahms clip, 37 are labeled music and 5 speech, so the file is music. Vibe Ace is music by a narrow margin of 23 to 19 segments.

Learned Audio Classifiers¶

The tree separates two classes with features designed for exactly these two classes. An archive needs many more labels: applause, laughter, telephone ringing, a crowd, an orchestra, a dog, rain. No one can design features for hundreds of classes, so the features are learned from labeled examples instead. The input is the log mel spectrogram: the logarithms log⁡M(t,b)\log M(t,b)logM(t,b) of the mel band energies of each frame over a few seconds, a matrix that can be treated like a single-channel image. Convolutional networks such as PANNs process it in the same way as a photo, with filters that slide over time and frequency, the approach that replaced hand-crafted image features (Learned Features: CNNs).

The Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST) follows the Vision Transformer for images instead: it cuts the spectrogram into patches of 16 by 16 values, turns each patch into a vector, and processes the sequence of patch vectors with a transformer encoder like the tokens of a sentence, so that every patch can attend to every other one. Both kinds of networks are trained on AudioSet, about two million ten-second clips from YouTube videos labeled with 527 sound classes. As with images, the second-to-last layer of such a network yields an audio embedding for similarity search. The Vision Transformer and its patches are described in Transformer Embeddings for Image Search.

Example

The clips are the LibriVox reading of The Ashiel Mystery, the Brahms clip, Vibe Ace, and two animal recordings: the 2.7-second call of a robin and the song of a humpback whale recorded in Glacier Bay, Alaska. CLAP: Zero-Shot Labels and Text Queries¶

A pretrained classifier knows only its 527 classes. CLAP (Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining) removes this limit with contrastive training, the method behind CLIP for images. An audio encoder and a text encoder are trained side by side on pairs of audio clips and their descriptions. Within each batch of pairs, the training pulls the vectors of a clip and its own description together and pushes the vectors of all mismatched pairs apart, so that after training, clips and texts share one vector space. To classify a clip, CLAP embeds one text prompt per label, such as “This is a sound of applause.”, and returns the label whose prompt lies closest to the clip, with a softmax over the similarities. To search an archive, it embeds a text query such as “a dog barking in the rain” and returns the nearest audio embeddings from a vector index. Neither task needs a classifier trained for the label or the query. The contrastive loss is worked out for CLIP, with a computed batch, in Transformer Embeddings for Image Search.

The whale song is labeled “music”, because none of the five prompts fits and a softmax always picks one of the offered labels; AST named it correctly because AudioSet has a class for it. The wording of the prompt matters too: with the bare word “speech” instead of the full sentence, the first reading gets only 0.442 for speech, with 0.304 for music and 0.253 for birdsong.

The learned models win on accuracy and on the range of labels, but the tree has not become useless. It needs two thresholds instead of a network with tens of millions of parameters, so it runs on a microcontroller in real time. Every one of its decisions can be traced back to two numbers, and it needs a few dozen labeled segments instead of two million clips. When a task has two classes, a clear acoustic difference, and tight limits on hardware, a hand-crafted feature and a small tree remain a sensible choice.

A zero-shot label is a choice among the offered labels

CLAP and CLIP compare a clip only with the prompts they are given. A high score means “closer than the other prompts”, not “present in the clip”. The obvious safeguards are weak. With a sixth prompt, “This is a sound of something else.”, the whale still gets 0.647 for music and only 0.199 for the catch-all. A threshold on the raw cosine similarity fails as well: the whale is closer to the music prompt (0.397) than the Brahms clip is (0.294). The reliable fix is a label set that covers the content of the archive, or a classifier with a class for it, such as AST with its class for whale vocalizations.
