# 6 - Vector Search

The previous chapter turned text into embeddings: dense vectors whose geometry captures meaning, so that "heart disease" and "cardiology treatment" land close together even though they share no words. Images, audio, and video can be encoded the same way. Once every document and every query is a point in the same space, retrieval becomes a geometric question: which stored points lie closest to the query point?

That question is easy to state and expensive to answer. A collection may hold millions or billions of vectors, each with hundreds or thousands of dimensions. Comparing the query against every stored vector is correct but slow, and the classical spatial indexes that accelerate low-dimensional search collapse as the number of dimensions grows. This chapter is about closing that gap: how to measure closeness, why exact search becomes hopeless in high dimensions, and how approximate methods recover speed by giving up a little accuracy that the application never needed.

```{figure} images/figure_6_3.png
:name: fig-embedding-space
:width: 75%

A 2D projection of word embeddings. Related terms cluster together, so proximity in the vector space stands in for similarity in meaning.
```

Two kinds of vectors meet in this chapter. Embeddings from learned models are compared with the dot product or cosine similarity, where a larger score means a better match. Feature vectors extracted directly from media, such as a color histogram of an image, are compared with a distance, where a smaller value means a better match. These two views are two sides of the same coin, and we will see that a simple normalization turns one into the other. The engineering problem, finding the closest points quickly, is the same in both cases.

```{tip} What you'll learn
- Explain how dot product, cosine similarity, and distance measures express closeness, and convert between them.
- Describe how classical spatial indexes (Voronoi diagrams, grid files, R-trees) answer nearest-neighbor queries.
- Explain the curse of dimensionality and why it forces exact search back to a full scan.
- Compare approximate nearest-neighbor methods (IVF, HNSW, LSH, product quantization) and their recall-speed trade-off.
- Choose a vector-search framework and combine similarity search with metadata filters.
```
