Method Comparison¶

The chapter’s search methods trade recall, speed, and memory in different ways. Exact methods guarantee the true neighbors; the approximate methods relax that guarantee and pull one of three levers: fewer dimensions, fewer bits per vector, or fewer vectors read.

The levers are complementary rather than exclusive. A production index reduces the dimensions, selects a few lists or graph regions to read, encodes the vectors in those lists compactly, and reranks the best k′k'k′ candidates with exact distances. For one billion 768-dimensional vectors, the stack PCA384,IVF4096,PQ48,RFlat reads about 190 MB per query instead of 3 TB. On the ann-benchmarks GloVe-100 dataset, the fastest methods answer 60 to 850 times more queries per second than a full scan, depending on the recall target. Recall against exact search is only part of the story: after reranking, retrievers with far lower recall reach almost the quality of exact search (see Key Takeaway 6).

Key Takeaways¶

  1. Vectors come from learned embeddings or from perceptual features such as color histograms. They are compared either with a similarity score, where larger is better (dot product, cosine), or with a distance, where smaller is better (Euclidean, Manhattan). The comparison function belongs to the feature. Length normalization turns cosine similarity into a plain dot product, and on the unit sphere ranking by Euclidean distance equals ranking by cosine.

  2. Classical spatial indexes (Voronoi diagram, grid file, R-tree) were built for two and three dimensions. The optimal algorithm of Hjaltason and Samet runs on all of them and needs only an optimistic bound per region, so it also works for the dot product. Even in 2D, a nearest-neighbor search follows several paths, because it must prove that no closer point exists.

  3. The curse of dimensionality is quantifiable. Distances grow like d\sqrt{d}d​ while their spread stays constant, so the contrast between the nearest and the farthest point vanishes, and dot products of random vectors concentrate around zero. Once the expected nearest-neighbor distance exceeds the size of a leaf, even the optimal algorithm visits every leaf, and decades of R-tree variants did not change this.

  4. Loss of contrast also weakens the meaning of “nearest”. Real data still works because related items form clusters that stand out from the background, but within a cluster the exact nearest neighbor is just one of several good matches. Vector search often serves as a first stage whose candidates a reranker reorders, and even without a reranker users want good matches rather than the single best one. Both arguments justify trading exactness for speed.

  5. Approximate search pulls three levers. Fewer dimensions (PCA, Matryoshka) is a modest lever that still reads every vector. Fewer bits per vector (scalar quantization, product quantization, LSH) makes every comparison cheaper; the compression errors are small compared to the gap between good matches and the background, and the VA-file shows that bounds on the quantized cells even keep the search exact. Fewer vectors read (IVF, HNSW) visits only the regions near the query. Combined, the levers cut the work per query by two to three orders of magnitude, but their errors compound, so their parameters must be tuned together, on end-to-end quality rather than on recall alone.

  6. Recall against exact search understates the quality of approximate search. On TREC-COVID, retrievers that read hundreds of times less data than a full scan return only a quarter of the exact top 10, yet after reranking many come within a few points of exact search, because the documents they return instead are often just as relevant. A reranker repairs fine-filter errors, such as coarse PQ codes, but not coarse-filter errors, such as unprobed IVF lists, so the coarse filter should stay generous.

  7. In practice, vector search lives inside libraries (FAISS, Lucene), search engines (OpenSearch), and databases (pgvector). Realistic queries combine a vector search for meaning, a keyword search for names and terms, and filters for hard constraints. Filters are applied before, after, or during the search: pre-filtering suits selective filters, post-filtering suits graph indexes that deliver a stream of candidates, and filtering during the search suits the list scans of an inverted file. Dense and sparse rankings are fused with reciprocal rank fusion, as in Building Semantic Search.

Exam focus

  • Similarity scores (“larger is better”) versus distances (“smaller is better”), why the comparison function belongs to the feature, and how normalization connects cosine, dot product, and Euclidean distance.

  • Why dense embeddings cannot be pruned the way sparse term vectors can in an inverted file.

  • How the optimal nearest-neighbor algorithm works, why it needs only an optimistic bound per region, and why it follows several paths even in 2D.

  • Why exact nearest-neighbor search degrades to a full scan in high dimensions, and why the loss of contrast and the retriever-reranker pipeline make good matches sufficient.

  • The three levers of approximate search, what each one reduces, and why compression errors rarely change which matches stand out. How product quantization and LSH encode vectors, and how IVF and HNSW limit the vectors read.

  • Pre-filtering, post-filtering, and filtering during the search: which one fits which filter selectivity and which index, and the failure mode of post-filtering with a selective filter.

  • Why recall against exact search understates end-to-end quality, and why a reranker repairs fine-filter errors but not coarse-filter errors.

Self-Check Questions¶

  1. (Understand) Why does storing length-normalized vectors let a system use the dot product in place of cosine similarity?

  2. (Understand) Name the three levers of approximate search. For each, give one method and state whether it reduces the number of vectors read or the cost of reading each one.

  3. (Apply) A query q=(2,7)\mathbf{q} = (2, 7)q=(2,7) meets an R-tree leaf with rectangle [3,5]×[1,4][3, 5] \times [1, 4][3,5]×[1,4]. Compute the lower bound of the Euclidean distance, and state when the optimal algorithm may skip this leaf.

  4. (Understand) An R-tree performs well at d=4d = 4d=4 but no better than a full scan at d=100d = 100d=100. What happens to the contrast between near and far points as the dimension grows, and why does this defeat pruning?

  5. (Apply) A collection stores 512-dimensional float vectors. Product quantization splits them into m=64m = 64m=64 sub-vectors with b=8b = 8b=8 bits per code. How many bytes does a vector need before and after encoding, and how many entries do the lookup tables of one query hold?

  6. (Evaluate) A colleague insists that a retriever must return the exact nearest neighbors of the query embedding. Using the loss of contrast and the retriever-reranker pipeline, argue why approximate results are usually sufficient.

  7. (Analyze) A query for the ten nearest neighbors that also match a rare metadata value returns only two results, though many matching documents exist. Which filtering strategy was used, and how would you fix it for an HNSW index and for an IVF index?

  8. (Analyze) A user searches for “a space adventure with Harrison Ford from the 1980s”. Split the query into the parts a vector search, a keyword search, and a filter should handle, and explain why no single method covers all of them.

  9. (Evaluate) Given a fixed recall target of 0.95, describe how you would use an ann-benchmarks recall-throughput plot to choose between HNSW and IVF with product quantization, and which information the plot does not show.

  10. (Analyze) A retriever reaches a recall@10 of only 0.3 against exact search, yet after reranking its top 50 the result is as good as with exact search. Explain how this is possible, and why IVF with a single probed list does not recover in the same way.

Further Reading¶

  • Guttman, A. (1984). R-trees: a dynamic index structure for spatial searching. Proceedings of ACM SIGMOD. ACM. The original R-tree, the bounding-rectangle index whose search algorithm and overlap problem this chapter builds on.

  • Hjaltason, G. R., & Samet, H. (1999). Distance browsing in spatial databases. ACM Transactions on Database Systems, 24(2), 265-318. PDF. The incremental nearest-neighbor algorithm of the low-dimensional section: a priority queue over regions and points that returns neighbors one by one in order of distance and visits no more regions than necessary. The same stream of candidates reappears in post-filtering.

  • Ram, P., & Gray, A. G. (2012). Maximum inner-product search using cone trees. Proceedings of ACM SIGKDD, 931-939. arXiv preprint (titled Maximum inner-product search using tree data-structures). Derives the bounds that let tree indexes prune for the dot product, including the bound q⋅c+r∥q∥\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{c} + r \lVert \mathbf{q} \rVertq⋅c+r∥q∥ for a ball with center c\mathbf{c}c and radius rrr.

  • Weber, R., Schek, H.-J., & Blott, S. (1998). A quantitative analysis and performance study for similarity-search methods in high-dimensional spaces. Proceedings of VLDB. PDF. Shows analytically that above a moderate dimension any partitioning index degrades to a scan, and introduces the VA-file as the response, the core argument behind this chapter’s curse-of-dimensionality section and the scalar quantization of the approximate-search section.

  • Weber, R. (2001). Similarity search in high-dimensional vector spaces. Doctoral thesis, ETH Zurich, Diss. ETH No. 13974. DISDBIS, 74, Akademische Verlagsgesellschaft. ETH Research Collection (record only; the full text is closed access). The extended treatment of the VLDB paper above: cost models for tree-based and scan-based similarity search in high-dimensional spaces, and the VA-file in depth.

  • Beyer, K., Goldstein, J., Ramakrishnan, R., & Shaft, U. (1999). When is “nearest neighbor” meaningful? Database Theory: ICDT’99, LNCS 1540, 217-235. Technical report PDF. Proves that for broad classes of data the contrast between the nearest and the farthest neighbor vanishes as the dimension grows, the result behind this chapter’s loss-of-contrast formula.

  • Aggarwal, C. C., Hinneburg, A., & Keim, D. A. (2001). On the surprising behavior of distance metrics in high-dimensional space. Proceedings of ICDT, LNCS 1973. PDF. Explains why distance concentration hits some metrics harder than others and why lower-norm distances can behave better in high dimensions.

  • Weber, R., & Böhm, K. (2000). Trading quality for time with nearest-neighbor search. Advances in Database Technology: EDBT 2000, LNCS 1777, 21-35. Springer. Turns the VA-file into an approximate method and measures how much query time a small, controlled loss of result quality saves, the trade at the heart of the approximate-search section.

  • Indyk, P., & Motwani, R. (1998). Approximate nearest neighbors: towards removing the curse of dimensionality. Proceedings of ACM STOC, 604-613. PDF. Introduces locality-sensitive hashing and shows that approximate nearest-neighbor search escapes the curse of dimensionality that defeats exact search.

  • Charikar, M. S. (2002). Similarity estimation techniques from rounding algorithms. Proceedings of ACM STOC, 380-388. PDF. Introduces hashing with random hyperplanes and shows that two vectors receive different bits with probability θ/π\theta / \piθ/π, the basis of LSH for cosine similarity.

  • Malkov, Y. A., & Yashunin, D. A. (2020). Efficient and robust approximate nearest neighbor search using hierarchical navigable small world graphs. IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 42(4), 824-836. arXiv preprint 2016. The HNSW method that underlies most modern vector databases.

  • Jégou, H., Douze, M., & Schmid, C. (2011). Product quantization for nearest neighbor search. IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 33(1), 117-128. PDF. Introduces product quantization, the compact encoding that makes billion-scale vector search fit in memory.

  • Voorhees, E., Alam, T., Bedrick, S., Demner-Fushman, D., Hersh, W. R., Lo, K., Roberts, K., Soboroff, I., & Wang, L. L. (2020). TREC-COVID: constructing a pandemic information retrieval test collection. ACM SIGIR Forum, 54(1), 1-12. arXiv preprint. The test collection behind the end-to-end benchmark of the approximate-search section, including why its pooled judgments miss documents that new retrievers surface.

Tools and benchmarks: FAISS (composite index library), Lucene (search library with HNSW vector fields), OpenSearch vector search (distributed k-NN and hybrid search), pgvector (PostgreSQL vector search), and ann-benchmarks (standard recall-throughput comparison).
