Automated Feature Extraction

Send the Taj Mahal photo to a commercial image analysis service, and within a second it returns two kinds of results. Figure 1 shows them. The perception branch lists the dominant colors with their shares (steel blue 35%, a light beige 22%, gray 14%) and measures brightness, sharpness, and contrast. The recognition branch lists labels with confidence values: “Arch” 96.5%, “Architecture” 96.5%, “Person” 94.9%, “Tomb” 78.5%, “Fun” 72.9%, “Monument” 65.7%, “Dome” 57.5%. For a second image, the service detects a face and estimates attributes such as age range and glasses; for a third, it recognizes the actress Brie Larson with 99% confidence.

Nobody annotated these photos. The two branches correspond to two levels of automated content analysis. The colors describe how the image looks; the labels describe what it shows. This section walks up these levels, from features that describe perception to classifiers that produce labels, and ends with how neural networks took over the classification step.

The Abstraction Ladder¶

Automated content analysis works in layers. Each layer transforms the output of the layer below into a more abstract representation and closes a part of the semantic gap. Figure 2 shows the layers for a photo of a coyote on a snowy road.

  • Raw level: the file as stored, such as JPEG, MP3, or MP4. It holds pixel intensities or amplitude samples. This level preserves the content for playback but helps little with search, because similar content rarely means similar raw data: two photos of the same building, taken a few steps apart, share almost no identical pixel values.

  • Perceptual level (low level): features that describe how the content looks or sounds, such as color histograms, textures, and edges for images, or the spectral properties of sound. They support comparisons such as “images with similar colors”, but they do not interpret the content. Perceptual features invert the argument of the raw level: they assume that similar statistics of the signal indicate similar content. This assumption often holds, but not always.

  • Structural level (mid level): patterns and categories recognized in the features, such as detected objects (“wolf”, “road”, “snow”), scene types, music genres, or shot boundaries in a video. Classifiers and learned embeddings work at this level.

  • Semantic level (high level): interpretable descriptions such as captions, keywords, transcribed speech, or recognized text. At this level, the content is described in concepts that people use.

  • Contextual level: information from outside the content, such as timestamps, locations, device data, user tags, and social context. Metadata from Metadata and Its Limits lives here, and the outputs of the higher levels are stored here as new metadata.

The layers show where each technique of this part of the book sits. The following subsections cover the perceptual and structural levels in general terms; the semantic level is the subject of Multimodal Transformers.

Perceptual Features¶

Perceptual features describe content the way humans perceive it. They do not attempt to recognize objects or interpret scenes. Instead, they measure properties of the signal that matter to human perception: the distribution of colors, the texture of surfaces, the shapes of edges, the pitch and timbre of a sound, the motion in a video. Each feature turns an item into a feature vector. A color histogram, for example, counts how many pixels fall into each of a fixed set of color bins, and the vector of counts describes the color impression of the image.

The idea is familiar from text retrieval. In Vector Space Retrieval, a document became a vector of term frequencies. A color histogram is the image counterpart of such a term vector: the bins play the role of terms, and the pixel counts play the role of term frequencies. The difference is that a term refers to a concept, while a color bin refers to a sensation. A red bin is high for a red car and for a sunset alike.

A system uses perceptual feature vectors in two ways:

  1. Similarity search. The system compares feature vectors directly with a distance measure and returns the items closest to a query. This is content-based retrieval: the query is an example image, a sketch, a color palette, or a hummed melody instead of keywords. The distance measures and index structures of vector search apply directly. The comparison function belongs to the feature: color histograms, for example, come with their own distances, as Distance and Similarity Measures explains.

  2. Classification. The system feeds the feature vectors into a classifier that predicts labels such as “outdoor”, “speech”, or “contains a face”. The labels are text, so they return to the keyword path of Metadata and Its Limits, but now the system writes them automatically.

Good perceptual features must be stable under the variations that human perception ignores. A photo of a cat is still a photo of a cat after small changes, and a song is still the same song when played a little louder. The most important invariances are:

  • Translation: an object shifted to a different position in the image is still the same object.

  • Rotation: a slightly rotated coffee mug does not lose its identity.

  • Scale and resolution: a tree in the background and the same tree close up, or a high-resolution photo and its thumbnail, show the same content.

  • Lighting and loudness: a face in harsh sunlight and in soft indoor light is the same face; a song at two volume levels is the same song.

  • Noise and distortion: sensor noise, compression artifacts, background noise in a recording, or a low-quality microphone should not change the feature much.

Each modality has its own perceptual features, and they are inspired by how human vision and hearing work. The following chapters develop them in detail: color, texture, and shape for images; time-domain, frequency-domain, and psychoacoustic features for audio; shot and motion features for video.

Perceptual similarity is not semantic similarity

A red car and a sunset have similar color histograms, while a black cat on a white carpet and a ginger cat on a green lawn have very different ones. It is tempting to expect perceptual features to find “similar content”. They find content that looks or sounds similar, which is useful for queries by example, near-duplicate detection, or “more like this”, but not for concept queries such as “pictures of cats”.

Classification: From Features to Labels¶

Classification moves from the perceptual to the structural level. A classifier learns from labeled examples how feature values relate to classes and then predicts the class of new items. For two decades, the standard approach combined hand-crafted perceptual features with a classical learning algorithm such as a decision tree or a support vector machine, both described in B - Classical Machine Learning Methods.

A classic example is separating speech from music in a radio stream. Speech alternates between voiced sounds and short pauses, so its energy drops frequently; music keeps a more continuous energy level. The signal of speech also crosses zero at a more irregular rate than the signal of most music. A few features computed over short time frames, such as the share of low-energy frames and the variation of the zero-crossing rate, are enough for a decision tree to separate the two classes with high accuracy. The predicted labels “speech” and “music” become metadata, and they decide which analysis follows: speech goes to transcription, music to genre classification.

How well such a classifier works is measured with the confusion matrix and the metrics derived from it: accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and the ROC curve. They apply to image, audio, and video classifiers exactly as to the text classifiers of Evaluating Text Classifiers.

Automatically extracted labels are claims too. The classifier behind Figure 2 labeled the animal on the snowy road “wolf”. It is a coyote, as the metadata at the bottom of the figure states. The two species look alike, and the classifier confused them. Better models reduce such errors but do not remove them. A classifier knows only the classes it was trained on and tends to confuse similar ones. Even the multimodal transformers of Multimodal Transformers, which can name almost anything, still mistake similar species, buildings, or persons, and sometimes describe details that are not in the image. Like the human metadata of Metadata and Its Limits, automatically extracted metadata must be evaluated before it is trusted.

A confidence value is not a probability of being right

The service in Figure 1 labels the Taj Mahal as “Gothic Arch” with 89.4% confidence, although the building is Mughal, not Gothic. For the face in the car, it reports “not smiling” with 63.5% and “appears to be happy” with 70%. Confidence values express how strongly the model favors a label, not how often such labels are correct. Before turning predicted labels into metadata, choose a threshold per label based on an evaluation against ground truth, as in Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves.

The weak point of this approach is the hand-crafted feature. A human expert must decide which properties of the signal separate the classes. That works for speech versus music, but not for the thousands of object classes that users search for. No one can design a feature that separates “Taj Mahal” from “other white buildings with domes”. The breakthrough came when neural networks learned the features themselves.

Neural Networks: Key Steps¶

Neural networks for image classification went through several generations. Each one solved a specific obstacle of its predecessors, and most of them have since been replaced. Figure 3 shows the milestones, and the dropdown below tells the story in more detail; CNN Architectures describes the architectures themselves.

Several advances worked together. The ReLU activation function reduced the vanishing gradient problem that had blocked the training of deep networks. Large labeled datasets such as ImageNet and fast GPUs made it possible to train millions of parameters. Regularization methods such as dropout and data augmentation reduced overfitting, and better optimizers such as Adam made training faster and more stable. Convolutional networks exploit the spatial structure of images: they share weights across positions, which gives them translation invariance by design. Residual connections let the gradient flow back to early layers, so depth stopped being a problem.

The layers of a trained convolutional network form their own abstraction ladder. Early layers respond to edges and color blobs, much like the perceptual features above. Middle layers respond to textures and parts, and the last layers respond to whole objects. The output of the second-to-last layer is a dense vector that describes the image, and these vectors became the first learned image embeddings. Two photos of mountains have close vectors even when their color histograms differ.

From hand-wired face detectors to residual networks (optional reading)

Rowley, Baluja, and Kanade at Carnegie Mellon University built one of the first practical neural face detectors in 1998. To keep the network small, they trained it on preprocessed 20 by 20 pixel windows: each window was corrected for lighting, its histogram was equalized, and a second “router” network first estimated the rotation of a potential face so that the window could be rotated upright. At detection time, the system slid the window over the image at many positions and over a pyramid of downscaled copies to find faces of different sizes. The method worked well, but every step except the final decision was designed by hand.

LeNet, developed by Yann LeCun and colleagues for reading handwritten digits, showed that a network could learn the features itself. Its convolution layers apply small learned filters across the whole image, and its pooling layers reduce the resolution step by step. On the MNIST digits it reached accuracies close to human performance without any manual feature design. Yet for more than a decade, convolutional networks remained a niche: the images were small, the datasets were small, and training deeper networks failed because the gradients vanished on their way back to the early layers.

AlexNet changed this in 2012. It won the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, which asks for one of 1,000 classes per image, with a top-5 error of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the best entry based on hand-crafted features. It was deeper than LeNet, processed 224 by 224 pixel images, used the ReLU activation instead of saturating functions such as tanh, and was trained on two GPUs. Its two large fully connected layers needed most of its 60 million parameters. The result convinced the computer vision community within a year.

The following years were a race for depth. VGG from Oxford built networks from repeated blocks of 3 by 3 convolutions. GoogLeNet won the 2014 challenge with inception modules that run convolutions of several sizes in parallel and concatenate the results. ResNet added a shortcut around every block. If f(x)f(\mathbf{x})f(x) is the mapping that a block should compute, the block learns only the residual g(x)=f(x)−xg(\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{x}g(x)=f(x)−x and adds it to its input, so the output is x+g(x)\mathbf{x} + g(\mathbf{x})x+g(x). If a block is not needed, it can learn g=0g = 0g=0, and the gradient flows back through the shortcut unchanged. ResNet trained networks with 152 layers and became the standard backbone for years. DenseNet went one step further and connected every layer to all following layers.

The Vision Transformer of 2020 finally removed the convolutions. It cuts an image into patches of 16 by 16 pixels, embeds each patch like a token, and processes the sequence with the same transformer encoder that Transformer Embeddings describes for text. With enough training data, it matches or beats convolutional networks, and it opened the door to models that process text and images with one architecture.

Most of these networks are no longer trained for retrieval systems today. Each one learned a fixed set of classes from a labeled dataset, and adding a new class meant collecting new labels and training again. Two developments replaced them. Self-supervised learning trains models on huge unlabeled or weakly labeled collections, for example by predicting masked parts of the input, so manual labels are no longer the bottleneck. Contrastive learning aligns different modalities in one embedding space, so that a model learns from image-caption pairs found on the web instead of from fixed label sets. The ideas of the older networks survive: convolutions, residual connections, and patches are building blocks of current models. The next section shows what these models do for retrieval.
