Clustering

Where this is used

Approximate Nearest-Neighbor Search builds the codebooks of product quantization with k-means, and Losses and Performance Measures lists the measures that judge clusters without labels. Naive Bayes in Naive Bayes can model a continuous feature with a mixture of Gaussians, whose parameters the expectation maximization of this section estimates. Classical speech recognizers quantize acoustic feature vectors with k-means before an HMM models them.

Clustering is unsupervised learning: the algorithm observes data without targets and finds groups of similar items. The result is a finite set of clusters and a rule that assigns new items to one of them, or to several with different weights. In multimedia retrieval, clustering serves four purposes. Feature quantization reduces a multi-dimensional feature to a small number of discrete values, much like a histogram approximates a distribution. Vector compression maps groups of dimensions to a few bits, as product quantization does for vector search. Cluster analysis extracts groups from a collection and infers labels for them. Image segmentation groups the pixels of an image into regions that belong together: clustering first quantizes the colors, and morphological operators then build coherent regions. This section describes k-means, expectation maximization with Gaussian mixtures, and how to choose the number of clusters.

k-Means¶

k-means goes back to the 1950s, when Lloyd developed it at Bell Labs to quantize signals for transmission; MacQueen gave it its name in 1967, and it became one of the most popular methods for cluster analysis. It represents each of KKK clusters by its centroid, the mean of the cluster’s items, and assigns each item to the cluster with the nearest centroid. The centroids divide the space into Voronoi cells, which define the shapes of the clusters.

For NNN items x1,…,xN\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_Nx1​,…,xN​ in ddd dimensions, k-means looks for a partition into KKK sets S1,…,SK\mathbb{S}_1, \dots, \mathbb{S}_KS1​,…,SK​ that makes the clusters as compact as possible:

The sum of the squared distances between the items and the centroids of their clusters. k-means minimizes it: small values mean tight clusters around their centroids.

Finding the partition with the smallest WCSS is NP-hard, but Lloyd’s algorithm finds a local optimum with a simple iteration:

  1. Choose KKK initial centroids.

  2. Assign each item to the cluster of its nearest centroid; if several centroids are equally near, choose one at random.

  3. Compute the new centroids as the means of the items in each cluster.

  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the assignments no longer change.

Neither step can increase the WCSS. Step 2 moves each item to its nearest centroid, and step 3 places each centroid at the point that minimizes the squared distances to its items. The algorithm therefore converges, usually in a few iterations, but the local optimum depends on the initial centroids.

In the first iteration, only the item 1 is nearest to the centroid 1; all others go to the centroid 2. The new centroids are 1 and 6.6, with a WCSS of 59.2. In the second iteration, the item 2 joins the first cluster, and the centroids become 1.5 and 7.75 (WCSS 33.25). The fourth iteration changes nothing, and the algorithm stops. For comparison, a single cluster has a WCSS of 85.3.

Choosing the Initial Centroids¶

The result depends on the start, and three strategies are common. Random points picks KKK random items as centroids, which spreads the centroids across the data. Random partition assigns every item to a random cluster and computes the centroids of these clusters, which places them close together near the center of the data. k-means++ (Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2007) chooses the first centroid at random and each further one among the remaining items with a probability proportional to the squared distance to the nearest centroid chosen so far. Distant items are therefore likely to become centroids. The seeding costs more, but it leads to much smaller final errors and faster convergence, and it is the default in most libraries. Running k-means several times with different starts and keeping the result with the smallest WCSS further reduces the risk of a poor local optimum.

Expectation Maximization with Gaussian Mixtures¶

k-means makes a hard assignment: each item belongs to exactly one cluster. A Gaussian mixture model makes a soft assignment instead. It assumes that the data comes from KKK Gaussian distributions N(μk,σk2)\mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)N(μk​,σk2​), each representing one cluster, and that an item comes from cluster kkk with the prior probability wkw_kwk​, where ∑kwk=1\sum_k w_k = 1∑k​wk​=1. Figure 1 shows two Gaussians and their mixture. With enough components, a mixture can approximate any distribution, which is why Gaussian mixtures also serve as density estimators (Tasks and Experience).

Estimating the parameters would be easy if we knew which cluster each item came from: the mean and the variance of each cluster would follow from its items, and the prior from its share of the items. Conversely, if we knew the parameters, Bayes’ rule would give the probability that an item comes from each cluster. Expectation maximization (EM) alternates between the two. It starts with initial parameters and repeats two steps. The E-step computes for every item xix_ixi​ and cluster kkk the responsibility γi,k\gamma_{i,k}γi,k​, the probability that the cluster produced the item, from the current parameters:

In ddd dimensions, each cluster becomes a multivariate Gaussian N(μk,Σk)\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)N(μk​,Σk​) with a covariance matrix, and the M-step computes Σk\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_kΣk​ as the responsibility-weighted average of (xi−μk)(xi−μk)⊤(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top(xi​−μk​)(xi​−μk​)⊤. The clusters can then be elongated and tilted ellipsoids instead of the spheres of k-means.

The name comes from the general form of the algorithm. The cluster memberships are latent variables: they are not observed, and the likelihood of the data is a sum over all their possible values, which has no closed-form maximum. EM instead maximizes the expected log-likelihood of the data together with the latent variables, where the expectation uses the distribution of the latent variables given the data and the current parameters (the E-step), and the maximization over the parameters has a closed-form solution (the M-step). Dempster, Laird, and Rubin (1977) showed that this never decreases the likelihood itself.

k-means is a special case of EM. With hard responsibilities, γi,k=1\gamma_{i,k} = 1γi,k​=1 for the nearest centroid and 0 otherwise, equal priors, and identical spherical covariances, the E-step becomes the assignment step and the M-step the centroid update. Gaussian mixtures with a common variance that shrinks toward zero turn into k-means in the limit.

Choosing the Number of Clusters¶

The number of clusters KKK is not known in advance, and it controls the capacity of the model like the polynomial degree of Over- and Underfitting. With K=1K = 1K=1, all items form one cluster, the extreme of underfitting. With K=NK = NK=N, every item is its own cluster with a WCSS of zero, the extreme of overfitting. Without labels, there is no validation accuracy, so we need measures that rate the clusters themselves: their compactness and their separation.

The elbow method plots the sum of squared errors, the WCSS for k-means, against KKK, as in Figure 2. The error always falls with growing KKK, but beyond a certain point it falls much more slowly: adding clusters then splits real groups instead of separating them. This point, the elbow, is a good choice for KKK. To find it automatically, we can compute the curve for KKK from 2 to an upper bound and pick the point farthest from the straight line between the first and the last point of the curve. Alternatively, we increase KKK step by step and stop when the distance of the curve point to the corner (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0) starts to grow, which needs fewer runs. Both methods need the two axes normalized, for example by min-max scaling. For Gaussian mixtures, the squared Mahalanobis distance (x−μk)⊤Σk−1(x−μk)(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(x−μk​)⊤Σk−1​(x−μk​) takes the place of the squared Euclidean distance.

The silhouette score (Rousseeuw 1987) rates each item by how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the next one. With aia_iai​ the mean distance of item iii to the other items of its cluster and bib_ibi​ the mean distance to the items of the nearest other cluster, the silhouette of the item is

(4)

It lies between -1 and 1: values near 1 mean that the item sits well inside its cluster, values near 0 that it lies between two clusters, and negative values that it is probably in the wrong cluster. An item that forms a cluster of its own gets si=0s_i = 0si​=0. The mean silhouette over all items rates the clustering, and the KKK with the highest mean is a good choice.

For the k-means result {1,2,4}\{1, 2, 4\}{1,2,4} and {7,8,12}\{7, 8, 12\}{7,8,12} above, the item 1 has a=(1+3)/2=2a = (1 + 3)/2 = 2a=(1+3)/2=2 and b=(6+7+11)/3=8b = (6 + 7 + 11)/3 = 8b=(6+7+11)/3=8, so s=(8−2)/8=0.75s = (8 - 2)/8 = 0.75s=(8−2)/8=0.75. The item 4 lies closer to the other cluster and gets only 0.50, the item 7 only 0.36. The mean silhouette is 0.58. Splitting off the item 12 as a third cluster lowers the WCSS from 18.7 to 5.2, but the mean silhouette drops to 0.54. The items 7 and 8 gain, since their cluster is now tight (0.79 and 0.75), but the single item 12 gets a silhouette of 0, and the item 4 falls to 0.29, because the new cluster {7,8}\{7, 8\}{7,8} lies only 3.5 away on average. The silhouette prefers two clusters.

Other measures exist: the Davies-Bouldin index compares the spread within clusters with the distance between their centroids, and with reference labels, the adjusted Rand index compares a clustering with a known partition (Losses and Performance Measures). No measure is right for every purpose: for vector compression, more clusters always mean less error, and KKK follows from the memory budget instead.
