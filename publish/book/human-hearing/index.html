Human Hearing and Sound Physics

Where this is used

From Sound to Spectrogram keeps decibels and one paragraph on the cochlea, which analyzes a sound by frequency before the brain sees it. Perceptual Features builds the mel scale and the MFCC features on two properties of the ear described below: a frequency resolution that decreases with frequency and a roughly logarithmic perception of energy. The onset strength of Musical Features counts only increases of energy, as the adapting hair cells of the cochlea do. Speech Recognition with HMMs relies on the same front end.

Sound has two meanings. In physics, it is a mechanical wave that travels through a medium. In perception, it is the sensation that this wave creates in a listener. This section follows a sound from the vibrating source through the air into the ear, along the cochlea, and up the auditory pathway to the cortex. On the way, we meet the properties that audio features imitate.

Sound Waves¶

Sound begins when something vibrates and disturbs the medium around it. A loudspeaker membrane pushes the air molecules in front of it together and pulls them apart, creating alternating regions of compression and rarefaction. These regions travel outward as a wave. The molecules themselves only oscillate around their resting positions; what travels is the pattern of pressure. Because sound passes from molecule to molecule, it needs a medium and cannot travel through empty space.

The speed of the wave depends on the medium. In air, it grows with the temperature TTT in degrees Celsius, approximately as v=331+0.6⋅Tv = 331 + 0.6 \cdot Tv=331+0.6⋅T m/s, which gives 343 m/s at 20 °C. Sound travels faster in liquids, about 1,482 m/s in water at 20 °C, and faster still in solids, around 4,000 m/s in wood and about 12,000 m/s in diamond, where the stiff structure passes vibrations on quickly. The speed links the frequency fff of a wave to its wavelength λ\lambdaλ.

Example: Wavelengths of audible sound

At 20 °C, the lowest audible frequency of 20 Hz has a wavelength of 343/20=17.1343 / 20 = 17.1343/20=17.1 m, a tone of 1 kHz has 34.3 cm, and the highest audible frequency of 20 kHz has 17.1 mm. Audible sound therefore spans wavelengths from the size of a building to the width of a fingertip. In water, the same 1 kHz tone has a wavelength of 1.48 m, more than four times as long as in air, because the frequency stays the same and the speed changes.

The wavelength decides how sound interacts with objects. Waves bounce off surfaces that are large compared with their wavelength and bend around smaller obstacles. A wall reflects a high tone and lets a low one bend around its edge, which is why we hear the bass of music from the next room better than the voices. At the boundary between two media, part of the wave is reflected and part continues in a new direction. On its way, the wave spreads over a growing area and loses energy to the medium, and high frequencies lose it faster than low ones, which is why distant thunder rumbles instead of cracking.

The pressure of the wave varies over an enormous range, from 20 µPa at the threshold of hearing to about 20 Pa near the threshold of pain, a factor of a million. From Sound to Spectrogram therefore measures sound pressure on the logarithmic decibel scale, with 0 dB at the threshold of hearing and 120 dB near the threshold of pain. Hearing damage depends on the level and the duration: long exposure to levels above about 85 dB damages the hair cells of the cochlea, and since these cells do not regrow in humans, the loss is permanent.

Hearing Range and Perceptual Attributes¶

Humans hear frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and the upper limit falls with age. Other species hear different parts of the spectrum; Heffner and Heffner (2007) give an overview for laboratory animals. Cats hear tones well above our upper limit, and bats hear far into the ultrasound, which they use to navigate and to hunt by echolocation: they emit calls and locate obstacles and prey from the echoes.

Within the human range, the physical properties of a sound do not map one to one onto what we perceive. The following table lists six perceptual attributes, the physical property behind each, and how the two differ.

The Ear¶

The ear does not pass raw pressure changes to the brain. Figure 1 shows its three parts. The outer ear collects and filters the sound, the middle ear transfers it from the air into a fluid, and the inner ear breaks it down by frequency and converts it into nerve signals.

Outer Ear¶

The pinna, the visible part of the ear, reflects sound into the ear canal. Its folds reflect sounds from different directions differently, so the spectrum that arrives at the eardrum carries a direction-dependent coloring. The brain learns these colorings and uses them to tell whether a sound comes from above or below, and from the front or the back, which the differences between the two ears alone cannot decide.

The ear canal is a tube of about 2.5 cm, open to the outside and closed by the eardrum. Like an organ pipe closed at one end, it resonates at the frequency whose quarter wavelength equals its length: 343/(4⋅0.025)≈3.4343 / (4 \cdot 0.025) \approx 3.4343/(4⋅0.025)≈3.4 kHz. Around this resonance, the canal raises the sound pressure at the eardrum by about 10 to 20 dB. The boost falls into the range in which consonants such as “s” and “f” differ, and it is one reason why the ear is most sensitive between 2 and 5 kHz.

Middle Ear¶

The middle ear solves a mismatch. The cochlea is filled with fluid, and a sound wave that hits a fluid surface from the air is almost completely reflected, because the fluid resists motion far more than the air does. The acoustic impedance, the product of density and speed of sound, measures this resistance: about 413 Pa·s/m for air and 1.48×1061.48 \times 10^61.48×106 Pa·s/m for water. For a wave that hits the boundary head-on, the transmitted fraction of the energy is 4Z1Z2/(Z1+Z2)2=0.00114 Z_1 Z_2 / (Z_1 + Z_2)^2 = 0.00114Z1​Z2​/(Z1​+Z2​)2=0.0011, about 0.1 percent or a loss of 30 dB.

The middle ear recovers most of this loss. The sound moves the eardrum, which passes the vibration through three small bones, the ossicles: the hammer (malleus), the anvil (incus), and the stirrup (stapes). The footplate of the stapes sits in the oval window, the entrance to the cochlea. The eardrum is much larger than the footplate, so the force collected over a large area acts on a small one, and the ossicles add a lever. Both raise the pressure at the oval window. The round window below it bulges outward when the oval window moves inward, because the fluid cannot be compressed. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the throat and equalizes the air pressure on both sides of the eardrum, which we notice in an airplane or a tunnel.

Two small muscles stiffen the chain of ossicles when a loud sound arrives, the acoustic reflex. It attenuates loud low-frequency sounds, but it needs a few tens of milliseconds to respond and therefore cannot protect the ear against sudden bangs.

Inner Ear and the Cochlear Frequency Map¶

The inner ear consists of the vestibular system, whose semicircular canals sense balance and the motion of the head, and the cochlea, a fluid-filled tube coiled two and a half times like a snail shell. Uncoiled, the cochlea is about 35 mm long. The basilar membrane runs along it and divides it lengthwise. Near the oval window, at the base, the membrane is narrow and stiff; toward the tip, the apex, it becomes wider and more flexible.

A vibration of the stapes sets the fluid in motion and launches a wave along the basilar membrane. Because the stiffness changes along the way, the wave of each frequency grows to a maximum at one place and dies out beyond it: high frequencies peak near the base, low frequencies near the apex. Each place on the membrane responds to its own frequency band, so the cochlea works as a bank of band-pass filters, and the nerve receives a time-frequency representation, something like a spectrogram, rather than a waveform. This arrangement by frequency is called tonotopy, and it continues all the way to the cortex.

Greenwood (1990) fitted a single function to the frequency at each place for humans and many other species.

Hair Cells¶

The organ of Corti sits on the basilar membrane and converts its motion into nerve signals. It holds two kinds of hair cells: one row of about 3,500 inner hair cells and three rows of about 12,000 outer hair cells. Their hair bundles bend when the membrane moves.

The outer hair cells act as amplifiers. They change their length in step with the vibration and push against the membrane, which strengthens and sharpens its motion at the place of the current frequency. This makes the ear sensitive to quiet sounds and lets it separate frequencies that lie close together. The amplification is strongest for quiet sounds and weak for loud ones, which compresses a range of input levels of a factor of a million in pressure into a much smaller range of motion. Loud noise damages the outer hair cells first, and the ear then loses both its sensitivity and its sharp frequency tuning.

The inner hair cells are the actual sensors. When their bundles bend, they release a neurotransmitter onto the fibers of the auditory nerve. Their response is not proportional to the motion: it grows strongly for small movements and saturates for large ones. The cochlear nerve contains about 30,000 fibers, most of them connected to inner hair cells, and each fiber responds best to one frequency, the frequency of its place, within a range of levels. Fibers with different thresholds cover different parts of the loudness range together. This compressive, roughly logarithmic response is why decibels describe loudness well and why audio features take the logarithm of energies.

The cochlea also adapts. When a tone stays constant, the response of the fibers declines within a few tens of milliseconds, while a new or changing sound produces a strong response at its onset. Changes stand out against a steady background. The onset strength of Musical Features, which counts only increases of energy, imitates this emphasis on onsets. Together, amplification, compression, and adaptation make the ear both highly sensitive and highly selective, and features that mimic perception must reflect these non-linear and adaptive properties.

The Auditory Pathway¶

The cochlear nerve joins the vestibular nerve, which carries the signals for balance, to form the eighth cranial nerve. From there, sound information reaches the brain on two routes. The reticular pathway merges the input of all senses and decides which events deserve attention, for example whether a noise should wake us up. The primary auditory pathway analyzes the sound itself. Figure 2 shows its stations in sections of the brain stem, the midbrain, and the forebrain.

  1. The cochlear nuclei in the brain stem receive the fibers of the cochlear nerve. They are the first stage that analyzes the frequency, intensity, and duration of the signal, and some of their cells respond mainly to onsets.

  2. The superior olivary complex, or superior olive, is the first stage that receives input from both ears. It compares the arrival times and the levels of a sound at the two ears, the two main cues for its direction.

  3. The inferior colliculus in the midbrain integrates the direction cues with the frequency analysis and triggers fast reflexes, such as turning the head toward a sudden sound.

  4. The medial geniculate body of the thalamus relays the information to the auditory cortex.

  5. The auditory cortex in the temporal lobe handles both basic and advanced aspects of hearing. Its neurons are arranged in tonotopic maps that help identify sound sources and suppress the distortions caused by reflections. It also processes the fine timing of sounds, which speech and music depend on.

Example: How the brain locates a sound

A sound from the right reaches the right ear first. For a head with a radius of 8.75 cm, the delay is at most 0.0875⋅(π/2+1)/343=0.660.0875 \cdot (\pi/2 + 1) / 343 = 0.660.0875⋅(π/2+1)/343=0.66 ms, for a source directly to the side, and the superior olive detects differences of a few tens of microseconds. For high frequencies, whose wavelengths are shorter than the head (17 cm at 2 kHz), the head also casts an acoustic shadow, and the sound is louder at the near ear. The brain therefore uses mainly time differences for low frequencies and level differences for high frequencies. Neither cue tells front from back, since a source in front and its mirror image behind produce the same differences; the direction-dependent coloring by the pinna resolves this.

The ear thus passes on an organized description of the sound, not the raw signal: separated by frequency with a resolution that decreases with frequency, compressed in level, emphasizing changes, and compared between the two ears. Audio features copy the first three properties. The mel scale and the logarithm of the MFCC features model the cochlear map and the compression, and the onset strength models the adaptation. Classical features rarely use the fourth, since most collections store sound in mono or as stereo without the geometry of a head.
