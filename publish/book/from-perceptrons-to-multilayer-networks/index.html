From Perceptrons to Multilayer Networks

Where this is used

Every network in this book is built from the neuron defined in this section. Word2vec in Word Embeddings is a network with one hidden layer, the transformer layers of Transformer Embeddings contain fully connected feed-forward networks between their attention layers, and the convolutional networks of end in fully connected layers that compute the class scores. Automated Feature Extraction lists the milestones of neural image classification that the history below explains.

A neural network computes its output with many simple units, the neurons, each of which forms a weighted sum of its inputs and passes it through a nonlinear function. This section follows the idea from its beginnings to the multilayer network. We start with a short history, because the setbacks of the field explain many design decisions of today’s networks. Then we look at the perceptron, the first trainable neuron, and at the reason why one layer of perceptrons is not enough. The section ends with the multilayer network and a small example network that Backpropagation and Gradient Descent trains.

Three Waves of Neural Network Research¶

Figure 1 shows the milestones of the first decades. The first wave began with a model of the biological neuron. McCulloch and Pitts described in 1943 a neuron as a threshold unit that fires when the weighted sum of its inputs exceeds a threshold. In 1958, Rosenblatt turned this unit into the perceptron, a neuron that learns its weights from examples, and built it in hardware. Several perceptrons could work in parallel, but each connected the inputs directly to an output. In 1969, Minsky and Papert showed that such a single layer cannot compute even simple functions such as the exclusive or (XOR), which the next subsection explains. Interest in neural networks declined for more than a decade.

The second wave came in the 1980s with networks that have hidden layers between input and output, and with an algorithm to train them. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams popularized backpropagation in 1986; the underlying method of computing derivatives backward through a chain of functions had appeared earlier, in work by Linnainmaa (1970) and Werbos (1974). The revival produced convolutional networks, recurrent networks, and belief networks, many of the concepts of modern deep learning. Yet deeper networks were unstable and slow to train, for reasons that Training Deep Networks explains, and computers were too slow for large networks. From the early 1990s until the early 2010s, support vector machines with well-chosen kernels matched or beat neural networks on most benchmarks.

Convolutional networks existed long before they won. LeNet read handwritten digits in the 1990s, but for almost two decades it was not clear whether convolutional networks would scale from small digit images to large collections of realistic photos. Early networks also depended on many hand-designed steps and solved one task only, as the face detector in CNN Architectures shows. The TensorFlow Playground trains small fully connected networks in the browser and makes the limits of the early multilayer networks tangible.

The third wave started in the mid-2000s. A research program of the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research, led by Hinton, kept the field alive when funding was scarce. In 2006, Hinton, Osindero, and Teh showed how to train deep belief networks layer by layer, and the field adopted the name deep learning. At the same time, graphics processors became fast and programmable. They compute the matrix products of a network in parallel and trained networks in days instead of weeks. Figure 2 shows how the energy efficiency of these processors grew over the following decade. In 2012, a network trained by the Google Brain project on 16,000 processor cores learned to detect cats in YouTube frames without any labels. In the same year, AlexNet won the ImageNet challenge with a large margin and showed for the first time that features learned from data beat features designed by hand on a large benchmark. Figure 3 shows the jump on object detection benchmarks that followed.

The transformer of 2017, which Transformer Embeddings explains, replaced recurrent networks for text and later reached images and audio as well. It is the architecture behind today’s large language models. In 2024, Hopfield and Hinton received the Nobel Prize in Physics for their foundational work on neural networks.

The Perceptron¶

The perceptron is a binary classifier. It maps an input vector x∈RK\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^Kx∈RK to the output 1 or 0 with a weight vector w∈RK\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^Kw∈RK and a bias bbb:

The equation w⊤x+b=0\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0w⊤x+b=0 describes a hyperplane, a line in two dimensions, and the perceptron outputs 1 on one side and 0 on the other. Rosenblatt’s learning rule adjusts the weights only when the perceptron makes a mistake. For each training example x\mathbf{x}x with target t∈{0,1}t \in \{0, 1\}t∈{0,1}, the perceptron computes its output ooo and then updates its parameters.

If the output is correct, t−o=0t - o = 0t−o=0 and nothing changes. If the output is 0 but should be 1, the weights move toward the example; if it is 1 but should be 0, they move away from it. The learning rate α>0\alpha > 0α>0 sets the step size.

In the second epoch, this perceptron now answers 1 for (0,0)(0,0)(0,0), because 0+0+1>00 + 0 + 1 > 00+0+1>0, and the rule subtracts: b=0b = 0b=0. Corrections continue in both directions, ten in total. In the sixth epoch, no example is misclassified anymore, and the perceptron has converged to w=(2,1)\mathbf{w} = (2, 1)w=(2,1) and b=−2b = -2b=−2.

The line 2x1+x2=22x_1 + x_2 = 22x1​+x2​=2 separates (1,1)(1,1)(1,1), where 2+1−2=1>02 + 1 - 2 = 1 > 02+1−2=1>0, from the other three examples. For (1,0)(1,0)(1,0), the sum is exactly 0, so the output is 0: the example lies on the line, which the strict inequality of the step function still classifies correctly.

The rule does not minimize an objective function. It is a heuristic that stops as soon as all examples are classified correctly. If a separating hyperplane exists, that is, if the data is linearly separable, the rule finds one after a finite number of corrections. It finds one of many possible hyperplanes, and which one depends on the order of the examples. If no separating hyperplane exists, the rule never stops correcting.

The exclusive or is the classic counterexample. XOR outputs 1 for (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and 0 for (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) and (1,1)(1,1)(1,1). No line separates the two pairs, because the points of each pair lie on opposite corners of the unit square. A single perceptron therefore cannot compute XOR, and neither can any single layer of perceptrons, since each output is a perceptron of its own.

Figure 5 compares the perceptron with the support vector machine on a separable problem. The perceptron accepts any separating line, while the support vector machine chooses the one with the largest distance to the nearest examples of both classes. Linear Classifiers: Logistic Regression and Support Vector Machines describes the support vector machine, the kernel trick that lets it separate data that is not linearly separable, and how binary classifiers combine into multi-class classifiers.

Multilayer Networks¶

A multilayer network differs from a layer of perceptrons in four ways. It has hidden layers between the input and the output layer. Its neurons use smooth activation functions instead of the step function, so their outputs are real numbers. It is trained by minimizing an objective function, the loss of Losses and Performance Measures. And it uses backpropagation to compute how the loss depends on every weight, which Backpropagation and Gradient Descent derives.

The hidden layer transforms the inputs into a new representation, (h1,h2)(h_1, h_2)(h1​,h2​), in which the two classes are linearly separable. This is the general role of hidden layers: each layer computes a representation in which the next layer’s task is easier. The weights above were chosen by hand. The point of a multilayer network is to learn them, and the perceptron rule cannot do this, because it needs a target for every neuron, and hidden neurons have none.

In matrix notation, a layer computes h=ϕ(Wx+b)\mathbf{h} = \phi(\mathbf{W} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})h=ϕ(Wx+b), with the activation function applied to each component. A deep network stacks many such layers; Figure 8 shows a network with three weight layers. Networks of this kind are called multilayer perceptrons or fully connected networks, although their neurons are no longer perceptrons.

How powerful are such networks? The universal approximation theorem states that a network with a single hidden layer and enough neurons can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region as closely as desired (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991). The theorem says nothing about how many neurons are needed or how to find their weights. In practice, deep networks with many smaller layers learn more efficiently than shallow networks with one huge layer, because each layer can build on the representation of the previous one.
