Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts

Recall the query “uni basel” from the chapter opener. Figure 1 shows two of the many matching pages: a course page that mentions “Universität Basel” in its header (left) and the university home page (right). By term counts alone, both are plausible, and BM25 may even prefer the shorter course page. Classical retrieval treats every occurrence of a term as equal evidence and ignores where it occurs. Web search engines extend the document description with additional signals:

  • proximity of the query terms in the document (the closer, the better)

  • field weighting: occurrences in the title or a heading count more than occurrences in body text

  • anchor text: the words that other pages use when they link to this page

  • location of the user and the page

  • language of the user and the page

  • quality and trust: penalties for spam, thin content, and untrustworthy sites

This section covers these content signals. Link structure, the strongest web-specific signal, gets its own section (Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS), and Learning to Rank shows how a search engine combines all signals into one ranking.

Term Proximity¶

The query “roger federer” is not a request for any page that contains “roger” and “federer”: a marathon result list with a Roger in row 12 and a Federer in row 4,000 matches both terms but answers nothing. In Chapter 3 we added frequent phrases such as “New York City” to the vocabulary as n-grams. That works for a fixed list of known phrases, but it cannot adapt to the new names and phrases that appear with every news event. Names matter a lot on the web, since people, places, and products are among the most frequent search topics. We therefore want a proximity measure that works for any combination of query terms and is computed at query time.

The index provides the raw material. Instead of storing only the term frequency in a posting, a positional index stores every position at which the term occurs in the document. This list is called a hit list. As document DDD, we take the lead section of the English Wikipedia article on Roger Federer (revision of 25 October 2026, CC BY-SA 4.0). We count positions in its plain text without the pronunciation guide; the lead is 626 words long. For the query “roger federer”, the hit lists are:

termpositions in DDD
“roger”1, 532
“federer”2, 57, 119, 165, 184, 235, 279, 337, 373, 419, 472, 509, 533, 560, 619

The article writes the full name only twice: in its first words, “Roger Federer (born 8 August 1981) is a Swiss former professional tennis player”, and near the end, in “the Roger Federer Foundation”. In between, it follows the usual style and refers to him by his surname alone.

At query time, we pair up occurrences of the two terms. The full cross product would create 30 pairs, most of them meaningless: “roger” at position 1 and “federer” at position 619 are more than 600 words apart. Instead, we merge both hit lists into one list sorted by position and form a pair wherever two neighbouring entries belong to different terms (Büttcher, Clarke, and Lushman; see the Further Reading section of the chapter summary). The merged list starts with roger (1), federer (2), federer (57) and ends with federer (509), roger (532), federer (533), federer (560), federer (619).

This gives three pairs: (1, 2), (509, 532), and (532, 533); position 532 belongs to two of them. The thirteen surname-only mentions in between form no pair, since a pair needs both terms. The pair (509, 532) is in reversed order and links the end of one mention to the start of the next: “Federer played an instrumental role in the creation of the Laver Cup team competition. He is also an active philanthropist, having established the Roger”. The rule treats both query terms alike, needs no window size, and works unchanged for queries with more than two terms.

Next, we sort the pair distances into bins, an idea that goes back to the original Google design of Brin and Page. Bin iii counts the pairs at distance iii, for i=1,…,10i = 1, \ldots, 10i=1,…,10. Pairs that are farther apart are “not even close” and do not count. The distances are 1, 23, and 1, so bin 1 holds two pairs, and the reversed pair at distance 23 drops out. Finally, a weight vector that decreases with distance turns the bin counts into a score.

The two pairs that count are the two places where the article writes the full name. The thirteen surname-only mentions earn nothing, a typical weakness of person search, since writers fall back to the surname.

Now take a marathon result list of 5,000 runners: a long table of ranks, names, and finishing times. Six runners have the first name Roger and six the surname Federer, among them a Roger in row 12 and a Federer in row 4,000. By term counts, the list is a serious candidate: it contains “roger” three times as often as the article. Merging the hit lists gives 11 pairs, more than the article’s 3. But every pair joins two different runners hundreds of rows apart, all pairs drop out of the bins, and the list scores 0. Proximity pushes the article above the list.

Production systems use more refined functions. They weight in-order pairs higher than reversed ones, treat a pair that crosses a sentence or paragraph boundary as weaker evidence, and often replace the bins by a smooth function of the distance ddd, such as 1/d21/d^21/d2 in the method of Büttcher et al. Our example treats both orders alike and ignores boundaries. Here this makes no difference: the only reversed pair, which also crosses a sentence boundary, is too far apart to count. The idea stays the same: the closer the query terms appear, the more likely the page is about the combination and not just about each term on its own. Proximity has a storage price. The postings lists of Inverted Files store only the documents that contain a term; a positional index adds every position of the term and is therefore considerably larger. Web search engines pay this price, because the same positions also answer phrase queries such as “new york city” without indexing every n-gram.

Field Weighting with BM25F¶

HTML gives us more than a sequence of words. A term in the <title> was chosen by the author to describe the whole page. A term in an <h1> heading names a main topic, and a term in bold marks something the author wanted to stress. Classical retrieval counts all of these occurrences alike. Figure 2 shows the home page of the University of Basel with every occurrence of “university” and the tag that surrounds it.

The page contains “university” nine times. Grouped by tag, we get one occurrence in the title, two in <h1> headings, two in bold text, three in paragraphs, and one in a link. A natural first idea is to saturate the count in each tag separately, as BM25 does for the whole document, and then add up the weighted results. This idea is flawed. If every field saturates on its own, a spammer can repeat a keyword in the title, in every heading, in bold text, and in the body, and collect the full saturated score once per field. The combination grows almost linearly with the number of fields, and the protection that saturation was meant to provide is gone.

BM25F (BM25 with fields) does it the other way around. It first combines the per-field counts into one weighted term frequency and only then applies the BM25 saturation:

Saturation matters more on the web than in a curated collection, because keyword stuffing is a standard SEO technique (The Web Search Landscape). Field weighting also extends beyond HTML tags: the URL, the domain name, and the anchor texts of incoming links (next subsection) are all fields with their own weights.

Anchor Text¶

The anchor text of a link is the clickable text that leads to another page. It usually describes the target page in a few precise words, chosen not by the target’s author but by someone else. Generic anchors such as “click here” or “more” carry no information, but with thousands of links to a page the useful descriptions dominate. Figure 3 shows two pages that link to the University of Basel home page: a Wikipedia list of Swiss universities and an Erasmus exchange portal. Both describe the target as “University of Basel” or “Universität Basel”.

Anchor text is valuable because it is hard to manipulate. A page author controls every word on their own page, but not the words that thousands of other sites use to link to it. Anchor text also describes pages that contain little text of their own, such as a home page made of images and navigation menus, and it can even describe pages the crawler has never fetched.

Using anchor text requires work at crawl time. When the crawler parses a page, it extracts each link together with its anchor text and stores the pair under the target URL (Crawling the Web). After the crawl, the search engine builds a separate anchor index in which each target page is described by the concatenation of all anchor texts pointing to it. At query time, the anchor index contributes its own score, usually with a higher weight than the page’s own text.

The saturation for anchor counts must be gentler than BM25’s. Many independent pages voting for a description is exactly the evidence we want, so the score should keep growing with the number of votes, only more slowly. A logarithm such as log⁡(1+n)\log(1 + n)log(1+n) for nnn anchor occurrences has this property.

An unbounded score invites manipulation. A Google bomb is a coordinated attempt to attach a keyword to a target page by placing that keyword in a very large number of links. The best-known example linked the phrase “miserable failure” to a biography of George W. Bush in 2003 and 2004 and pushed the page to the top for that query. The countermeasure is to count votes per domain: each domain has at most one vote for how well a term describes a target page.

Location¶

Many queries have a local intent that the user does not spell out. Someone typing “best pizza” on a phone in Basel wants a restaurant within walking distance, not the best pizzeria in Naples. Google introduced a major local-search update, nicknamed Pigeon, in 2014, which tied local results more closely to the user’s location and to the distance to businesses. Today, location influences the ranking of restaurants, shops, services, events, and many general queries.

Location-aware ranking needs three pieces of information:

  1. The location of a page. For businesses, the owners often maintain an entry with address and opening hours in a directory such as Google Maps, so the search engine knows the location exactly. For other pages, the crawler estimates a location from the address on the page, the country domain, or the server’s IP address.

  2. The location of the user. The device may report its GPS position. Otherwise the search engine estimates the location from the IP address, which fails when the user connects through a VPN. If the query names a place, that place takes precedence: “best pizza in Paris” ranks Paris restaurants, even for a user in Basel. The query analyzer recognizes places as named entities (Query Understanding) and maps them to coordinates. This works for landmarks too, as in “best pizza near the Golden Gate Bridge”.

  3. How much location matters for this query. “best pizza” depends strongly on location. “Albert Einstein” is a global query, but a local museum with an Einstein exhibition can still receive a modest local boost.

Language¶

A user who asks for a recipe in German does not want English pages, even if an English page matches the query terms well. Language is therefore a signal of its own. The search engine detects the language of each page at indexing time, with the same classifiers that detect the language of a query (Chapter 3). On the user side, the language of the query, the interface language, the browser settings, and past queries indicate which languages the user reads.

The engine can use this signal in two ways. A classical filter restricts the results to pages in the user’s languages, like the language option of an advanced search form. A filter is simple, but it is all or nothing: it also removes an English page that would be the best result when no good German page exists. Learning to rank (Learning to Rank) instead treats the language match as one feature among many and learns from user behaviour how strongly to prefer pages in the query language. A recipe query then shows German pages first, while a query for an error message of a programming framework may still rank English pages high, because that is where the answers are.

Quality, Spam, and Trust¶

The Web Search Landscape described the kinds of low-quality content on the web, from outdated pages to scams and pages that exist only to rank. Search engines respond by estimating the quality of each page and the trustworthiness of its site. Typical measures include:

  • Penalties for low-quality pages: thin content that wraps a few sentences in ads, pages copied from elsewhere, aggressive keyword stuffing, and clickbait whose content does not deliver what the title promises.

  • Removal of entire sites from the results: link farms that exist only to create links, doorway pages that redirect users elsewhere, malware sites, and illegal content.

  • Site-level trust: signals such as the known ownership of a site, its age, its editorial quality, and its reputation in other sources. A registered news organization or a university earns more trust than an anonymous site created last week.

Quality updates can shift results on a large scale. Google’s 2011 Panda update, which targeted thin and low-quality content, noticeably affected about 12% of US English queries. Since then, quality assessment has moved to machine-learned classifiers. Google uses a spam detection system called SpamBrain, and its human quality raters judge pages by experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness (E-E-A-T); their judgments become training labels for the ranking models (Learning to Rank). Since 2023, large amounts of cheap, machine-generated text have made this defence harder and more important.

Quality signals have a cost. A ranking that rewards established, trusted sites makes it harder for a new site to reach the top, even if its content is excellent. Search engines therefore balance trust against freshness and diversity, a trade-off we meet again in Ranking Without a Query: Feeds.
