Training Deep Networks

Where this is used

Automated Feature Extraction names ReLU activations, dropout, data augmentation, and better optimizers such as Adam as the improvements that made deep networks trainable; this section explains each of them. The transformer layers of Transformer Embeddings combine residual connections with layer normalization, and the fine-tuning in relies on the regularization methods described here.

Backpropagation computes the gradient of any network, but for a long time, networks with more than a few layers did not train well. Adding layers did not improve the results, and larger networks became unstable. This section explains why and describes the techniques that solved the problem: activation functions that keep gradients alive, an initialization that keeps signals at a stable scale, normalization layers, regularization against overfitting, and optimizers that adapt their step sizes. Together, they turned neural networks into deep learning.

Vanishing and Exploding Gradients¶

The example in Backpropagation and Gradient Descent already showed the problem: the gradients of the first layer were about three times smaller than those of the second. Every step back through a sigmoid neuron multiplies the delta with ϕ′(s)=ϕ(s)(1−ϕ(s))\phi'(s) = \phi(s)(1 - \phi(s))ϕ′(s)=ϕ(s)(1−ϕ(s)), and Figure 1 shows that this derivative is at most 14\frac{1}{4}41​, reached at s=0s = 0s=0, and close to zero for large positive or negative sums.

With weights below 1 in absolute value, a delta that travels back through nnn sigmoid layers is multiplied by at most (14)n(\frac{1}{4})^n(41​)n, so it shrinks by a factor of at least 4n4^n4n: to at most 6⋅10−26 \cdot 10^{-2}6⋅10−2 of its size for two layers, 10-3 for five, and 10-6 for ten. The first layers of a deep network then receive almost no gradient and learn extremely slowly, even when their weights are far from good values. This is the vanishing gradient problem.

The opposite happens with large weights. If the products of weights and derivatives along the backward path exceed 1, the deltas grow exponentially with the depth. The updates become huge, the weights grow, and the next gradients are even larger, until the computation overflows. This is the exploding gradient problem. Both effects grow with the number of layers, which is why the second wave of neural network research stalled at shallow networks. Recurrent networks, which apply the same weights at every time step, suffer even more; Recurrent Networks returns to them.

Activation Functions¶

The rectified linear unit (ReLU), ϕ(s)=max⁡(0,s)\phi(s) = \max(0, s)ϕ(s)=max(0,s), replaced the sigmoid in deep networks. Its derivative is 1 for positive sums and 0 for negative ones, so a delta passes back through an active neuron unchanged instead of shrinking by a factor of at least 4. ReLU is also cheap to compute, and its outputs are exactly zero for about half of the neurons, which makes the representation sparse.

The zero derivative for negative sums has a drawback. A neuron whose sum is negative for every training example never receives a gradient and stops learning; such neurons are called dead. The leaky ReLU keeps a small slope for negative sums, ϕ(s)=0.01 s\phi(s) = 0.01\, sϕ(s)=0.01s for s<0s < 0s<0, so that its derivative never becomes zero and a dead neuron can recover. The parametric ReLU learns the slope α\alphaα for negative sums as a parameter of the network. Figure 2 compares ReLU and leaky ReLU.

ReLU and leaky ReLU have a kink at zero, where the derivative jumps. The exponential linear unit (ELU) is smooth for negative sums and approaches −α-\alpha−α there, which Figure 3 shows together with its derivative:

Transformers such as BERT and GPT use the Gaussian error linear unit (GELU), ϕ(s)=s⋅Φ(s)\phi(s) = s \cdot \Phi(s)ϕ(s)=s⋅Φ(s), where Φ\PhiΦ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution. It behaves like ReLU for large positive and negative sums and is smooth around zero. Without any nonlinear activation, a deep network would collapse to a single linear map, since a product of matrices is again a matrix.

The output layer is an exception. Classification networks end with a softmax that turns the logits into probabilities, trained with the cross-entropy loss; Losses and Performance Measures explains both.

Initialization¶

The initial weights decide whether signals survive the forward pass. In a layer with nnn inputs and weights drawn with standard deviation σ\sigmaσ, the variance of each sum is about nσ2n \sigma^2nσ2 times the variance of the inputs. If nσ2n\sigma^2nσ2 is below 1, activations shrink from layer to layer; if it is above 1, they grow. Gradients behave the same way in the backward pass. He initialization draws the weights of a ReLU layer with σ=2/n\sigma = \sqrt{2/n}σ=2/n​, where the factor 2 compensates for the half of the neurons that ReLU sets to zero. For sigmoid and tanh layers, Glorot initialization (also called Xavier initialization) uses σ=2/(nin+nout)\sigma = \sqrt{2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}σ=2/(nin​+nout​)​.

Normalization¶

Even with a good initialization, the distribution of a layer’s inputs changes during training, because the layers before it change their weights. Batch normalization stabilizes these distributions. For each feature, it computes the mean μB\mu_{\mathbb{B}}μB​ and the variance σB2\sigma_{\mathbb{B}}^2σB2​ over the current mini-batch, normalizes the value, and then scales and shifts it with two learned parameters γ\gammaγ and β\betaβ:

x^=x−μBσB2+ε,y=γ x^+β\hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu_{\mathbb{B}}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathbb{B}}^2 + \varepsilon}}, \qquad y = \gamma\, \hat{x} + \betax^=σB2​+ε​x−μB​​,y=γx^+β
(2)

The small constant ε\varepsilonε avoids a division by zero. The learned scale and shift let the network undo the normalization where that helps. During training, the layer also keeps running averages of the means and variances; at inference, it uses these averages, so that the output for an example does not depend on the other examples in its batch. Batch normalization needs mini-batches large enough for reliable estimates, which is difficult for tasks with high-resolution inputs such as object detection, where only a few images fit into memory at once.

Layer normalization computes the mean and the variance over the features of a single example instead of over the batch. It needs no batch statistics, behaves identically in training and inference, and works for sequences of any length. Transformers and recurrent networks use it. Group normalization divides the features into groups and normalizes each group separately, a middle ground that works well for convolutional networks with small batches. Weight standardization normalizes the weights of each layer instead of the activations and is often combined with group normalization. In practice, convolutional networks for image classification mostly use batch normalization, and transformers layer normalization.

Regularization¶

A deep network with millions of parameters can memorize its training data, the overfitting of Over- and Underfitting. Figure 4 shows the typical signs on a validation set: an almost perfect training accuracy, a much lower validation accuracy, and a gap that grows during training, sometimes with a phase in which the validation accuracy falls again. With regularization, a gap remains, but the validation accuracy follows the training accuracy much more closely. Making the network smaller is rarely the best remedy, because small networks cannot learn complex tasks. Regularization keeps the large network and constrains how it learns.

More training data is the most effective regularizer. Data augmentation creates new examples from existing ones without new labels: for images, small rotations and crops, changes of brightness, added noise, or blur. The augmented examples also make the model robust against these small changes. Language models obtain their data through self-supervised learning, described in Self-Supervised Learning: they learn to predict masked or next tokens in text collections of trillions of tokens. For a fixed computing budget, Hoffmann and colleagues found that a language model is best trained on about 20 tokens per parameter, so a model with one billion parameters needs about 20 billion tokens. A pretrained model can then be fine-tuned for a task with much less labeled data.

Early stopping monitors the loss on the validation set during training and keeps the weights of the epoch with the lowest validation loss, before the network starts to fit the noise of the training data.

Dropout changes the network during training (Figure 5). In each training step, every neuron of a layer is kept with probability ppp and dropped with probability 1−p1 - p1−p; a dropped neuron outputs 0 and does not pass back any gradient, so its weights receive no update in that step. Each step therefore trains a different thinned network, and a neuron cannot rely on the presence of specific other neurons. At inference, all neurons are active; to keep the expected input of the next layer the same as in training, their outgoing weights are multiplied by ppp. Libraries usually apply the equivalent inverted dropout, which divides the kept outputs by ppp during training and leaves the network unchanged at inference. Dropout can be seen as training many networks that share their weights and averaging them for the prediction.

Optimizers¶

Plain SGD uses one learning rate for all parameters and all steps. The learning rate can follow a schedule: it can stay fixed, decay after a number of epochs or when the validation loss stops improving, or grow linearly at the start of training, a warm-up that large transformer models need. Momentum, described in Backpropagation and Gradient Descent, accelerates progress along consistent directions.

RMSprop adapts the learning rate for each parameter. It keeps a running average of the squared gradients and divides each step by its square root, so that parameters with consistently large gradients take smaller steps and parameters with small gradients larger ones. Adam combines this with momentum. It keeps running averages of the gradients, m\mathbf{m}m, and of their squares, v\mathbf{v}v, and corrects both for their initialization at zero:

Adam’s steps depend on the direction and the consistency of the gradients, not on their scale, which makes the learning rate much easier to choose.

Adam is the default optimizer for most networks today. For transformers, the variant AdamW applies the weight decay directly to the weights instead of adding it to the gradient, because Adam’s division by v^\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}v^​ would otherwise weaken the decay for parameters with large gradients.
