Naive Bayes

Where this is used

Intent Routing and Classification detects the language of a query and routes it to a backend with Naive Bayes classifiers; this section gives the derivations behind them. Naive Bayes is also the baseline against which Text Classification compares transformer classifiers, and Tasks and Experience uses it as the example of a classifier that copes with missing features.

Naive Bayes goes back to a line of probabilistic thinking that begins with Thomas Bayes, whose work on inverse probability led to Bayes’ rule: how to update a belief when new evidence appears. The Naive Bayes classifier turns this rule into a practical method by assuming that the features of an example are independent of each other once the class is known. The assumption is rarely true, but it makes the model fast to train, easy to interpret, and robust when data is sparse. Text collections are the classic case: they are high-dimensional and dominated by rare terms, and Naive Bayes breaks the evidence into one small contribution per term. Learning reduces to counting how often terms occur in the documents of each class, which scales to large collections and updates easily when new documents arrive. For these reasons, Naive Bayes remains a standard baseline, and it performs well when only a few labeled examples per class exist.

The likelihood is the hard part. A document described by the presence of 10,000 terms has 210,0002^{10{,}000}210,000 possible feature vectors, and no training set can estimate a probability for each of them. Naive Bayes assumes that the features are conditionally independent given the class: once we know that a mail is spam, the presence of “money” tells us nothing more about the presence of “win”. Each factor involves a single feature and can be estimated from a few counts.

The Decision Rule¶

The classifier chooses the class with the highest posterior, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) decision. The evidence P(x)P(\mathbf{x})P(x) is the same for all classes and does not change which class wins, so we can drop it. A product of many small probabilities quickly falls below the smallest number a computer can represent, which is called underflow. Since the logarithm is monotonic, it does not change the winner either, and it turns the product into a sum.

Key Formula: Naive Bayes Decision Rule
k∗=argmax⁡k  (log⁡P(Ck)+∑j=1Mlog⁡P(xj∣Ck))k^* = \operatorname{argmax}_k \; \Bigl( \log P(C_k) + \sum_{j=1}^{M} \log P(x_j ∣ C_k) \Bigr)k∗=argmaxk​(logP(Ck​)+j=1∑M​logP(xj​∣Ck​))
(3)

Each feature adds its own piece of evidence for or against each class, and the prior adds what we knew before. The class with the largest total wins.

Missing features are easy to handle. If a feature is unknown for an example, its term is left out of the sum for all classes. The decision then rests on the observed features alone, which is what Tasks and Experience means by a classifier that marginalizes out missing inputs.

Smoothing¶

What happens if a term never occurs in the training documents of a class? Its estimated probability is zero, and the product ∏jP(xj∣Ck)\prod_j P(x_j ∣ C_k)∏j​P(xj​∣Ck​) becomes zero for every new document that contains the term, no matter how strongly the other terms support the class. One unseen word vetoes the class. Laplace smoothing, also called add-one smoothing, prevents this. It adds one to every count and adjusts the denominator so that the probabilities still sum to 1:

The left form is for categorical features with ∣Vj∣|\mathbb{V}_j|∣Vj​∣ possible values, the right form for multinomial term counts over a vocabulary of MMM terms. Every value receives a small probability, taken from the frequent ones.

Figure 1 shows the effect: probability mass moves from the frequent values to the values that were never observed, and the distribution keeps its shape. For Bernoulli features, the smoothed estimate is pk,j=(Nk(xj=1)+1)/(Nk+2)p_{k,j} = (N_k(x_j = 1) + 1)/(N_k + 2)pk,j​=(Nk​(xj​=1)+1)/(Nk​+2), which keeps every probability strictly between 0 and 1. The same problem appears whenever a probability is estimated from counts, and smoothing is the standard remedy.

Example: A tiny spam filter

The training set has three spam mails, “win money now”, “win prize money”, and “cheap money offer”, and three normal mails, “meeting tomorrow now”, “project meeting notes”, and “lunch tomorrow”. The vocabulary has M=11M = 11M=11 terms; the spam mails contain 9 term occurrences, the normal mails 8. Both classes have the prior 0.5. The new mail is “win money tomorrow”.

Feature Selection¶

A text classifier with a large vocabulary adds a lot of noise: most terms say nothing about the class. Feature selection keeps only the terms whose presence or absence is correlated with the class. For each term and class, a four-field table counts the documents with and without the term, inside and outside the class. A strong correlation in any field marks the term as discriminative. The chi-square test and the mutual information, introduced in Information Theory, are the most common measures; a simpler alternative selects the terms with the highest weight, as in the term weighting of classical text retrieval.

Strengths and Limits¶

The independence assumption is wrong for almost every real dataset: “machine” and “learning” occur together far more often than chance predicts. Naive Bayes therefore double-counts correlated evidence, and its probabilities are usually too extreme, close to 0 or 1. Its decisions are nevertheless often right, because classification only needs the correct class to have the highest score, not a correct probability. When calibrated probabilities matter, for example to choose a decision threshold as in Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves, the scores need to be recalibrated on validation data. Discriminative models such as the logistic regression of Linear Classifiers: Logistic Regression and Support Vector Machines learn the decision boundary directly and avoid the independence assumption, at the cost of needing more training data.
