Metadata and Its Limits

The quickest way across the semantic gap avoids the content altogether. If the Taj Mahal photo comes with the description “Taj Mahal, mausoleum in Agra, India, built by Shah Jahan”, then the query “Taj Mahal” matches the description and the photo is found. Every tool from the previous chapters applies unchanged: tokenization, an inverted file, BM25, embeddings, and filters. This section follows this keyword path from its strengths to its two fundamental problems: someone has to write the descriptions, and the descriptions are not always true.

The Keyword Path¶

Metadata is additional information attached to a media item that describes its content, its context, or its technical properties. For retrieval, the important property of metadata is that it is text, or at least structured values such as dates and coordinates. Once each item has metadata, a multimedia search becomes a text search over the metadata, and the semantic gap is bridged by whoever wrote it.

Metadata comes from three sources. Humans can annotate each item manually. The system can capture or harvest metadata automatically: the camera writes the date and the GPS position into the file, and a web page provides captions and surrounding text. Finally, machine learning models can analyze the content and produce labels or descriptions. This section covers the first two sources; the third is the subject of Automated Feature Extraction and Multimodal Transformers.

Metadata values describe the content at three levels:

  • Generic metadata names general classes: “building”, “sky”, “outside”, “summer”.

  • Specific metadata names individual entities and facts: “Taj Mahal”, “Agra”, “2006”, “UNESCO World Heritage Site”.

  • Abstract metadata names subjective concepts and interpretations: “love”, “devotion”, “iconic”, “beautiful”.

Annotation schemas organize these values into facets, each describing one aspect of the item. Figure 1 annotates the Taj Mahal photo along five facets: objects (from “building, water, sky” to “Taj Mahal”), space (from “outside” to “India, Uttar Pradesh, Agra”), events (from “tourism” to a specific expert meeting in 2006), time (“summer, daytime” and “2006”), and context (topic, related persons, abstract concepts such as “love, death, devotion”, and the history of the building). Each facet holds generic and specific values side by side.

Some of these values can be read from the pixels: “outside”, “sky”, “daytime”, and “building” are visible to any careful observer, human or machine. Others, such as “UNESCO”, “Mumtaz Mahal”, or “completed 1648”, require an annotator who knows the context, because no analysis of the pixels can produce them. This is the second part of the semantic gap from The Semantic Gap, and it explains why metadata remains valuable even when automated content analysis becomes very good. Facets also map directly to the filters and facet counts of Filtering, Facets, and Compression: a user narrows the results to “location: Agra” or “year: 2006” with one click.

Metadata Standards¶

Metadata is only useful across systems if everybody agrees where it is stored and what the fields mean. Several standards define the fields for the common media types:

StandardMediaTypical fieldsWritten by
EXIFPhotos (JPEG, TIFF, HEIF)Date and time taken, camera model, exposure, focal length, orientation, GPS coordinatesThe camera, automatically
IPTC Photo Metadata (stored as XMP)PhotosHeadline, caption, keywords, creator, copyright, location, persons shownPhotographers, editors, agencies
Dublin CoreAny resource15 elements: title, creator, subject, description, publisher, contributor, date, type, format, identifier, source, language, relation, coverage, rightsLibraries, archives, repositories
ID3Audio (MP3)Title, artist, album, year, genre, track number, cover image, lyricsLabels, music software, users
schema.orgWeb pages (JSON-LD)Types such as ImageObject, VideoObject, MusicRecording: name, description, thumbnail, upload date, durationWebsite owners, for search engines

EXIF is the metadata that every digital camera and smartphone writes without being asked. It is generic but precise: a timestamp and a GPS position answer the query “photos taken in Paris in 2023” without any content analysis. Many social platforms strip EXIF data on upload, mainly to protect the privacy of the uploader, so this valuable source often disappears on the way to the web. IPTC fields carry the editorial metadata of the news industry. They are usually stored inside the image file in XMP, an extensible metadata format that photo management software reads and writes. Dublin Core is the common denominator of libraries and digital archives. ID3 tags travel inside MP3 files and are the reason a music player shows title and artist. Finally, schema.org markup tells search engines what an embedded image or video shows; a VideoObject can even list the key moments of a video with their timestamps.

The standards fix the names of the fields, not their values. Two photographers who both fill in the IPTC keyword field may write “Taj Mahal” and “religious building”. The standards solve the problem of where to find the description; they do not solve the problem of what the description says.

Curated Catalogues¶

In some domains, metadata is excellent. IMDb holds records for millions of movies and episodes. Each record follows a fixed schema with cast, crew, release dates, genres, plot summaries, and user ratings, and links to related records. Volunteers, actors, crews, and studios maintain the content. MusicBrainz is a community-maintained open encyclopedia of music with artists, releases, recordings, and their relationships. Figure 2 shows its record for the song “Paparazzi” next to the lyrics of the song. Together, the two sources provide rich text features that cannot be derived from the video or audio signal alone.

With curated catalogues, the metadata alone often bridges the semantic gap. Searching for a movie by its director or a song by its lyrics never touches the video or audio. The content itself is only needed for special tasks, such as identifying the song that is currently playing. Curated catalogues exist where commercial or community interest pays for the effort: the revenue of streaming platforms and the enthusiasm of fans cover the cost of the annotation. For an arbitrary photo collection, no one pays.

Manual Annotation Does Not Scale¶

Human annotation has become an industry because machine learning needs labeled training data, and these labels are metadata at the same time. Crowdsourcing platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk give access to over 500,000 independent workers who perform small, well-defined tasks for a fixed price. Figure 3 shows the setup of a labeling job in Amazon SageMaker Ground Truth. The requester chooses the task type (image classification, bounding boxes, semantic segmentation, or label verification), the workforce, and a price per task. The suggested price for a segmentation task that takes 90 seconds to 2 minutes is $0.84.

Domain experts, such as archivists who recognize historical persons, cost far more than crowd workers. And the archive keeps growing every day.

Crowdsourcing scales the annotation to millions of items, but only for tasks that untrained workers can perform, and only for collections whose value justifies the cost. The fine-tuning of large language models relies on the same workforce: thousands of workers rated the quality of generated answers. Once a model is trained on such labels, it produces labels automatically, which is the step that Automated Feature Extraction takes.

Cost is not the only problem; consistency is another. Suppose two workers annotate the Taj Mahal photo. Worker A writes “Taj Mahal, India, iconic building, 17th century”. Worker B writes “religious building, great weather, few people outside, nice”. Both annotations are correct, but they sit at different levels. Worker A knows the building and uses specific terms; Worker B describes what any observer sees and uses generic terms. Similar relationships appear everywhere in language: horse and mammal, Matterhorn and mountain, Italy and Europe. The embeddings of Building Semantic Search can relate “Taj Mahal” to “religious building”, but only to a degree. Worker B’s “nice” is an abstract and subjective judgment: two people rarely agree on it, yet it may be exactly what a user means with “great architecture in India”.

The depth of the annotation varies as much as its level. Figure 4 contrasts a stock photo with a short list of keywords and an expert article that labels every architectural element of the building, from the finial on the dome to the calligraphy on the arches. The keyword list may be too thin to rank the photo for “Taj Mahal architecture”; the expert article is so detailed that most of its terms never appear in a query.

Stock photo agencies and media archives therefore maintain concise keyword lists and add faceted navigation for the most requested facets: prominent persons, locations, brands, and decades. Annotators examine the photos of an event and tag only selected shots that show known persons. Figure 5 shows the benefit and the drawback. A journalist who needs a picture of the tennis player Darlene Hard for an obituary browses a curated folder instead of thousands of pictures. But a photo of children playing tennis today remains unannotated, because none of them is famous yet. If one of them becomes a champion in twenty years, the archive holds the early photos and cannot find them. The archivists of The Semantic Gap face the same limit: a clip that was not judged valuable when it was filed cannot be found when it is needed.

Harvesting Context from the Web¶

When images and videos are embedded in web pages, the page provides metadata at almost no cost. The HTML tags <img> and <a> carry attributes such as alt and title that describe the referenced object. Figure 6 shows a news article about the Taj Mahal next to its HTML source: the image has the title “Taj Mahal” and an alt text that repeats the headline, and a caption element below the image repeats the name. The headline, the article text, and the page title contain many more of the keywords a user would type.

The difficulty is to decide which text belongs to the image. On early web pages, the describing text sat next to the <img> tag in the source, so a window of a few dozen tokens around the tag, plus the headers and the page title, captured most of the useful keywords. On modern pages, scripts and style sheets place elements on the screen independently of their position in the source. Extractors therefore judge proximity on the rendered page: text in the same visual zone as the image counts more than a sidebar with unrelated news, as Figure 7 shows. Web search engines add the anchor texts of links that point to an image, which describe it in the words of other authors, as Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts explains.

Harvested image-text pairs have since gained a second role. Open datasets such as LAION-5B collected billions of images together with their alt texts from web pages, and contrastive models such as CLIP in Multimodal Transformers learn from such pairs. The cheap and noisy metadata of the web became the training signal for the models that now match images and text without any metadata.

Can We Trust Metadata?¶

Harvested metadata is cheap, but it is written by people who may have their own goals. In 2001, Cory Doctorow listed in his essay “Metacrap” (see the Further Reading of the chapter summary) “seven insurmountable obstacles” between the world and a “meta-utopia” in which all content is accurately described:

  1. People lie. Content creators publish misleading metadata to attract traffic.

  2. People are lazy. Many publishers do not bother to annotate their content at all.

  3. People are stupid. Many publishers lack the skill to catalogue their content well.

  4. Mission impossible: know thyself. Even honest creators describe their own content inaccurately.

  5. Schemas aren’t neutral. Every classification scheme reflects the views and priorities of its designers.

  6. Metrics influence results. Agreeing in advance on what to record and how to measure it decides what can be found later.

  7. There’s more than one way to describe something. Description is subjective; different perspectives are all valid.

This does not make metadata worthless. Curated catalogues such as IMDb and MusicBrainz are highly reliable, because their maintainers have an interest in quality and control the content. Observational metadata, which describes an item in the words of others rather than its owner, is harder to manipulate. Web search engines trust the anchor texts of links from other pages more than the keywords the page owner chooses. Even this signal can be manipulated by coordinated campaigns, as the Google bombs described in Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts showed. The lesson is to weigh each metadata source by how much it can be trusted, and to evaluate it before relying on it.

Metadata is a claim, not a fact

A keyword attached to an image states what somebody wanted the image to be found for. A common mistake is to treat metadata as ground truth when evaluating a retrieval system. Before using metadata as relevance labels or as filters, check who wrote it, what interest they had, and whether a second source agrees.

The keyword path thus fails in two ways. Manual annotation is too expensive for arbitrary collections, and harvested metadata cannot always be trusted. Both problems point in the same direction: describe the content from the content itself, automatically and consistently. The next section starts with the simplest form of such descriptions.
