This section follows the path of light from a surface to the cortex. We start with the physical signal, then look at how the eye samples it, how fine these samples are compared with other species, and how the retina and the brain process them.

Light, Illumination, and Reflectance¶

Every image starts with light that falls on a surface and leaves it toward a viewer. Two quantities describe this interaction. The illumination l(x,y,z)l(x,y,z)l(x,y,z) is the amount of light that arrives at a point in space. We measure it in lux, a unit that weights the power at each wavelength by the sensitivity of the human eye. The reflectance r(x,y,z)r(x,y,z)r(x,y,z) is the fraction of that light that the surface at this point sends back. It lies between 0 for a surface that absorbs all light and 1 for a surface that reflects all of it.

Part of the light is reflected as in a mirror, at the same angle θ\thetaθ to the surface normal at which it arrived. The rest is scattered diffusely in all directions. The viewer sees only the light that leaves the fragment toward the eye. A small change in the direction, the intensity, or the color of the light source therefore changes how the same surface looks.

The right side of the figure shows the reflectance of natural surfaces for wavelengths from 0.4 to 2.0 micrometers. Snow reflects about 80 to 90 percent of the visible light. Water reflects only a few percent at all wavelengths. Vegetation reflects little visible light, has a small peak in the green near 550 nm, and reflects strongly in the near infrared above 700 nm, which we cannot see. Leaves look green because chlorophyll absorbs mainly blue light (around 430 to 455 nm) and red light (around 640 to 665 nm). Green light is absorbed less, and the cell structures of the leaf scatter it back toward the viewer.

These curves show that the physical signal is a rich, continuous function of the wavelength. Human vision and camera sensors reduce it to three numbers per point. Color Systems describes this reduction in detail.

The two factors vary on very different scales. Reflectance, in contrast, spans from about 1 percent for a black fabric to about 90 percent for fresh snow. The illumination thus changes by a factor of 100,000 and more between day and night, the reflectance only by a factor of about one hundred.

The Eye and Its Photoreceptors¶

The eye converts light into electrochemical signals that the brain can interpret. Figure 2 shows its structure. Light enters through the cornea, passes the pupil, and goes through the lens. Together, the cornea and the lens focus the image of an object onto the retina at the back of the eye. The lens changes its shape to focus on objects at different distances, and the iris changes the size of the pupil to adapt to the light level. A camera does the same with its focus and its aperture. The image on the retina is upside down, and the brain interprets it correctly.

The magnified part of the figure shows a surprising detail of the retina. Light passes through several layers of nerve cells before it reaches the photoreceptors, which point away from the light toward the pigment epithelium. There are two kinds of photoreceptors, rods and cones, and they serve different purposes.

Cones provide sharp, detailed, and colored vision in daylight. They are concentrated in the macula and in its center, the fovea, which is the part of the retina we point at whatever we look at. There are three types of cones with different sensitivity curves, shown on the right of the figure:

  • S cones (short wavelengths) peak near 420 nm, in the violet-blue.

  • M cones (medium wavelengths) peak near 534 nm, in the green.

  • L cones (long wavelengths) peak near 564 nm, in the yellow-green.

The common names “blue”, “green”, and “red” cones are convenient but misleading for the L cones. Their peak is not in the red. They earn their name because their sensitivity reaches furthest into the long wavelengths, so red light excites them much more than the M cones. The M and L curves overlap strongly. A single wavelength therefore excites two or three cone types at once, and the brain derives color from the comparison of their responses.

Rods dominate the periphery of the retina and work best in low light. They are essential for night vision and for noticing movement at the edge of the visual field. All rods share one pigment with a peak near 498 nm. With only one type of receptor, the rods cannot distinguish colors, which is why we see shades of gray at night.

The numbers of photoreceptors are large and vary between people. Curcio et al. (1990) counted an average of 4.6 million cones (4.1 to 5.3 million) and 92 million rods (78 to 107 million) per retina, with up to 199,000 cones per square millimeter in the center of the fovea. Many textbooks quote rounded values of 6 million cones and 120 million rods; the measured averages are lower.

The three cone types are far from equally frequent. S cones make up only about 7 percent of the cones near the fovea (Curcio et al., 1991). They are irregularly spaced, missing entirely in a small zone of about 0.35 degrees at the center of the fovea, and most dense in a ring just outside it. The ratio of L to M cones varies even more. In eight men with normal color vision, Hofer et al. (2005) measured ratios from 1.1:1 to 16.5:1. Fixed percentages for the three cone types should therefore be read as one example, not as a constant of human vision.

These variations do not cause color blindness: all the men in the study above saw colors normally. Color blindness arises when one cone type is missing or when its pigment is shifted to a different wavelength. The most common forms affect the L or the M cones and make red and green hard to tell apart.

Visual Acuity¶

Visual acuity measures how fine a detail the eye can resolve. The standard test is the Snellen chart: rows of letters of decreasing size, read from a distance of 20 feet (6 meters). A result of 20/20, written 6/6 in metric units, means that the eye resolves the critical details of a letter, such as the gap in a “C” or the width of a stroke, when they cover a visual angle of 1 arcminute (1/60 of a degree). In the European Union, a car driver needs a binocular acuity of at least 0.5 with both eyes, which is a MAR of 2 arcminutes or 20/40. The German screening test for a car licence is stricter and asks for 0.7, a MAR of about 1.4 arcminutes.

Vision in Other Species¶

Animals perceive the world very differently. Figure 3 shows on the left the same farmhouse scene as a human sees it and as a cat sees it: the cat’s version has fewer colors and much less detail. The right side shows the four cone types of goldfish and zebrafish, which reach into the ultraviolet.

Cats have two cone types, with peaks near 450 nm and 550 nm, so their color vision is limited. Their acuity of 20/100 to 20/200 means that a cat must be at 6 meters to see what a human sees at 20 to 30 meters. In return, cats see in dim light with about one-sixth of the light that humans need. They have many rods and a reflective layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum, which sends the light that passed the photoreceptors back through them for a second chance of absorption. This layer also makes cat eyes shine when a light falls on them at night. The field of view of a cat is about 200 degrees, compared with about 180 degrees for humans, which helps with hunting but does not add detail.

Dogs are also dichromats. Their two cone types peak near 429 nm and 555 nm, and their color vision resembles red-green color blindness in humans. Their acuity of about 20/75 is a bit better than that of cats but still far below human acuity. Asian elephants resolve gaps of 8 to 11 arcminutes, about 20/200. Small rodents see much less clearly: rats resolve about 1 cycle per degree and mice about 0.5. Insects see with compound eyes, whose acuity is limited by the angle between neighboring ommatidia, the small units of the eye. Most insects resolve less than 1 cycle per degree, and humans resolve more than a hundred times more detail than a fruit fly.

Birds of prey are the exception. The wedge-tailed eagle resolves 132 to 143 cycles per degree (Reymond, 1985), about twice the human limit. This lets it spot prey from great distances. Claims that eagles see four to eight times sharper than humans are not supported by measurements. Many birds also have four types of cones instead of three: they are tetrachromats, and the fourth cone type is sensitive to violet or ultraviolet light that humans cannot see. Goldfish and zebrafish are tetrachromats as well, with an extra cone type for ultraviolet light, useful for detecting UV light under water. The curves in the figure are schematic, with peaks near 370, 445, 508, and 565 nm. Measured peaks in zebrafish are 361, 411, 482, and 565 nm (Allison et al., 2004).

The comparison has a practical consequence for retrieval. Color spaces such as RGB are built for the three human cone types. They describe what a human sees, not what a bird, a fish, or a dog sees, and they are the right choice exactly because a human judges the search results.

Processing in the Retina¶

The retina does more than capture light. It already processes the signal, and this processing inspired the use of convolutions in deep learning. Figure 4 shows the main cell types and how they respond to light.

The photoreceptors show an unusual chemical behavior. In the dark, they release the neurotransmitter glutamate continuously. Light reduces this release. Most sensory receptors work the other way round and release more transmitter when they are stimulated. The continuous release in the dark costs energy, but it lets one signal carry changes in both directions: more light means less glutamate, less light means more.

The next stage, the bipolar cells, reads this signal in two opposite ways. In the primate retina, each bipolar cell collects its input from either rods or cones, not from both. ON bipolar cells respond to light with depolarization, which means they become more active when the light increases. OFF bipolar cells respond with hyperpolarization: they become less active in light and more active when the light decreases. Bipolar cells do not fire spikes. They signal with graded changes of their membrane potential.

The ganglion cells combine the signals of several bipolar cells and are the output neurons of the retina. Their axons form the optic nerve, and they send their signals as spikes. Each ganglion cell responds to light in a small region of the visual field, its receptive field. The receptive field has a center and a surround with opposite effects. An on-center ganglion cell fires strongly when light falls on the center and the surround is dark, and it is suppressed when light falls only on the surround. An off-center cell does the opposite. The right side of the figure shows the key observation: if light covers both center and surround, the cell barely reacts. Ganglion cells therefore do not measure brightness. They detect local contrast, such as a bright spot, a dark spot, or an edge that passes through the receptive field.

The retina also compresses the signal. About 97 million rods and cones feed only 0.7 to 1.5 million ganglion cells (Curcio & Allen, 1990). In the periphery, many rods converge onto one bipolar cell, which pools light and increases sensitivity at the cost of detail. In the fovea, convergence is low, so that each cone keeps an almost private line to the brain and the image stays sharp. A bipolar cell can also feed several ganglion cells, as the left side of the figure shows.

Two further cell types refine this processing. Horizontal cells connect neighboring photoreceptors, and amacrine cells connect neighboring bipolar and ganglion cells. Both act as inhibitors: an active cell suppresses its neighbors. This lateral inhibition amplifies the difference between strong and weak signals and sharpens contrast. It also exaggerates boundaries. Near the border between a dark and a bright region, the bright side looks brighter and the dark side looks darker than they are, an illusion known as Mach bands.

After-images, the visual impressions that persist after a stimulus is gone, have a different cause. When we look at a bright pattern for a long time, the photoreceptors and the retinal neurons adapt and become less sensitive. When we then look at a uniform surface, the adapted regions respond less than their neighbors, and we see a negative image of the pattern until the adaptation fades.

From the Retina to the Visual Cortex¶

Figure 5 shows the path of the signal from the eyes to the visual cortex. The optic nerves of both eyes meet at the optic chiasm. There, the fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross to the other side, while the fibers from the temporal halves stay on their side. As a result, each side of the brain receives the opposite half of the visual field from both eyes: the left visual field is processed by the right hemisphere, and the right visual field by the left hemisphere. After the chiasm, the optic tract leads to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) in the thalamus, and from there the optic radiation, labeled geniculocalcarine tract in the figure, leads to the primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe.

The lateral geniculate nucleus is more than a relay station. Its neurons have center-surround receptive fields similar to those of retinal ganglion cells, and it receives strong feedback from the visual cortex. The LGN has six main layers. Each layer receives input from only one eye; the signals of the two eyes are combined later, in V1. Layers 1 and 2, the magnocellular layers, carry fast signals with high contrast sensitivity that are well suited for motion but carry little color. Layers 3 to 6, the parvocellular layers, carry signals for color and fine detail of form. Thin koniocellular layers between them carry the blue-yellow signals of the S cones.

The primary visual cortex detects edges and their orientations. Hubel and Wiesel (1962) found two kinds of neurons there. Simple cells respond to an edge or a bar with a particular orientation at a particular position in their receptive field. Complex cells respond to the same orientation anywhere within a larger receptive field, so they tolerate small shifts. Neurons in the visual cortex fire when particular patterns appear in their receptive fields. Lower levels of the system respond to simple patterns such as edges. Higher levels respond to more complex patterns: area V4 to color and form, area MT (also called V5) to motion, and regions of the inferior temporal cortex to objects and faces.

From V1, the information follows two main paths to these higher levels (Goodale & Milner, 1992):

  • The ventral stream leads from V1 into the temporal lobe. It handles form recognition and object representation, answers the question “what is it?”, and connects to long-term memory.

  • The dorsal stream leads from V1 into the parietal lobe. It processes motion and the location of objects and coordinates eye and arm movements, for example when we reach for a cup.

The cortex does not represent all parts of the visual field equally. Cortical magnification means that the brain uses many more neurons for the center of the visual field than for the periphery. The fovea is a small spot on the retina, but it occupies a large part of V1. The reason is the high density of cones in the fovea and their low convergence onto ganglion cells: the cortical area devoted to a region of the visual field grows with the density of its ganglion cells (Curcio & Allen, 1990). The magnified central input lets us see fine detail, for example when we read or recognize faces.

The Visual System and Deep Networks¶

The human visual system and deep networks for image classification share a basic design. Both detect local features, process them in a hierarchy of layers, and learn from examples. The resemblance is not a coincidence. The simple and complex cells of Hubel and Wiesel inspired the Neocognitron (Fukushima, 1980), a network of alternating layers of “S-cells” that detect local patterns and “C-cells” that tolerate their shift. Convolutional networks keep this pattern: convolution layers play the role of the simple cells, and pooling layers the role of the complex cells. shows that the first-layer filters of a trained network resemble the oriented edge detectors of V1.

The differences are just as important. The human system is biological. It is limited in processing speed and capacity, but it adapts well to varied visual input and learns new categories from few examples. It also relies heavily on feedback, as the connections from the cortex back to the LGN show, while a standard convolutional network passes information only forward. Deep networks are artificial. They process very large amounts of data quickly and become highly efficient at the specific tasks they were trained for, but they can fail on changes of the input that humans hardly notice.
