Transformer Embeddings for Image Search

A label names one concept from a fixed list. ImageNet has 1,000 such classes, and even a list a thousand times longer would miss much of what users ask for. Labels such as “mountain”, “car”, or “flower” say which objects an image contains, but not how the scene is composed, and the composition is often what makes a photo relevant. A user who searches for “a red sports car in the Alps” wants the car, its color, and the setting together; a photo labeled “car” and “mountain” may show a gray van in front of a hill. Labels also do not support “more like this” by meaning: two photos with the label “alp” count as equally similar, whether they show the same peak or two peaks on different continents.

The web offers a way past the fixed list. Billions of images come with captions that people wrote, and these captions name millions of concepts and describe whole scenes. Models trained on such data, or on large collections of images alone, open three paths for image search. The first path generates a text description of every image, a caption and keywords, and searches the descriptions with the methods of text retrieval (Multimodal Transformers). The second path maps images and texts into one embedding space, so that a text query is compared with the images directly. The third path compares images with images, in an embedding space where distances reflect what the images show and how they look.

This section follows the second and the third path with two transformer encoders. CLIP learns from photos and their captions and places images and texts in one embedding space. DINOv2 learns from photos alone and captures visual similarity in detail. Neither is trained with class labels, and both turn every image into one vector that an image search system can index. At the end, one query runs through every generation of features in this chapter.

Images as Sequences of Patches¶

The Vision Transformer (ViT) treats an image like a sentence (Figure 1). It cuts the image into square patches, for example 16 by 16 pixels, flattens each patch into a vector, and projects it linearly to an embedding, the image counterpart of a token embedding. A position embedding records where the patch came from, and an extra learnable [class] token is placed in front of the sequence. The transformer encoder of Transformer Embeddings processes the sequence: in every layer, each patch attends to all other patches. Unlike a convolution, whose view grows only layer by layer, attention connects distant parts of the image from the first layer on. The output vector of the [class] token summarizes the whole image and serves as its embedding.

The attention maps on the right of Figure 1 show which patches the [class] token attends to: the dog, the airplane, the snake, not the background. The model has learned to focus on the object without ever being told where it is. The two models of this section differ in their patch size and training. CLIP ViT-B/32 cuts a 224 by 224 image into 7 by 7 patches of 32 pixels and produces a 512-dimensional embedding. DINOv2 ViT-S/14 uses 16 by 16 patches of 14 pixels and produces a 384-dimensional embedding.

CLIP: Images and Captions in One Space¶

Both models of this section are trained with objectives that compare embeddings, the metric learning of Learned Features: CNNs: CLIP compares images with captions, and DINO compares different views of the same image.

CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training), published by OpenAI in 2021, trains two encoders side by side. An image encoder, here a Vision Transformer, maps an image to a vector, and a text encoder, a transformer like the text encoders of semantic search, maps a caption to a vector of the same dimension, 512 for the model used in this chapter. The training data are 400 million image-caption pairs collected from the web. Nobody labels classes; the captions that people wrote for their images provide the supervision.

Each training step takes a batch of NNN image-caption pairs, encodes all images and all captions, and computes the cosine similarity of every image with every caption. The result is an N×NN \times NN×N matrix whose diagonal holds the NNN matching pairs, while all other entries belong to mismatched pairs. Training treats each row as a classification task, “which of the NNN captions belongs to this image?”, and each column as the same task in the other direction. CLIP was trained with batches of 32,768 pairs, so each image has to find its caption among 32,768 candidates.

Caption 1 fits the Zermatt photo almost as well as its own caption: both describe the same faces of the Matterhorn. The loss does not ask CLIP to push such a caption far away. Each row is a softmax, so the loss only asks that the own caption scores highest. Once the own caption leads by a few hundredths of cosine similarity, the temperature turns this lead into a probability close to 1, the push on the competing caption practically stops, and both captions stay close to both photos. In batches of 32,768 random pairs from the web, two captions that fit the same image this well are rare.

The example also shows how low CLIP’s similarities between images and texts are. Even matching pairs reach only about 0.33, because image embeddings and text embeddings occupy different regions of the shared space. Similarities between an image and a text typically lie between 0.1 and 0.4, while two images are much more similar to each other: even the unrelated Taj Mahal photo reaches 0.655 against the Matterhorn query. What carries the information is the difference between candidates, which the temperature amplifies. A difference of 0.014 in cosine similarity, 0.3274 against 0.3136, becomes a factor of about 4 in probability.

After training, the shared space supports search in every direction: a text finds images, an image finds similar images, and an image finds matching texts. It also turns CLIP into a classifier for any set of classes without training: the system compares an image with short text prompts such as “a photo of a mountain” and “a photo of a building” and picks the closest one. This zero-shot classification assigns the Matterhorn query to “a photo of a mountain” with 99.85% against four other prompts (Multimodal Transformers works through this example). CLIP’s embeddings capture what people write about images: objects, scenes, landmarks, styles, even text in the image. They are weaker on visual details that captions rarely mention, such as the exact shape of a ridge or the arrangement of objects.

DINO: Similarity from Images Alone¶

DINO learns from images alone. Its name stands for self-distillation with no labels. Two copies of the same network play different roles. Each training image is cut into two large crops, each covering most of the image, and several small crops. The teacher sees only the large crops; the student sees all of them. The training always pairs two different crops: the teacher’s output for one large crop with the student’s output for another crop, and the student must produce the same output as the teacher. When the student’s crop is a small one, it sees much less of the image than the teacher. To match the teacher from a small crop of the summit, the student must learn what the whole image contains. The teacher is not trained directly; its weights follow a slowly moving average of the student’s weights.

A trivial solution would let both networks output the same distribution for every image. DINO prevents this collapse by centering and sharpening the teacher’s outputs, so that no single output dominates for all images. DINOv2, published in 2023, scaled the method to 142 million curated images and added a second objective that predicts masked patches. The resulting embeddings capture visual similarity in detail: the same object from another angle, the same building in another photo, parts and shapes. DINOv2 has no text encoder, so it cannot answer text queries.

Image Search in Practice¶

Both models fit the same search architecture. At indexing time, the system runs every image once through the encoder, normalizes the embedding to unit length, and stores it in a vector index from 6 - Vector Search. At query time, it encodes the query and returns the nearest neighbors. The query can be an image, encoded with the same image encoder, or, for CLIP, a text, encoded with CLIP’s text encoder into the same space. Metadata filters from Filtering, Facets, and Compression restrict the search, for example to photos taken in winter, and a text search can run alongside: a multimodal language model writes a caption and keywords for each image at indexing time, a keyword index searches them, and the two rankings are fused. Multimodal Transformers compares these two approaches.

Three query types cover most image search applications:

  • Image-to-image search (“more like this”) compares image embeddings. DINOv2 tends to find visually similar images and other views of the same object; CLIP tends to find images of the same concept, even when they look different.

  • Text-to-image search compares a CLIP text embedding with the CLIP image embeddings. It finds images that nobody described, for any concept that captions on the web have covered.

  • Near-duplicate detection finds copies of an image that were resized, recompressed, cropped, or edited. It is the basis of copyright monitoring, of removing duplicates from photo libraries and training data, and of tracing where an image first appeared.

Similarity values do not transfer between models

Each model has its own range of similarity values. Unrelated photos score about 0.5 under CLIP and ResNet-18 but close to 0 under DINOv2, and a text query typically scores only 0.1 to 0.4 against images under CLIP, even for good matches, because image and text embeddings occupy different regions of the shared space. A threshold chosen for one model and one query type is meaningless for another; it must be chosen on evaluation data, as in Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves. Embeddings of different models are not comparable at all, so switching to a new model means encoding the whole collection again.

Then vs. Now¶

To compare all generations of features on the same task, we send the Matterhorn query to the 45 photos of the collection in Figure 10: 8 photos of the Matterhorn from other positions, seasons, and light, 12 photos of other snow-covered peaks, 9 scenes with the colors of the query but no mountain, and 16 photos of other subjects.

Each method ranks the 45 photos by its own similarity. The color histogram uses the quadratic distance of Color Features, SIFT the number of matches that pass the ratio test and the geometric check of Edges, Texture, and Shape, and ResNet-18, DINOv2, and CLIP the cosine similarity of their embeddings. Figure 4 shows the top 5 of each method, and the last row answers a text query with CLIP.

We measure the precision at 5, P@5P@5P@5 (Evaluating Ranked Results), at two levels of relevance: a result is relevant if it shows the Matterhorn, or, more generously, if it shows any mountain. The collection contains 8 Matterhorn photos and 20 mountain photos in total.

Each method fails in its own characteristic way:

  • The color histogram does better than expected: four of its five results show mountains. In this collection, snow and sky are typical of mountain photos, so the palette correlates with the category. But the ranking follows the palette, not the mountain. Its first result is Mont Blanc under a gray sky, its fifth a pond that reflects white clouds, and the Matterhorn photo from nearly the same spot in Zermatt lands at rank 26, because its sky is a deeper blue. Histogram intersection gives the same precision.

  • SIFT is all or nothing. The photo from nearly the same spot comes first, with 100 consistent matches. All other photos have at most 10, so the rest of its ranking is noise: a city at night, tulips, a sand dune, and, by chance, Mount Rainier.

  • ResNet-18 returns five mountains, three of them the Matterhorn. Its first result is Aoraki / Mount Cook, and the near-identical photo only comes sixth. The embedding knows “alp”, but not which one.

  • DINOv2 puts the near-identical photo first, followed by two more views of the Matterhorn and the most similar-looking other peaks: the rock face of the Eiger and the pyramid of the Weisshorn. It compares how the images look, in detail.

  • CLIP returns four photos of the Matterhorn, more than any other method, including one in winter and one in autumn. A plausible explanation is its training: captions on the web name landmarks, and the Matterhorn is among the most photographed mountains, so CLIP learned to place its photos close together.

The text query “a snow-covered mountain at sunset” shows what none of the other methods can do. All four photos of the collection that show a mountain at sunset or in alpenglow appear in CLIP’s top 5, so P@5=0.8P@5 = 0.8P@5=0.8 is the maximum possible. The remaining result, at rank 3, shows the south side of the Matterhorn in warm low-angle light, which many users would accept as well. The similarity values lie between 0.276 and 0.314, as low as expected for pairs of a text and an image. Other text queries work as well: “a sailboat on a lake” returns the sailboat photo first, and “a city at night” returns both night skylines at ranks 1 and 2. Finally, the linear probe of Learned Features: CNNs, which decides “mountain or not” from three labeled examples per class, reaches 99.1% with DINOv2 and 95.2% with CLIP embeddings, against 98.1% with ResNet-18 and 68.9% with color histograms.

The newer features also cost more. On the laptop CPU used for this chapter, a 125-bin Lab histogram computed from a 320-pixel thumbnail takes about 3 milliseconds per image. One forward pass through ResNet-18 takes about 50 milliseconds, through CLIP or DINOv2 about 100 milliseconds, and SIFT on the full 1280-pixel image about 150 milliseconds.

The comparison shows how far image features have come, but it does not make the classical features obsolete. They still win in four situations:

  • Exact color constraints. A shop that sells a sweater in a specific red needs “this exact red”, not “a sweater”. A color histogram with histogram intersection answers this directly; an embedding mixes the color with everything else.

  • The same object, with evidence. SIFT matches with a geometric check prove that two photos show the same object, even when only a small part of one photo appears in the other. The matched keypoints can be shown to a user or a court, which matters for copyright and forensic applications.

  • Explainability. A histogram bin, an edge density, or a set of matched keypoints can be inspected and explained. A cosine similarity of 0.82 between two 384-dimensional vectors cannot.

  • Cost. A color histogram needs no model and is 15 to 35 times cheaper than an embedding on the same CPU. A transformer encoder needs a GPU for large collections, and every model update means encoding the whole collection again.

Current systems combine both worlds. They use transformer embeddings for the semantic search and keep classical features for color filters, duplicate detection, and verification.
