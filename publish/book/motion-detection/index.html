Motion Detection

A camera mounted above a parking lot records day and night. It never moves and never cuts, so a whole day of footage is a single shot of 24 hours, and the shot detection of Shot Boundary Detection finds nothing to split. The guard who reviews the night does not want shots. The guard wants the moments when something moved: a person crossing the lot at 2:14, a car leaving at 4:30. Here motion is the retrieval unit.

Motion carries meaning in films as well. Within a shot, a chase differs from a dialogue mainly in how much and how fast things move, and a query for “fast action scenes” asks about motion, not about colors. Motion estimation is also everywhere in video technology. Video encoders predict each block of a frame from a shifted block of the previous frame and store only the shift and the remaining difference. Robots and driver-assistance systems estimate their own movement and the 3D structure of the scene from the way the image moves.

This section starts with the static camera, where differences between images reveal the moving objects, and turns these differences into features for retrieval. It then turns to the moving camera, where we must estimate how every pixel moves.

Background Subtraction¶

With a static camera, everything that does not move belongs to the background. If we have an image BBB of the empty parking lot, every pixel of the current frame III that differs clearly from BBB belongs to a moving object:

∣B(x,y)−I(x,y,t)∣>τ\left| B(x, y) - I(x, y, t) \right| > \tau∣B(x,y)−I(x,y,t)∣>τ
(1)

Here I(x,y,t)I(x, y, t)I(x,y,t) is the brightness of the pixel at position (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) in frame ttt, and τ\tauτ is a threshold that separates real changes from sensor noise. The result is a binary foreground mask: white for pixels that belong to a moving object, black for the background. Figure 1 shows the result for a pedestrian on the parking lot.

A fixed background image does not last long. The sun moves, clouds pass, and the light changes from day to night, so after a few hours every pixel differs from the morning’s background. Practical systems therefore update the background model continuously, as the loop in Figure 2 shows. A simple update blends the current frame into the background with a small weight α\alphaα, for example B←(1−α)B+αIB \leftarrow (1 - \alpha) B + \alpha IB←(1−α)B+αI with α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, and applies it only to pixels that are not part of a moving object. The background then follows slow changes of the light, while a person crossing the lot stays in the foreground.

Several problems remain. When a parked car leaves, the background model still contains it, and the empty parking space appears as a “ghost” object until the update has absorbed it. Leaves moving in the wind and reflections on wet asphalt trigger the detector, although nobody wants to find them. A background model with several typical values per pixel, such as the mixture of Gaussians that Stauffer and Grimson proposed in 1999, learns that a pixel in a tree alternates between leaf and sky, and accepts both as background. Above all, the method requires a camera that does not move. A single pan or zoom shifts every pixel against the background model.

Raw foreground masks contain isolated noise pixels, and the object itself may break into several pieces. Morphological operations remove isolated pixels and close small gaps: an opening removes specks smaller than its window, and a closing fills gaps narrower than its window. Grouping the connected white pixels into blobs then turns the mask into a list of objects, each with a position and a size. A blob that appears at the edge of the lot and moves across it over several seconds is what the guard is looking for.

Figure 3 applies these steps to the breakup on the bridge in “Tears of Steel”, the open film of this chapter. From 0:25 to 0:40, the camera stays on Thom and Celia, the closest the film comes to a static camera. There is no frame of the empty bridge, so the first frame of the shot serves as the background, with τ=30\tau = 30τ=30 gray values. At 0:27.5, the raw mask consists of 417 separate regions. An opening with a window of 3 by 3 pixels and a closing with 9 by 9 pixels leave 23, and two of them have at least 1,500 pixels. The first is Thom, who has moved to the right since the first frame: his blob covers both his current position and the ghost of his old one. The second is only Celia’s head, because the rest of her body has hardly moved and still matches the background.

Frame Differencing¶

Instead of maintaining a background model, we can compare each frame with the previous one:

∣I(x,y,t−1)−I(x,y,t)∣>τ\left| I(x, y, t-1) - I(x, y, t) \right| > \tau∣I(x,y,t−1)−I(x,y,t)∣>τ
(2)

The previous frame serves as the background (Figure 4). Because the reference is only one frame old, the method adapts immediately to changes of the light and recovers quickly after the camera has moved. While the camera moves, however, every edge in the image changes and appears as motion. Frame differencing also sees only part of an object. Where a uniformly colored object moves, its interior pixels keep their value from one frame to the next, and edges that run parallel to the motion do not change either. The mask shows mainly the leading and trailing edges of the object. When the object stops, it disappears from the mask, and when it starts moving again, the area it leaves behind shows up as a second object.

The gap between the two compared frames matters. With a larger gap, the object has moved further, and more of its outline differs, but the mask then contains the object twice: at its old and at its new position. A refinement that removes the second copy, and a way to keep shadows out of the mask, are described in the following dropdown.

Three-frame differencing and shadow removal (optional reading)

Three-frame differencing removes the second copy that a large gap creates. It compares the current frame with a frame from the past and with a frame from the future, for example 15 frames before and 15 frames after. Each difference mask shows the object twice: once at its position at time ttt and once at its position in the other frame. Only the position at time ttt appears in both masks, so a logical AND of the two masks keeps the current position and removes both copies (Figure 5).

The price is a delay. To locate the object in frame ttt, the method must wait for frame t+15t + 15t+15, which at 25 frames per second is 0.6 seconds later. The best gap depends on the size and speed of the objects: it must be large enough for the object to move clear of its old position, and small enough that the object does not leave the scene. A surveillance system designed for walking pedestrians needs a different gap than one designed for cars on a motorway.

All differencing methods detect shadows as well as objects. A pedestrian on a sunny day drags a dark shadow across the asphalt, and the mask contains both. The shadow distorts the shape and position of the object, it can connect two separate people into one blob, and it can trigger a detection on its own when it falls into a monitored region.

A shadow changes the brightness of the surface it falls on, but not much its color. The chromaticity, the hue and saturation of a color without its brightness, therefore stays nearly the same. We can map each pixel into a chromaticity space, such as the aaa and bbb channels of Lab or the hue and saturation of HSV, and difference only there: ∣Bchroma(x,y)−Ichroma(x,y,t)∣>τ\left| B_{\text{chroma}}(x, y) - I_{\text{chroma}}(x, y, t) \right| > \tau∣Bchroma​(x,y)−Ichroma​(x,y,t)∣>τ. A shadow on gray asphalt keeps the chromaticity of the asphalt and disappears from the mask, while a person in a red jacket differs in chromaticity and remains. The method fails for objects whose color matches the background, which is why practical systems combine the brightness and the chromaticity tests. Color spaces that separate chromaticity from brightness are treated in Color Features.

Motion History Images¶

A foreground mask shows where something moves in one frame. To describe how something moved over a period of time, we accumulate the masks. Let D(x,y,t)D(x, y, t)D(x,y,t) be 1 if the pixel is in the foreground of frame ttt and 0 otherwise. The motion history image MMM keeps the most recent motion at full brightness and lets older motion fade by a decay γ\gammaγ per frame.

Key Formula: Motion History Image
M(x,y,t)=max⁡(D(x,y,t), max⁡(0, M(x,y,t−1)−γ))M(x, y, t) = \max\Big( D(x, y, t),\ \max\big(0,\ M(x, y, t-1) - \gamma\big) \Big)M(x,y,t)=max(D(x,y,t), max(0, M(x,y,t−1)−γ))
(3)

A pixel that moves now has the value 1. A pixel that moved earlier loses γ\gammaγ per frame and reaches 0 after 1/γ1 / \gamma1/γ frames, so the brightness of a pixel tells how recently it moved.

A pedestrian crosses a pixel in frames 1 and 2. With γ=0.25\gamma = 0.25γ=0.25, the pixel evolves as follows:

Figure 6 shows the pipeline: frame differencing produces the current mask, and the max operation combines it with the decayed history. The result is a single image that summarizes the motion of the last 1/γ1 / \gamma1/γ frames. A larger γ\gammaγ gives a shorter history. The brightness gradient along the trail shows the direction of motion, from dark (old) to bright (recent). Bobick and Davis used such images, together with the binary image of all pixels that moved at all, as templates to recognize human movements such as sitting down, waving, or crouching.

For retrieval, the motion history image becomes a feature vector in the same way as any other image. The fraction of non-zero pixels measures how much motion a shot or a time window contains. A histogram of the values describes whether the motion is recent or spread over the whole period, and the moments of the bright pixels give the center and the extent of the moving region. Histograms of the gradient directions in the motion history image describe the dominant direction of movement. Such features answer queries like “shots with a lot of action”, “someone walking from left to right”, or, for the parking lot, “motion near the entrance between midnight and 6 a.m.”. The MPEG-7 standard defines related descriptors for the intensity of motion activity and for camera motion.

Optical Flow¶

All differencing methods assume a static camera. In a film, the camera pans, tilts, zooms, and follows the actors, and every pixel changes from frame to frame. To describe motion in such footage, we need to know where each pixel moves: a motion vector (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) for each position, the optical flow. The flow of the whole frame reveals the motion of the camera, and the parts that move differently from the camera are the moving objects.

The starting point is the assumption of brightness constancy: a point of the scene keeps its brightness while it moves, at least over the short time Δt\Delta tΔt between two frames, for example 0.04 seconds at 25 frames per second. If the point moves from (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) to (x+u,y+v)(x + u, y + v)(x+u,y+v), then

I(x+u,y+v,t+Δt)=I(x,y,t)I(x + u, y + v, t + \Delta t) = I(x, y, t)I(x+u,y+v,t+Δt)=I(x,y,t)
(4)

For small motions, a first-order Taylor expansion linearizes the left side:

I(x+u,y+v,t+Δt)≈I(x,y,t)+∂I∂xu+∂I∂yv+∂I∂tΔtI(x + u, y + v, t + \Delta t) \approx I(x, y, t) + \frac{∂ I}{∂ x} u + \frac{∂ I}{∂ y} v + \frac{∂ I}{∂ t} \Delta tI(x+u,y+v,t+Δt)≈I(x,y,t)+∂x∂I​u+∂y∂I​v+∂t∂I​Δt

We write IxI_xIx​ and IyI_yIy​ for the spatial gradients, the rates at which the brightness changes in the horizontal and in the vertical direction, and ItI_tIt​ for the change of brightness at the fixed position (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) between the two frames, the term ∂I∂tΔt\frac{∂ I}{∂ t} \Delta t∂t∂I​Δt. The Sobel operator estimates IxI_xIx​ and IyI_yIy​ with two kernels of 3 by 3 pixels that weigh the brightness differences between the neighbors on the two sides of a pixel, as Edges, Texture, and Shape shows in detail.

If the image gets brighter towards the right (Ix>0I_x > 0Ix​>0) and the pixel gets darker over time (It<0I_t < 0It​<0), the pattern is moving to the right. The equation links the measurable gradients to the unknown motion (u,v)(u, v)(u,v).

One equation does not determine two unknowns. If (u,v)(u, v)(u,v) satisfies the equation, so does every vector that adds a component perpendicular to the gradient (Ix,Iy)(I_x, I_y)(Ix​,Iy​), that is, along the edge. A pixel can measure only the motion across an edge, not along it. This is the aperture problem (Figure 7). Through a small window that shows only one straight edge of a rectangle moving right and down, the observer sees the edge move to the right and nothing else. The barber pole illusion is a related effect in human vision. The stripes of a rotating pole move sideways, but they seem to move along the pole: the motion of each stripe is ambiguous, and the eye resolves it with the ends of the stripes, which slide along the edges of the pole.

The Lucas-Kanade Method¶

Lucas and Kanade proposed in 1981 to assume that all pixels in a small window move together. A window of 5 by 5 pixels around (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) then yields 25 equations for the same two unknowns, one per pixel:

[Ix(x−2,y−2)Iy(x−2,y−2)⋮⋮Ix(x+2,y+2)Iy(x+2,y+2)]⏟A[uv]⏟d=[−It(x−2,y−2)⋮−It(x+2,y+2)]⏟b\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} I_x(x-2, y-2) & I_y(x-2, y-2) \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ I_x(x+2, y+2) & I_y(x+2, y+2) \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{d}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -I_t(x-2, y-2) \\ \vdots \\ -I_t(x+2, y+2) \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}A⎣⎡​Ix​(x−2,y−2)⋮Ix​(x+2,y+2)​Iy​(x−2,y−2)⋮Iy​(x+2,y+2)​⎦⎤​​​d[uv​]​​=b⎣⎡​−It​(x−2,y−2)⋮−It​(x+2,y+2)​⎦⎤​​​

The sums run over all pixels of the window. The window supplies gradients in several directions, and their combination determines the full motion vector.

The system has a reliable solution only if the 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix A⊤A\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A}A⊤A is invertible and well conditioned: both eigenvalues must be large, and their ratio must not be too large. In a flat region, all gradients are small, and both eigenvalues are close to zero. On a straight edge, all gradients point in the same direction, and one eigenvalue is zero: the aperture problem in matrix form. Only at corners and in textured regions do gradients in different directions make both eigenvalues large. Shi and Tomasi turned this condition into a rule for choosing the points to track (“good features to track”). SIFT applies a closely related test when it discards keypoint candidates on straight edges and keeps only points with strong curvature in two directions, such as corners and blobs; see Edges, Texture, and Shape.

The Taylor expansion holds only for small motions, typically about one pixel per frame. Larger motions need an image pyramid (Figure 8). Both frames are repeatedly blurred and halved in size. With four levels, the coarsest image is 8 times smaller than the original, and a motion of 8 pixels shrinks to 1 pixel. The method estimates the flow at the coarsest level, doubles it, uses it as the starting point at the next finer level, and estimates only the remaining correction there, down to the full resolution.

Tracking corner points over many frames yields trajectories of persons and vehicles. Figure 9 tracks the corner points of a shot of “Tears of Steel” at 4:30.8 over eight frames, a third of a second, while the camera pans with Thom as he walks. Of 500 points, 380 pass a forward-backward check: each point is tracked back to the previous frame and kept only if it returns to within one pixel. The 291 points on the background move by about 10.6 pixels per frame to the left with the pan, while the 89 points on Thom move by about 4.8 pixels per frame, mostly up and down with his steps. The flow of the whole frame reveals the motion of the camera, and the points that do not follow it belong to the moving object.

In footage from a moving car, the flow vectors fan out from the point the car drives toward, and vectors that do not fit this pattern belong to objects with their own motion. A surveillance system can combine both worlds: differencing finds the moving vehicles, and the flow inside each blob measures their direction and speed. For retrieval, the flow yields features such as the dominant camera motion of a shot (pan, tilt, zoom, or static), the average speed of the moving objects, and a histogram of motion directions.

Optical flow measures apparent motion

Optical flow measures how brightness patterns move in the image, not how objects move in the scene. A uniformly colored ball that rotates in place shows no flow, because no pattern moves. A light that is switched on changes the brightness everywhere and produces flow although nothing moves, and a moving shadow produces flow on a static floor. Brightness constancy fails in exactly these cases, and every method built on it inherits the failure.

Then vs. Now: Learned Optical Flow¶

Lucas-Kanade is a local method: it estimates the flow where a window contains enough structure, and it has nothing to say about flat regions. Learned methods estimate a dense flow field, one vector for every pixel. RAFT, published by Teed and Deng in 2020, computes a feature vector for every pixel of both frames, compares every pixel of the first frame with every pixel of the second, and refines the flow field over several iterations with a recurrent network that looks up these comparisons. Because the comparisons cover the whole image, large motions need no image pyramid, and flat regions inherit the flow of their surroundings.

Training such networks needs the true flow of every pixel, which no camera can measure. A common source is computer-generated film. The MPI-Sintel benchmark, introduced by Butler and colleagues in 2012, is built from “Sintel”, an open movie of the Blender Foundation, the studio that also made “Tears of Steel”. The rendering software knows where every pixel moves, so the benchmark provides the exact flow for scenes with motion blur, fog, and fast action. On its hardest version, RAFT reduced the average error of the best previous method from 4.10 to 2.86 pixels.

Figure 10 compares both methods on the first two frames of the same pan shot at 4:30.8. Thom moves little in the image, while the background moves by about 8 pixels per frame to the left. This is too far for the Taylor expansion, and the Lucas-Kanade implementation of OpenCV therefore works on a pyramid. It selects 500 corner points with the eigenvalue criterion of Shi and Tomasi and tracks 493 of them in 18 milliseconds; its windows of 21 by 21 pixels around these points cover 24% of the image. RAFT computes a vector for each of the 683,520 pixels in 6.8 seconds on the same processor, almost 400 times longer. Where both methods have an estimate, they agree closely: at the tracked points, the median difference between the two vectors is 0.26 pixels, and 84% of the points differ by less than one pixel. The difference lies in the other 76% of the image, such as the pavement, the dark door, and Thom’s jacket, where Lucas-Kanade has no corner points and RAFT fills in the flow.

Lucas-Kanade remains the method of choice when a few points must be tracked in real time on modest hardware, for example in a surveillance camera or a smartphone. The learned methods are preferred when the flow of every pixel is needed, and their flow fields also serve as input for the video models of Video Understanding with Transformers.
