Perceptual Features

The archivist of the radio station wants to mark where the talk ends and the music begins. Two recordings show the task: a man reading a novel aloud and a string orchestra playing a dance by Brahms. A listener needs a fraction of a second to tell them apart. A spectrogram, the energy of each frequency in each short frame, contains this information, but it is a large matrix: 40 ms frames with a hop of 20 ms turn the 14.8-second reading into 741 spectra of 442 bins each. A classifier and an index need a few numbers per frame and per segment that describe how the sound is heard.

Listeners describe sounds by a handful of perceptual attributes: loudness (soft or loud), pitch (low or high), duration, and timbre, the color of a sound that distinguishes a violin from a voice at the same pitch and loudness. Two more attributes describe mixtures: the texture of many overlapping sources, such as a busy market, and their location in space. Perceptual features measure properties of the signal that correspond to these attributes. They come in three groups: features computed directly from the samples of a frame (time domain), features computed from its spectrum (frequency domain), and features that model the frequency resolution and loudness perception of the ear. Pitch has its own section, Musical Features.

Segment Statistics¶

A single frame of 40 ms says little about whether someone is speaking. The pattern over several seconds does: speech alternates between syllables and short pauses, about four to five syllables per second, while music tends to keep a more continuous level. Segment features capture such patterns. A segment SjS_jSj​ of 4 seconds contains M=(4000−40)/20+1=199M = (4000 - 40)/20 + 1 = 199M=(4000−40)/20+1=199 frames, and the simplest segment features are the moments of each frame feature over these frames: mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis. Histograms of the frame values are possible too, but they need more numbers per feature than moments and are less common.

Two ratios were designed specifically to separate speech from music. The low short-time energy ratio (LSTER) is the share of frames in a segment whose energy lies below half the mean energy of the segment. The high zero-crossing rate ratio (HZCRR) is the share of frames whose ZCR lies above 1.5 times the mean ZCR of the segment:

Here Eˉj\bar{E}_jEˉj​ and Zˉj\bar{Z}_jZˉj​ are the means of E(t)E(t)E(t) and Z(t)Z(t)Z(t) over the MMM frames of segment SjS_jSj​, and the numerators count the frames that meet the condition. The thresholds of one half and 1.5 follow the original definition by Lu, Zhang, and Jiang (2002), who computed both ratios over windows of 1 second. The speech/music classifier of Castán et al. (2010) uses them on segments of 4 seconds. LSTER uses the linear energy E(t)E(t)E(t): a threshold of “half the mean” makes no sense on decibel values, which can be negative.

Speech has many low-energy frames in its pauses and many high-ZCR frames in its hissing consonants, so both ratios tend to be higher for speech than for music.

Example: speech versus Brahms

The two running clips are the 14.8-second LibriVox reading of The Ashiel Mystery and the 45.8-second Brahms recording by a string orchestra. Figure 1 shows the data behind the two ratios: the zero-crossing rate of every 40 ms frame (left), and LSTER and HZCRR of every 4-second segment with a hop of 1 second, 11 segments for the reading and 42 for Brahms (middle and right).

LibriVox readingBrahms
Frame ZCR: mean (median)0.058 (0.036)0.121 (0.095)

The frame ZCR points the other way: Brahms crosses zero twice as often on average, because the strings produce many high harmonics. Most frames of the reading, its vowels and voiced consonants, cross zero rarely, and a long tail of hissing consonants reaches beyond 0.3. Speech does not cross zero more often on average; it varies more from frame to frame, and the ratios capture exactly this variation.

The 11 segments of the reading lie in narrow bands, because the rhythm of syllables and pauses stays the same throughout the reading. The Brahms segments spread widely. Ten of them reach at least the lowest LSTER of the reading (0.42), and all ten lie in the last 15 seconds, where the music changes its dynamics abruptly: a rest of the orchestra around 34 seconds, strong accents, and the fade-out from 42 seconds on. Like the pauses of speech, these contrasts put many frames below half the mean energy. Only three Brahms segments reach the lowest HZCRR of the reading (0.17), and only two, starting at 37 and 41 seconds, look like speech on both ratios. The highest HZCRR, 0.31, belongs to the last segment: in the fade-out, little more than noise remains, and its frames form the small bump near 0.55 in the left panel.

A decision tree on exactly these two ratios is the speech/music classifier of Classifying Audio.

The spectral roll-off SR is the frequency below which 85% of the magnitude lies; it rises when a frame contains much high-frequency energy, as in the hissing consonants of speech. The spectral bandwidth SB is the spread of the spectrum around its centroid: a pure tone has a small bandwidth, noise a large one. The spectral flux SF compares the normalized spectrum with that of the previous frame and is high when the sound changes abruptly, for example at the start of a note or a syllable. The band energy BE measures the energy in one frequency band selected by a weighting function v(k)v(k)v(k), for example 0 to 600 Hz for the fundamentals of voices; several bands give several features. All of these frame features are summarized per segment with moments, just like the time-domain features.

Loudness¶

Energy measures what reaches the microphone, not what the listener hears. Figure 2 (left) shows the equal-loudness contours: each curve connects the sound pressure levels at which pure tones of different frequencies sound equally loud to an average listener. The curves dip between 2 and 5 kHz, where the ear canal resonates and the ear is most sensitive, the range that also matters most for understanding speech. Near the threshold of hearing, a 50 Hz tone needs about 40 dB more than a 1000 Hz tone to sound equally loud, and at the level of a conversation still about 30 dB more.

Frequency weightings approximate these contours with a correction A(f)A(f)A(f) in decibels that is added to the energy of each frequency before summing (Figure 2, right). The A-weighting of the international standard IEC 61672 is by far the most common, although it follows the contour of quiet sounds. It subtracts 30.3 dB at 50 Hz, 19.1 dB at 100 Hz, and 3.2 dB at 500 Hz, leaves 1000 Hz unchanged, adds 1.3 dB at 2500 Hz and 1.0 dB at 4000 Hz, and subtracts 2.5 dB at 10 kHz. Noise limits and the “dB(A)” values of sound-level meters use this weighting.

Loudness also builds up over time: a click of a few milliseconds sounds quieter than a tone of the same level that lasts half a second. A loudness feature therefore averages the weighted energy over the last second rather than over a single frame. With a hop of 20 ms, the last second spans Q=50Q = 50Q=50 frames:

Broadcasting uses the same idea to keep programs at a consistent volume: loudness meters following the EBU R 128 recommendation report a momentary loudness over 400 ms and a short-term loudness over 3 seconds.

The Mel Scale¶

Pitch perception is not linear in frequency either. Listeners judge the step from 100 Hz to 200 Hz as large and the step from 10,000 Hz to 10,100 Hz as tiny, as the cochlear frequency map of From Sound to Spectrogram predicts. The mel scale measures pitch so that equal distances in mel sound like equal steps in pitch. It is roughly linear below 1000 Hz and roughly logarithmic above.

Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients¶

The mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC) combine the mel filter bank with the logarithmic loudness perception of the ear into a short vector per frame. Introduced for speech recognition by Davis and Mermelstein in 1980, they became the standard frame feature for speech and music analysis for three decades. The computation has five steps.

1. Spectrum. Multiply the frame with a Hamming window and compute its Fourier transform X(t,k)X(t,k)X(t,k), as in the STFT. Some descriptions omit the window; the standard implementations apply it, for the reason of spectral leakage given in From Sound to Spectrogram.

2. Mel spectrum. Sum the energy of the bins in each band, weighted with the triangles:

M(t,b)=∑k=0Kd(b,k)⋅∣X(t,k)∣2,1≤b≤BM(t,b) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} d(b,k) \cdot |X(t,k)|^2, \qquad 1 \le b \le BM(t,b)=k=0∑K​d(b,k)⋅∣X(t,k)∣2,1≤b≤B
(10)

The 442 bins of a 40 ms frame shrink to B=26B = 26B=26 band energies, with a fine resolution at low and a coarse resolution at high frequencies, as in the ear.

3. Logarithm. Replace each band energy by log⁡M(t,b)\log M(t,b)logM(t,b). This follows the logarithmic perception of loudness, and it has a second effect. A voice is a source, the vibrating vocal cords, filtered by the vocal tract, whose shape changes with each vowel and consonant. In the spectrum, the filter multiplies the source; after the logarithm, it adds to it. A change in recording level or microphone becomes a constant offset as well.

4. Cepstral coefficients. Apply a discrete cosine transform (DCT) to the 26 log energies and keep the first 13 coefficients:

Key Formula: Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients
c(t,j)=∑b=1Blog⁡M(t,b)⋅cos⁡(j (2b−1) π2B),0≤j≤12c(t,j) = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \log M(t,b) \cdot \cos\left(\frac{j\,(2b-1)\,\pi}{2B}\right), \qquad 0 \le j \le 12c(t,j)=b=1∑B​logM(t,b)⋅cos(2Bj(2b−1)π​),0≤j≤12
(11)

The cosine transform describes the shape of the log mel spectrum with a few numbers: c(t,0)c(t,0)c(t,0) is the overall level, c(t,1)c(t,1)c(t,1) the tilt from low to high frequencies, and the higher coefficients increasingly fine ripples. Keeping 13 coefficients keeps the smooth envelope, which reflects the vocal tract and the timbre, and drops the fine structure, which reflects the pitch.

The result is called a cepstrum, a play on the word “spectrum”, because it is in a sense the spectrum of a spectrum. The DCT has a further benefit: neighboring mel bands are strongly correlated, and the cosine coefficients are nearly uncorrelated, which suited the classifiers of the time with their diagonal covariance matrices.

5. Derivatives. The coefficients describe a frame in isolation. Their changes over time carry information about transitions between sounds, so the feature vector is extended with the first and second differences over neighboring frames:

Δc(t,j)=c(t+1,j)−c(t−1,j)2,Δ2c(t,j)=Δc(t+1,j)−Δc(t−1,j)2\Delta c(t,j) = \frac{c(t+1,j) - c(t-1,j)}{2}, \qquad \Delta^2 c(t,j) = \frac{\Delta c(t+1,j) - \Delta c(t-1,j)}{2}Δc(t,j)=2c(t+1,j)−c(t−1,j)​,Δ2c(t,j)=2Δc(t+1,j)−Δc(t−1,j)​
(12)

The 13 coefficients, their 13 first differences, and their 13 second differences give the classic MFCC vector of 39 values per frame. Classical speech recognizers modeled sequences of such vectors with hidden Markov models, as described in Transcription.

Example

The coefficient c(t,2)c(t,2)c(t,2) varies more in speech than in music, because each new vowel and consonant reshapes the spectral envelope while an orchestra holds its timbre for longer. Computed with 26 bands between 300 and 8000 Hz, its standard deviation over all frames is 10.75 for the LibriVox reading and 7.26 for the Brahms clip. Four more public recordings confirm the pattern: two further LibriVox readings by other readers reach 12.84 and 10.50, while Tchaikovsky’s Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy and the jazz piece Vibe Ace reach 9.99 and 7.76. All three readings lie between 10.5 and 12.8, all three music pieces between 7.3 and 10.0. Like LSTER and HZCRR, this segment statistic is a classic feature for speech/music classification.

MFCC values depend on the implementation

Libraries differ in the window, the mel formula, the number and range of the bands, the normalization of the triangles, the logarithm base, the scaling of the DCT, and whether c(t,0)c(t,0)c(t,0) is kept or replaced by the frame energy. MFCC vectors from different implementations, or with different parameters, are not comparable. The index and the queries must use the same pipeline.
