Hidden Markov Models

Where this is used

Query Understanding tags the words of a query with their parts of speech, where a hidden Markov model learns the tag transitions from an annotated corpus and the Viterbi algorithm of this section decodes the most likely tag sequence. Classical speech recognizers model each phoneme with a hidden Markov model, the pipeline that Transcription summarizes and Speech Recognition with HMMs describes in detail.

Sequential data arises whenever observations unfold over time: words in a sentence, acoustic frames in speech, nucleotides in DNA. Often the process that generates the data has a state that we cannot observe directly. A speech recognizer measures acoustic features, not the phonemes that produced them; a part-of-speech tagger sees words, not their grammatical roles. A hidden Markov model (HMM) describes such a process with a sequence of hidden states that follows a Markov chain and emits one observation per step. This section defines the model and the three algorithms that make it practical: the forward algorithm, Viterbi decoding, and Baum-Welch training.

From Markov Chains to Hidden States¶

A Markov chain describes a sequence of states X1,X2,…,XTX_1, X_2, \dots, X_TX1​,X2​,…,XT​ in which the next state depends only on the current one, the Markov assumption:

P(Xt∣Xt−1,…,X1)=P(Xt∣Xt−1)P(X_t ∣ X_{t-1}, \dots, X_1) = P(X_t ∣ X_{t-1})P(Xt​∣Xt−1​,…,X1​)=P(Xt​∣Xt−1​)
(1)

A weather model with the states sunny, cloudy, and rainy, and the probabilities of moving from one to another, is a Markov chain. It is adequate when we can see the states. Many systems provide only indirect evidence. In a hidden Markov model, the state sequence evolves as a Markov chain, and each state emits an observable symbol according to its own probability distribution. The observations are noisy reflections of the hidden states. An HMM consists of

  • a finite set of hidden states 1,…,N1, \dots, N1,…,N,

  • a set of observable symbols, or a density for continuous observations,

  • the transition probabilities aij=P(Xt=j∣Xt−1=i)a_{ij} = P(X_t = j ∣ X_{t-1} = i)aij​=P(Xt​=j∣Xt−1​=i),

  • the emission probabilities bj(o)=P(Ot=o∣Xt=j)b_j(o) = P(O_t = o ∣ X_t = j)bj​(o)=P(Ot​=o∣Xt​=j),

  • and the initial distribution πi=P(X1=i)\pi_i = P(X_1 = i)πi​=P(X1​=i).

For a state sequence x1,…,xTx_1, \dots, x_Tx1​,…,xT​ and an observation sequence o1,…,oTo_1, \dots, o_To1​,…,oT​, the joint probability factors into the initial, transition, and emission terms:

P(x1,…,xT,o1,…,oT)=πx1 bx1(o1)∏t=2Taxt−1xt bxt(ot)P(x_1, \dots, x_T, o_1, \dots, o_T) = \pi_{x_1}\, b_{x_1}(o_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} a_{x_{t-1} x_t}\, b_{x_t}(o_t)P(x1​,…,xT​,o1​,…,oT​)=πx1​​bx1​​(o1​)t=2∏T​axt−1​xt​​bxt​​(ot​)
(2)

The factorization expresses two assumptions: the states follow the Markov property, and each observation depends only on the state at its own time step. In speech recognition, the states can stand for parts of phonemes, and each emits acoustic feature vectors from a mixture of Gaussians (Clustering).

We use a small model as the running example. A friend lives far away and spends every day walking, shopping, or cleaning, depending on the weather, which we cannot observe. The hidden states are Rainy and Sunny, with the initial probabilities 0.6 and 0.4. Rainy days are followed by rainy days with probability 0.7, sunny days by sunny days with 0.6. On a rainy day, the friend walks with probability 0.1, shops with 0.4, and cleans with 0.5; on a sunny day, the probabilities are 0.6, 0.3, and 0.1. On three days, the friend reports walk, shop, clean.

Working with an HMM involves three problems. Evaluation computes the probability of an observation sequence, which measures how well a model explains the data and lets us choose the best of several models, for example one per word. Decoding finds the most likely sequence of hidden states, such as the phonemes or the part-of-speech tags. Learning estimates the parameters from data.

The Forward Algorithm¶

The probability of the observations is the sum of the joint probability over all state sequences. With NNN states and TTT steps, there are NTN^TNT sequences, far too many to enumerate. The Markov property allows a recursion instead. The forward variable αt(j)\alpha_t(j)αt​(j) is the probability of the first ttt observations together with the state jjj at time ttt. It is computed from the forward variables of the previous step, because the state at time ttt depends only on the state at time t−1t - 1t−1:

Viterbi Decoding¶

Decoding looks for the single most likely state sequence. The Viterbi algorithm uses the same recursion as the forward algorithm with a maximum instead of a sum. This is dynamic programming: the best path to a state at time ttt consists of the best path to some state at time t−1t - 1t−1 plus one transition, so we only need to keep the best path to each state.

The best final state is Rainy with 0.01344. Its backpointer leads to Rainy on the second day, whose backpointer leads to Sunny on the first: the most likely weather was sunny, rainy, rainy. On the second day alone, Sunny had the larger δ\deltaδ; the best overall path still goes through Rainy, because only Rainy explains the cleaning on the third day well. This is why Viterbi keeps one path per state and decides only at the end.

Learning the Parameters¶

With labeled data, where every observation sequence comes with its state sequence, as in a part-of-speech corpus, the parameters are relative frequencies: count the transitions and emissions and normalize them, with smoothing (Naive Bayes) for the counts that are zero.

Without state labels, the Baum-Welch algorithm estimates the parameters with the expectation maximization of Clustering. Its E-step uses the forward and backward variables to compute the expected number of times each state is visited and each transition and emission is used. Its M-step sets the parameters to these expected counts, normalized. Each iteration increases the likelihood of the observations. Viterbi training is a simpler alternative that updates the parameters from the single best state sequence instead of the expected counts, a hard assignment like k-means; it is faster but more prone to poor local optima. Both depend on a good initialization, because the likelihood has many local optima.

Numerical Stability¶

The forward variables and the Viterbi probabilities are products of many probabilities and fall below the smallest representable number after a few hundred steps, which is a short utterance in speech recognition. Viterbi runs in log space, where products become sums and the maximum is unaffected. The forward algorithm needs a sum of probabilities, which in log space becomes log⁡∑ieli\log \sum_i e^{l_i}log∑i​eli​, computed stably by factoring out the largest term; alternatively, the forward variables are rescaled to sum to 1 at every step and the scaling factors are accumulated in log space.

Applications and Extensions¶

Hidden Markov models were the standard for speech recognition for three decades, and they have been used for part-of-speech tagging, for gene prediction, where hidden states stand for coding and non-coding regions of DNA, and for the segmentation of sensor data into the phases of an activity. Several extensions relax their assumptions. Higher-order models let a state depend on several previous states, hierarchical HMMs nest models at several levels of abstraction, for example phonemes inside words, and discriminative models such as conditional random fields train for the accuracy of the labels instead of the likelihood of the observations. On large datasets, the neural sequence models of Recurrent Networks and transformers have replaced HMMs in most applications, but HMMs remain useful where the model must be small, trained on little data, or interpretable.
