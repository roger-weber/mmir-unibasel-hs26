Information Theory

Where this is used

Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting chooses the tests of a decision tree by the entropy of the labels and the information gain, and Naive Bayes selects features by their mutual information with the class. Cross-Entropy trains classifiers with the cross-entropy loss, and so do the language models of Model Families and Pretraining and the self-distillation of . Phrases and Compounds scores phrases with the pointwise mutual information. Generative Language Models measures the uncertainty of the next token by its entropy, and Extended Audio and Music Features measures how evenly the energy of a frame is spread.

Information theory measures uncertainty in bits. Claude Shannon (1948) developed it to describe how much a message can be compressed and how fast it can be sent over a noisy channel, and its quantities have since become the standard measures for comparing probability distributions. We define entropy and its meaning as the length of a code, then cross-entropy and the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which explain why the cross-entropy is the standard loss, and finally the mutual information, which measures how much two variables say about each other.

Entropy¶

An event of probability ppp carries the information −log⁡2p-\log_2 p−log2​p bits: a certain event carries none, an event of probability 1/21/21/2 one bit, and an event of probability 1/81/81/8 three bits. The rarer the event, the more it tells us when it happens. The entropy of a random variable is the information we expect from one observation.

Shannon gave the entropy a concrete meaning: it is the smallest average number of bits per symbol with which a sequence of independent symbols can be encoded without loss. A code reaches this bound by giving each symbol a code word of about −log⁡2p(x)-\log_2 p(x)−log2​p(x) bits, short words for frequent symbols and long words for rare ones.

The same idea, short codes for frequent values, makes the d-gap compression of Filtering, Facets, and Compression effective: small gaps are frequent in long posting lists and receive short codes. The entropy of the gap distribution is the limit that no such code can beat.

Cross-Entropy and the Kullback-Leibler Divergence¶

Suppose the data follow a distribution ppp, but we encode them with a code built for a model distribution qqq, with code words of −log⁡2q(x)-\log_2 q(x)−log2​q(x) bits. The average length is the cross-entropy. It exceeds the entropy by the bits that the wrong model wastes, the Kullback-Leibler divergence. We write ppp for the distribution of the data and qqq for the model, as Cross-Entropy and most texts on information theory do.

Why the Cross-Entropy Is the Standard Loss¶

A model qqq trained on examples x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_nx1​,…,xn​ is usually fitted by maximum likelihood: it should assign the observed examples a high probability. The average negative log-likelihood is

where p^\hat{p}p^​ is the empirical distribution of the training data, the share of each value among the examples. The entropy H(p^)H(\hat{p})H(p^​) does not depend on the model. Minimizing the cross-entropy, maximizing the likelihood, and minimizing the KL divergence from the data to the model are therefore the same goal. For classification with one-hot labels, the entropy of each label is zero, and the loss of Cross-Entropy is the KL divergence itself. The causal language modeling of Model Families and Pretraining applies the same loss to the next token at every position.

With soft targets, the loss cannot reach zero. The self-distillation of trains a student qqq to match a teacher ppp. In its example, the teacher gives (0.7,0.2,0.1)(0.7, 0.2, 0.1)(0.7,0.2,0.1), the student (0.5,0.3,0.2)(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)(0.5,0.3,0.2), and the loss is 0.887 nats. Its minimum is the entropy of the teacher, 0.802 nats, and the difference of 0.085 nats is the KL divergence that training can remove. The distillation of small models from large ones in Model Families and Pretraining works the same way.

The perplexity turns the cross-entropy into a number of choices. A language model with a cross-entropy of HHH bits per token on a test text is as uncertain as a uniform choice among 2H2^H2H tokens. The next-token distribution of the example in Generative Language Models has an entropy of 5.44 bits, the uncertainty of a uniform choice among 25.44=432^{5.44} = 4325.44=43 tokens. Preference alignment uses the KL divergence in a further role: it keeps the aligned model close to the model it started from, so that it learns the preferences without losing what it knew.

Mutual Information¶

Two variables share information when knowing one reduces the uncertainty about the other. The mutual information measures this reduction: the entropy of YYY minus the entropy that remains once XXX is known. It equals the KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of the marginals, the distribution the two variables would have if they were independent.

The mutual information connects several measures of the chapters.

  • Information gain. The information gain of Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting subtracts the average entropy of the labels after a split from the entropy before it, which is H(Y)−H(Y∣X)H(Y) - H(Y ∣ X)H(Y)−H(Y∣X) on the training data. It is the mutual information between the feature and the label: for Outlook and the weather labels, 0.247 bits.

  • Pointwise mutual information. The PMI of Phrases and Compounds, log⁡2p(t1,t2)p(t1) p(t2)\log_2 \frac{p(t_1, t_2)}{p(t_1)\, p(t_2)}log2​p(t1​)p(t2​)p(t1​,t2​)​, is the term inside the sum for one pair of values. The mutual information is its expected value over all pairs. A single PMI value can be negative and is large for rare pairs; the average weights each pair by its probability, so rare pairs count little.

  • Feature selection. Naive Bayes keeps the terms with the highest mutual information between the presence of the term and the class.

Example: A term and the class “spam”

Of 100 documents, 50 are spam. The term “free” occurs in 30 spam and 5 other documents. Its PMI with spam is log⁡2100⋅3035⋅50=0.778\log_2 \frac{100 \cdot 30}{35 \cdot 50} = 0.778log2​35⋅50100⋅30​=0.778 bits, and summing the four cells of the table (term present or absent, spam or not) gives a mutual information of 0.214 bits. A term that occurs in 10 spam and 10 other documents has a mutual information of exactly 0: it says nothing about the class.

Contrastive training, as in the image-text models of , connects to the mutual information as well. Its loss is a cross-entropy over a batch: for each image, the model must pick the matching text among the NNN texts of the batch. Van den Oord et al. (2018) showed that minimizing this loss maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information between the two views, and the bound can never exceed log⁡N\log NlogN, one reason why contrastive models train with large batches.
