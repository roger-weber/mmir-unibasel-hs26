The Curse of Dimensionality

Optional Background Reading

This section explains why exact nearest-neighbor search fails in high dimensions, with the geometry and statistics behind it, and it is here as background. The next section only needs its conclusions, so a reader focused on the search methods can skim or skip it. The next section opens with a short recap of what carries forward.

All the structures of the previous section were developed with two or three dimensions in mind. Their inventors argued with drawings in the plane: rectangles that enclose points, spheres that touch rectangles, regions that can be skipped. When feature vectors with dozens or hundreds of dimensions arrived, the natural expectation was that these arguments would carry over, and that an index would prune the search in 100 dimensions much as it does in 2. This intuition turned out to be wrong. This section shows why, first for distances and then for the dot product, and draws a conclusion that shapes the rest of the chapter: in high dimensions, the exact nearest neighbor is not worth chasing.

Distances grow with the dimension¶

Take the unit square and two points that are clearly close: the center c=(0.5,0.5)\mathbf{c} = (0.5, 0.5)c=(0.5,0.5) and a point p=(0.4,0.4)\mathbf{p} = (0.4, 0.4)p=(0.4,0.4) slightly off-center. Around p\mathbf{p}p we draw a circle of radius r=3r = 3r=3, shown in Figure 1. The farthest corner of the square is only 0.85 away from p\mathbf{p}p, so the circle covers the whole square many times over. A radius of 3 looks absurdly large.

Now we add dimensions, keeping the same pattern: c\mathbf{c}c has the value 0.5 and p\mathbf{p}p the value 0.4 in every dimension. The two points differ by 0.1 in each of ddd dimensions, so their Euclidean distance is 0.1d0.1\sqrt{d}0.1d​. The farthest corner of the unit cube lies at distance 0.6d0.6\sqrt{d}0.6d​ from p\mathbf{p}p. Both distances grow with d\sqrt{d}d​, while the radius stays fixed:

  • Up to d=25d = 25d=25, the sphere of radius 3 still contains the whole unit cube, because 0.625=30.6\sqrt{25} = 30.625​=3.

  • Beyond d=25d = 25d=25, corners of the cube start to fall outside the sphere, and their number grows quickly.

  • Beyond d=900d = 900d=900, even the center c\mathbf{c}c falls outside, because 0.1900=30.1\sqrt{900} = 30.1900​=3.

Nine hundred dimensions are common for features extracted from media. In such a space, a point we would call “close” by any 2D standard lies outside a radius that covers the entire square in 2D. Distances simply grow with the dimension, and they grow for all pairs of points alike.

Distances lose their contrast¶

Growing distances alone would only change the scale. The real problem is that the distances of all points to a query grow at the same pace, so they become ever more similar. A simple measure for this is the contrast between the farthest and the nearest point.

Key Formula: Loss of Contrast

For a query and a fixed set of data points, let δmax⁡\delta_{\max}δmax​ and δmin⁡\delta_{\min}δmin​ be the distances to the farthest and the nearest point. For independent random data, as d→∞d \to \inftyd→∞:

δmax⁡−δmin⁡δmin⁡→0\frac{\delta_{\max} - \delta_{\min}}{\delta_{\min}} \to 0δmin​δmax​−δmin​​→0
(1)

The farthest point is barely farther away than the nearest one; “nearest” loses its distinction.

The loss of contrast has two consequences. The first concerns the index structures of the previous section; the second concerns the meaning of the nearest neighbor itself.

Consequence 1: trees can no longer prune¶

The optimal algorithm of the previous section must inspect every node whose region intersects the NN-sphere, the sphere around the query that passes through the nearest neighbor. If the nearest neighbor is far away, this sphere is large, and few regions can be skipped. To estimate the cost, we use a simple model: the data space is the unit cube, the dimensions are independent and uniformly distributed, and we use Euclidean distance. In this model, the probability that a point falls into a region equals the region’s volume.

Two quantities compete, shown side by side in Figure 3. The first is the expected nearest-neighbor distance, which grows with the dimension (left). For one million points, it is 0.24 at d=10d = 10d=10, 1.37 at d=40d = 40d=40, 2.03 at d=60d = 60d=60, and 2.97 at d=100d = 100d=100.

The second is the size of a leaf (right). A tree cannot split every dimension. Each split in the middle of a dimension halves the number of points in a region, so one million points support only about 14 splits: after that, a leaf holds 106/214≈6110^6 / 2^{14} \approx 61106/214≈61 points, about one disk page. A leaf is therefore split along d′≈14d' \approx 14d′≈14 dimensions and spans the full range in all others. The largest distance from any query to such a leaf is lmax⁡=0.5d′≈1.87l_{\max} = 0.5\sqrt{d'} \approx 1.87lmax​=0.5d′​≈1.87, no matter how many dimensions the space has.

Once the expected nearest-neighbor distance exceeds lmax⁡l_{\max}lmax​, every leaf lies closer to the query than the query’s own nearest neighbor. Every leaf intersects the NN-sphere, and even the optimal algorithm must visit all of them. For one million points, this happens between d=40d = 40d=40 and d=60d = 60d=60. Beyond that point, the hierarchy only adds overhead: why build a tree if every leaf must be read anyway? In practice the index loses even earlier. A full scan reads the data sequentially, while a tree reads its leaves one by one; once a tree must visit a sizeable fraction of its leaves, the scan is faster. Weber, Schek, and Blott showed that for uniform data this happens beyond roughly ten dimensions (see the Further Reading section of the chapter summary).

Two more surprises of high-dimensional space (optional reading)

The growth of distances is one of several ways in which high-dimensional space defies intuition. Two further puzzles make the point. Each one ends with a question: try to answer it before you open the resolution.

The escaping sphere. Place a square of side 2 with a circle of radius 1 at each corner, so that the circles cover all edges of the square. A further circle at the center just touches the four corner circles, as in Figure 4.

What is the radius rrr of the central circle? Now repeat the construction in ddd dimensions, with a hypersphere at each corner of the hypercube and a central hypersphere that touches all of them. Does the central sphere stay inside the cube?

Resolution

The distance from the center of the square to a corner is 2\sqrt{2}2​, so the central circle has radius r=2−1≈0.41r = \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.41r=2​−1≈0.41. In ddd dimensions, the distance from the center of the hypercube to a corner is d\sqrt{d}d​, so r=d−1r = \sqrt{d} - 1r=d​−1. At d=4d = 4d=4 the radius is exactly 1, and the central sphere touches the faces of the cube. At d=100d = 100d=100 it is 9, and the “inner” sphere reaches far outside the cube that supposedly contains it.

The reason is that the corner spheres cover less and less of each face as the dimension grows. Already in 3D, the center of each face is left uncovered, and in higher dimensions the central sphere escapes through these gaps.

Where have all the points gone? Place a smaller square of side s=0.99s = 0.99s=0.99 at the center of the unit square, as in Figure 5, and distribute points uniformly over the unit square. The inner square seems to cover almost everything.

Almost every point lies within 0.005 of one of the 2d2d2d faces of the cube. The center of a high-dimensional space is empty, and any partition of the space into regions produces regions that almost certainly contain no data.

Consequence 2: what does “nearest” still mean?¶

The second consequence is more fundamental. If all points are almost equally far from the query, the nearest neighbor is only marginally nearer than the rest. Proximity stops expressing similarity: a point that is 14 percent farther away than the nearest neighbor is not meaningfully less similar.

Real data is kinder than this model. The analysis above assumes uniformly distributed, independent dimensions, and real feature vectors and embeddings are neither. They form clusters: images of sunsets lie closer to each other than to images of forests, and documents about cardiology lie closer to each other than to documents about tax law. Relative to the uniform background, in which everything appears equally far away, the members of a cluster stand out as outliers with clearly smaller distances. This is why vector search works at all in high dimensions, and why the approximate methods of the next section can exploit the structure of the data.

Within such a cluster, however, the loss of contrast remains. The nearest neighbor and the next few points lie at almost the same distance from the query. The exact nearest neighbor is not necessarily the best match in the sense the user cares about; it is one good match among several that are about equally close. Spending a full scan, or a visit to every leaf, to determine which of them is nearest buys a distinction that carries little meaning.

The dot product: losing direction¶

Embeddings are usually compared with the dot product or cosine, so we repeat the argument for similarity scores. In 2D, it seems reasonable to assume that a query and its best matches lie in the same quadrant. In Figure 6, the query q\mathbf{q}q points to the upper right. Under cosine similarity, d\mathbf{d}d has the highest score because it has the smallest angle to q\mathbf{q}q. Under the dot product, e\mathbf{e}e wins, because no other point lies beyond the hyperplane through e\mathbf{e}e orthogonal to q\mathbf{q}q. Both best matches lie in the query’s quadrant.

The high-dimensional counterpart of a quadrant is an orthant: the region in which every component has a fixed sign. Two dimensions have 4 quadrants; ddd dimensions have 2d2^{d}2d orthants. Two vectors share an orthant if their components agree in sign in every dimension. For random vectors, each sign agrees with probability 1/21/21/2, so two vectors share an orthant with probability 2−d2^{-d}2−d.

The number of orthants soon outgrows any collection. With one billion vectors, there are on average about one million vectors per orthant at d=10d = 10d=10, but fewer than one at d=30d = 30d=30, and about 8×10−228 \times 10^{-22}8×10−22 at d=100d = 100d=100. Most orthants are empty, and almost no two vectors share one. “The same direction as the query” no longer describes any stored vector.

Conceptually, this is not yet a problem. Each dimension of an embedding can be read as a latent concept, and two vectors that agree on most concepts are similar even if they disagree on a few. Agreement in many dimensions is good enough. The trouble lies in how much “many” still stands out.

Dot products concentrate around zero¶

Figure 7 shows the dot product between pairs of random unit vectors. At d=2d = 2d=2, the values spread over the full range from -1 to 1. As the dimension grows, they concentrate around 0, and at d=8192d = 8192d=8192 they form a narrow spike: two random vectors in high dimensions are almost orthogonal.

A short statistical argument explains the spike. Draw the components xkx_kxk​ and yky_kyk​ of two vectors independently and uniformly from [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1]. Each product xkykx_k y_kxk​yk​ has expected value 0 and variance 1/91/91/9. The dot product is the sum of ddd such products, so it has expected value 0 in any dimension, and its standard deviation grows like d/3\sqrt{d}/3d​/3. The lengths of the two vectors, however, grow faster: each is about d/3\sqrt{d/3}d/3​, so their product is about d/3d/3d/3. Dividing the two gives the cosine, whose spread shrinks with the dimension.

For two independent random vectors in ddd dimensions, the cosine similarity has expected value 0 and a standard deviation of about

The positive and negative products cancel out more and more completely; random vectors become almost orthogonal.

What does this mean for the dot product? For unit vectors, which most embedding models produce and which Figure 7 uses, the dot product is the cosine, and nothing changes. For vectors that are not normalized, the spread of the dot product grows like d/3\sqrt{d}/3d​/3, so in absolute numbers it does not shrink. But it must be read against the scale of the vectors: the largest possible dot product of two vectors is the product of their lengths, about d/3d/3d/3. Relative to this scale, the spread is (d/3)/(d/3)=1/d(\sqrt{d}/3) / (d/3) = 1/\sqrt{d}(d​/3)/(d/3)=1/d​, exactly as for the cosine.

The lengths themselves concentrate as well. In our example, the length of a random vector is about d/3\sqrt{d/3}d/3​ with a spread of about 0.26 in every dimension, so its relative variation shrinks like 1/d1/\sqrt{d}1/d​: about 4.5 percent at d=100d = 100d=100 and 1.6 percent at d=768d = 768d=768. The dot product is the cosine multiplied by two almost equal lengths, and it ranks random vectors almost exactly like the cosine. In a simulation with 20,000 pairs, the correlation between the two measures is 0.88 at d=2d = 2d=2, 0.998 at d=100d = 100d=100, and 0.9997 at d=768d = 768d=768. The length effect that separates the dot product from the cosine in 2D fades, and the concentration applies to both measures. Learned embeddings may still carry some information in their lengths, but the random background against which a good match must stand out concentrates in the same way.

For the 768-dimensional embeddings of the previous chapter, σcos⁡≈1/768=0.036\sigma_{\cos} \approx 1/\sqrt{768} = 0.036σcos​≈1/768​=0.036; at d=8192d = 8192d=8192, it is 0.011, and the values that separate good from bad matches differ in the third decimal. Even the best of one million random vectors reaches a cosine of only about 0.17 at d=768d = 768d=768.

The discussion now runs parallel to the one for distances. Vectors that live alone in their orthants are hard to group, because no two of them agree in all signs, and the loss of contrast again weakens the meaning of similarity. Real data again saves the method. The embeddings of related documents form clusters, and their cosine values stand far above the narrow band around zero that random pairs occupy. A relevant document is an outlier in the distribution of scores. But among these outliers, the top few scores lie close together, and the document with the highest score is not necessarily the best semantic match. Any of the top-kkk results may be an equally good, or even better, answer.

Good matches instead of the best match¶

High-dimensional space does not behave like the plane on which our intuition was trained. Distances grow, their contrast fades, and directions become meaningless; index structures that prune well in 2D end up visiting every leaf. At the same time, the loss of contrast questions the goal itself. When the nearest neighbor is only marginally nearer than its competitors, chasing the exact nearest neighbor is wasted effort. What matters is to find good matches: points that clearly stand out from the uniform background, even if they are not provably the nearest.

The retrieval pipelines of Building Semantic Search relax the requirement further. A fast retriever based on embeddings collects candidates, and a reranker reorders them with a more precise but more expensive model. The retriever does not need to deliver the exact top-kkk in the exact order, as long as the good hits are among its candidates; the reranker restores the precision. Exact nearest-neighbor search is therefore rarely needed. Giving up exactness on purpose, and searching approximately for good matches, buys back the speed that the curse of dimensionality took away. This is the subject of the next section.
