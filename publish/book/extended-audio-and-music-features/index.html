Extended Audio and Music Features

Where this is used

Perceptual Features mentions the entropy of energy as a third time-domain feature next to the short-time energy and the zero-crossing rate; the first section below defines it. Musical Features introduces chroma, the fundamental frequency, the onset strength, and the tempo from its autocorrelation, and it names three methods without their details: the harmonic sum, the estimation of several simultaneous pitches, and beat tracking by dynamic programming. The sections below supply these details. All methods work on the frames and the spectrum of From Sound to Spectrogram.

Each method answers a question that the basic features leave open. How abrupt is the energy change within a frame? Which pitch class does a spectrum bin belong to? Which fundamental explains a set of harmonics, and which several fundamentals explain a chord? Which of the many periodicities of an onset pattern is the tempo, and where exactly are the beats?

Entropy of Energy¶

The short-time energy of Perceptual Features says how loud a frame is, but not how the energy is distributed within it. A frame with a steady tone and a frame that is quiet except for a short click can have the same energy. The entropy of energy distinguishes them. It splits frame ttt of NNN samples into LLL sub-frames of NsubN_\text{sub}Nsub​ samples each, with N=L⋅NsubN = L \cdot N_\text{sub}N=L⋅Nsub​, computes the energy EjE_jEj​ of each sub-frame, and normalizes these energies by their sum, so that they add up to 1 like a probability distribution.

The entropy is the information-theoretic entropy of Information Theory applied to the distribution of energy over time. A low value signals an abrupt change, such as the onset of a note, a plosive sound in speech, or a gunshot. The analogous spectral entropy applies the same formula to the energies of frequency bands instead of sub-frames; it is high for noise, whose energy spreads over all bands, and low for a tone.

Example: A tone and a click

A frame of 40 ms at 16 kHz has 640 samples. With L=10L = 10L=10 sub-frames of 4 ms, a steady 440 Hz tone gives H=3.320H = 3.320H=3.320 bits, almost the maximum of log⁡210=3.322\log_2 10 = 3.322log2​10=3.322 bits. A click of 2 ms that falls into two neighboring sub-frames, over a quiet tone of one percent of its amplitude, gives H=0.547H = 0.547H=0.547 bits.

In silent frames, all EjE_jEj​ are zero and the normalization divides by zero. Implementations such as pyAudioAnalysis (Giannakopoulos, 2015), which uses ten sub-frames by default, add a small constant to the denominator and inside the logarithm.

Fundamental Frequency by Harmonic Summation¶

A harmonic tone with the fundamental f0f_0f0​ has peaks in its spectrum at f0,2f0,3f0,…f_0, 2 f_0, 3 f_0, \dotsf0​,2f0​,3f0​,…. The harmonic sum turns this into an estimator: for every candidate f0f_0f0​, add up the magnitudes at its first MMM harmonics, and choose the candidate with the largest sum, its salience. Summing with equal weights fails, because a candidate at half the true fundamental collects the same peaks: every harmonic of 220 Hz is also an even harmonic of 110 Hz. Klapuri (2006) therefore weights the harmonics.

Several Simultaneous Pitches¶

A chord contains several fundamentals, each with its own harmonics. The harmonic sum finds the strongest one; the others follow by iteration.

  1. Compute the magnitude spectrum ∣X(0)(k)∣|X^{(0)}(k)|∣X(0)(k)∣ of the frame, the starting point of the iteration.

  2. Estimate f^0\hat{f}_0f^​0​ on the current spectrum ∣X(i)(k)∣|X^{(i)}(k)|∣X(i)(k)∣ with the harmonic sum. The weighting is the same in every iteration.

  3. Subtract the magnitudes at the harmonics of f^0\hat{f}_0f^​0​ to obtain ∣X(i+1)(k)∣|X^{(i+1)}(k)|∣X(i+1)(k)∣, so that the next estimate cannot find the same note again.

  4. Repeat from step 2 as long as the remaining magnitude ∑k∣X(i)(k)∣\sum_k |X^{(i)}(k)|∑k​∣X(i)(k)∣ exceeds a threshold ε\varepsilonε, or until a given number of notes is found. The threshold, or the number of notes, decides how many pitches the method reports.

Example: A fifth

A chord of 220 Hz (five harmonics as above) and 330 Hz (four harmonics with the amplitudes 0.8, 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2) gives 221 Hz in the first iteration. After its harmonics are removed, the second iteration gives 332 Hz. The partial at 660 Hz is the third harmonic of 220 Hz and the second of 330 Hz, and it disappears with the first note; the second estimate rests on the remaining harmonics of 330 Hz. Practical estimators therefore subtract less than the full peak at each harmonic.

Fundamental Frequency by Autocorrelation¶

The time domain offers an alternative. A periodic signal resembles itself when shifted by one period, so the autocorrelation of the frame is large at the lag of one period. With a search range from fminf_\text{min}fmin​ to fmaxf_\text{max}fmax​ for the fundamental, the lags mmm in samples are limited to fs/fmax≤m≤fs/fminf_s / f_\text{max} \le m \le f_s / f_\text{min}fs​/fmax​≤m≤fs​/fmin​, and the lag m0m_0m0​ with the largest autocorrelation gives the fundamental:

(4)

For the tone at 220 Hz with a search range from 60 to 1,000 Hz at 16 kHz, the lags run from 16 to 266 samples. The maximum lies at m0=73m_0 = 73m0​=73, which gives 16,000/73=219.216{,}000 / 73 = 219.216,000/73=219.2 Hz. The true period is 72.7 samples, and the neighboring lags 72 and 74 would give 222.2 Hz and 216.2 Hz, so the resolution of the estimate is limited by the integer lags; interpolating between neighboring lags refines it. The autocorrelation is also large at twice the period, since a signal shifted by two periods matches itself as well. Because the sum has fewer terms at larger lags, the true period usually wins, but the margin can be small.

Tempo with a Tempo Prior¶

Musical Features estimates the tempo from the autocorrelation of the onset strength o(t)o(t)o(t). With a hop of HHH samples, the onset strength has fr=fs/Hf_r = f_s / Hfr​=fs​/H frames per second, and a lag of lll frames corresponds to 60⋅fr/l60 \cdot f_r / l60⋅fr​/l beats per minute. The conversion uses the frame rate of the onset strength, not the sampling rate of the audio. The autocorrelation has peaks at the beat period and at its multiples and fractions, and the highest of them is not always the one a listener would tap.

Ellis (2007) therefore weights the autocorrelation with a tempo prior, a Gaussian on a logarithmic time axis around a preferred period τ0\tau_0τ0​. On the training data of a beat-tracking evaluation, the best agreement with the tempos chosen by listeners, 77 percent, was reached with τ0=0.5\tau_0 = 0.5τ0​=0.5 s, which corresponds to 120 bpm, and a width of στ=1.4\sigma_\tau = 1.4στ​=1.4 octaves.

The prior, the red curve, peaks at 0.5 s and lowers the peaks far from it. The highest weighted peak lies at about 0.36 s, a tempo of about 168 bpm; the second, at twice the lag, corresponds to 84 bpm.

A synthetic onset strength of 6 seconds at fr=100f_r = 100fr​=100 frames per second has onsets of strength 1 on every beat at 0.25, 0.75, 1.25, ... s, a tempo of 120 bpm. Beat Tracking by Dynamic Programming¶

The tempo gives the period, not the position of the beats. A beat tracker chooses beat times t1,…,tnt_1, \dots, t_nt1​,…,tn​ that coincide with strong onsets and follow each other at intervals close to the period τp\tau_pτp​. The two goals conflict: onsets alone would place beats on every accent and off-beat, and the period alone would tick like a metronome that ignores the music. Ellis (2007) combined both in one objective with a balance factor α\alphaα.

The penalty FFF is 0 for an interval equal to the period and symmetric on a logarithmic axis, so an interval of twice the period costs as much as one of half the period. The tracker maximizes CCC over the number and the positions of the beats.

The objective decomposes into a sum over consecutive beats, which allows dynamic programming. The best score C∗(t)C^*(t)C∗(t) of all beat sequences that end with a beat at time ttt is the onset strength at ttt plus the best combination of a predecessor τ\tauτ and its own best score:

The search covers predecessors from 2τp2 \tau_p2τp​ to τp/2\tau_p / 2τp​/2 before ttt, since the penalty makes more distant ones unlikely. The algorithm computes C∗C^*C∗ and the backpointer P∗P^*P∗ for every frame from the start, takes the frame with the largest C∗C^*C∗ near the end as the last beat, and follows the backpointers to the first. This is the structure of the Viterbi algorithm of Hidden Markov Models: one best path per end point, and the decision at the end. Ellis’s Matlab version of both loops has ten lines, and the cost grows linearly with the length of the piece, with 1.5 τp1.5 \, \tau_p1.5τp​ predecessors per frame.

Example: Choosing α

We track the beats of the synthetic rhythm of the tempo example with τp=50\tau_p = 50τp​=50 frames. With α=2\alpha = 2α=2 or less, the tracker returns 15 beats: the nine beats at 0.25, 1.25, 1.75, 2.25, 3.25, 3.75, 4.25, 5.25, and 5.75 s, and around every accent a detour over the off-beat, 0.62 and 0.95 s instead of the beat at 0.75 s, and likewise near 2.95 and 4.95 s. Each detour replaces two intervals of 0.5 s by three intervals of 0.37, 0.33, and 0.30 s, which costs (0.091+0.173+0.261)⋅α=0.52⋅α(0.091 + 0.173 + 0.261) \cdot \alpha = 0.52 \cdot \alpha(0.091+0.173+0.261)⋅α=0.52⋅α, and gains between 1.2 and 1.4 in onset strength, since it collects 0.7 and 1.5 instead of 1. Above α≈2.6\alpha \approx 2.6α≈2.6, every detour costs more than it gains. With α=5\alpha = 5α=5 or more, the tracker returns the 12 beats at 0.25, 0.75, ..., 5.75 s, all on the beat.

The value of α\alphaα depends on the scale of the onset strength. Ellis used 400 by default and found 680 best on the evaluation data. With a fixed τp\tau_pτp​, the tracker follows tempo changes of about 10 percent; larger changes require a local period from the tempogram, at the price of losing the guarantee that dynamic programming finds the best sequence. The librosa library (McFee et al., 2015) implements the onset strength, the tempogram, the tempo prior, and this beat tracker.
