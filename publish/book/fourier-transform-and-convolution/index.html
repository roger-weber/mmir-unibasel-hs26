Fourier Transform and Convolution

Where this is used

From Sound to Spectrogram defines the discrete Fourier transform and the short-time Fourier transform, and it introduces windows against spectral leakage; this section explains where leakage comes from and what the windows trade. Perceptual Features applies a cosine transform to the log mel energies of MFCC. convolves images with Sobel and Gabor kernels, Convolutional Networks learns such kernels, and Extended Image Features computes Gabor filters in Fourier space with the convolution theorem. Extended Audio and Music Features computes autocorrelations, and the cepstral mean normalization of Speech Recognition with HMMs relies on a convolution becoming a sum.

The Fourier transform describes a signal as a sum of waves. Three of its properties carry the methods of the chapters: it is a change of basis that loses nothing, it can be computed fast, and it turns every convolution into a product. We treat these in turn, then look at the price of cutting a signal into frames and at the cosine transform.

Why the FFT Is Fast¶

Computed directly, the NNN coefficients need NNN products each, N2N^2N2 in total. The fast Fourier transform (Cooley and Tukey, 1965) splits the sum into the samples with even and with odd indices. Each half is a DFT of length N/2N/2N/2, and with E(k)E(k)E(k) and O(k)O(k)O(k) for their coefficients,

Convolution and the Convolution Theorem¶

A convolution slides a kernel hhh along a signal and computes a weighted sum at every position. On a frame of NNN samples that is treated as periodic, the circular convolution is

(x∗h)(n)=∑m=0N−1h(m) x((n−m) mod N)(x * h)(n) = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} h(m)\, x\bigl((n - m) \text{ mod } N\bigr)(x∗h)(n)=m=0∑N−1​h(m)x((n−m) mod N)

Smoothing, edge detection with the Sobel kernels, Gabor filtering, and the layers of a convolutional network are convolutions or, without the flip of the kernel, the closely related cross-correlations (Convolutional Networks).

The waves are the eigenvectors of every convolution, and the DFT coefficients H(k)H(k)H(k) of the kernel are its eigenvalues. Since the DFT writes any signal as a sum of waves, a convolution scales each wave by H(k)H(k)H(k) and adds them up again. In matrix form, a circular convolution is a matrix whose rows are shifted copies of the kernel, and the DFT diagonalizes every such matrix, as the eigenvectors of Eigenvectors, SVD, and PCA diagonalize a symmetric one.

The theorem computes circular convolutions. A signal of length LLL convolved with a kernel of length KKK has a linear convolution of length L+K−1L + K - 1L+K−1, and the products in Fourier space reproduce it only if both are padded with zeros to at least this length. Otherwise, the end of the result wraps around to the beginning.

Example: Wrap-around

The linear convolution of (1,2,3)(1, 2, 3)(1,2,3) with the difference kernel (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) is (1,1,1,−3)(1, 1, 1, -3)(1,1,1,−3). Transforms of length 4, with one zero appended to the signal and two to the kernel, give exactly this result. Transforms of length 3 give (−2,1,1)(-2, 1, 1)(−2,1,1): the last value -3 has wrapped around and been added to the first.

For images, the wrap-around makes a filter near the left border reach across to the right one. Extended Image Features measures this error, pads against it, and compares the cost of the two routes: a direct convolution needs K2K^2K2 products per pixel for a K×KK \times KK×K kernel, the route through Fourier space a number per pixel that grows only with the logarithm of the image size. Small kernels, such as the 3×33 \times 33×3 kernels of Sobel and of most convolutional networks, are therefore applied directly, and large kernels and filter banks, which share the transform of the image, in Fourier space.

The same theorem computes correlations. The autocorrelation of a frame, which estimates the fundamental frequency and the tempo in Extended Audio and Music Features, is the inverse transform of the power spectrum, R=F−1(∣X∣2)R = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(|X|^2)R=F−1(∣X∣2), with zero padding to twice the frame length against wrap-around. This computes all lags at once in O(Nlog⁡N)O(N \log N)O(NlogN) instead of O(N2)O(N^2)O(N2).

Windows and Spectral Leakage¶

The DFT assumes that the frame repeats periodically. A tone that completes a whole number of cycles in the frame continues smoothly into the next copy, and its energy falls into a single bin. A tone with a fractional number of cycles jumps at the boundary between copies, and the jump spreads energy over all bins, the spectral leakage of From Sound to Spectrogram.

The convolution theorem explains the effect and the cure. Cutting a frame out of a longer signal multiplies the signal with a window w(n)w(n)w(n), a rectangle of ones for a plain cut. In the frequency domain, the product becomes a convolution: every spectral line is replaced by a copy of the window’s spectrum W(k)W(k)W(k). The rectangle has a narrow central peak and high side lobes, which carry energy far from the true frequency. Bell-shaped windows such as Hann and Hamming fade the frame in and out, remove the jump, and have much lower side lobes, at the price of a central peak twice as wide.

Example: A tone between two bins

A tone that completes 10.5 cycles in a frame of 64 samples falls between bins 10 and 11. Without a window, the spectrum at bin 20, ten bins away, is only 26.7 dB below the peak, and at bin 30 still 31.1 dB below. With a Hamming window, the levels drop to -46.0 and -49.9 dB, with a Hann window to -67.2 and -83.0 dB. A tone with exactly 10 cycles shows no leakage even without a window.

The two windows trade differently. The Hamming window has the lowest nearest side lobe, while the side lobes of the Hann window fall off much faster with the distance, which is why it suppresses the far bins of the example better. A wider central peak blurs two tones that lie close together, a lower side lobe keeps a weak tone visible next to a strong one. Zero padding a frame before the transform samples the spectrum more densely, but it does not separate close tones: the resolution is set by the duration of the frame, as the key formula of From Sound to Spectrogram states. Hann windows that overlap by half their length add up to a constant, so every sample of the signal receives the same total weight over the frames; this is one reason for the hop of half a frame in many spectrograms.

The Discrete Cosine Transform¶

The discrete cosine transform (DCT) represents a real signal with real cosines only:

c(k)=∑n=0N−1x(n)cos⁡(π(2n+1)k2N),0≤k<Nc(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n) \cos\left(\frac{\pi (2n + 1) k}{2N}\right), \qquad 0 \le k < Nc(k)=n=0∑N−1​x(n)cos(2Nπ(2n+1)k​),0≤k<N
(7)

It corresponds to the DFT of the signal extended by its mirror image, which continues smoothly at both ends. Without the jump of the periodic continuation, the energy of smooth signals concentrates in a few low coefficients. MFCC apply the DCT to the log mel energies of a frame (Perceptual Features), JPEG to blocks of 8×88 \times 88×8 pixels.

The DCT is more than a convenient transform: for signals whose neighboring values are strongly correlated, it approximates PCA. Ahmed, Natarajan, and Rao (1974) compared it with the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of such signals, the optimal basis of Eigenvectors, SVD, and PCA.

Example: DCT and principal components

For signals of 8 values in which neighbors jjj steps apart have the correlation 0.9j0.9^j0.9j, we compute the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix and the 8 DCT basis vectors. Each eigenvector has a cosine of at least 0.998 with the corresponding DCT vector, so the two bases are nearly identical. The first two principal components capture 90.1 percent of the variance, the first two DCT coefficients 89.9 percent.

The log mel energies of neighboring bands are strongly correlated, and the DCT decorrelates them almost as well as PCA would, with a fixed basis that needs no training data. Keeping the first 13 coefficients is then a dimensionality reduction that keeps most of the variance, the same reasoning as truncating a PCA.
