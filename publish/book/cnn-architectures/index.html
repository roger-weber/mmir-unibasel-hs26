CNN Architectures

Where this is used

Automated Feature Extraction lists the milestones of neural image classification and links here for the architectures behind them. uses a pretrained ResNet as a feature extractor, and builds on the Vision Transformer described at the end of this section.

This section describes the architectures that lead from hand-designed pipelines to learned features: Rowley’s face detector, LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, GoogLeNet with its inception modules, ResNet, DenseNet, and the Vision Transformer. For each network, we look at its layer structure, the problem its design solved, its size, and its results on the benchmark of its time. Automated Feature Extraction tells the story of these milestones, and shows how a trained network serves as an image feature extractor, what its first-layer filters look like, and how it transfers to new tasks. The building blocks used below, such as convolution, strides, padding, pooling, and 1×11 \times 11×1 convolutions, are introduced in the previous subsection.

Rowley’s Face Detector¶

In 1998, Henry Rowley, Shumeet Baluja, and Takeo Kanade at Carnegie Mellon University published a neural face detector that worked on cluttered photos. Its design keeps the network small and moves most of the work into fixed processing steps around it. Figure 1 shows the version that also finds rotated faces.

The detector examines windows of 20 by 20 pixels. To find faces larger than the window, it builds an image pyramid: it reduces the image repeatedly by a factor of 1.2 and applies the network at every pixel position of every level. Each window passes two preprocessing steps. First, a linear brightness function fitted to an oval face region is subtracted to compensate for uneven lighting. Second, histogram equalization stretches the contrast.

The detection network receives the 400 preprocessed pixel values, but its hidden units do not see the whole window. Four hidden units look at 10 by 10 subregions, 16 at 5 by 5 subregions, and 6 at overlapping horizontal stripes of 20 by 5 pixels. The stripes can detect a pair of eyes or a mouth, and the square fields can detect single eyes, the nose, or the corners of the mouth. The final systems replicate these hidden units two or three times. A single output unit makes the decision: it is trained to produce 1 for a face and -1 for anything else. The local fields resemble the receptive fields of a convolutional layer, but they are fixed by hand, and their weights are not shared across positions.

Face examples are easy to collect: the authors gathered about 1050 of them from face databases. Examples of nonfaces are harder, because almost any image region is a nonface. A bootstrap procedure solves this. The network is trained, applied to 120 scenery images that contain no faces, and every false detection is added to the training set as a negative example; then the network is trained again. A typical run selected about 8000 nonface windows out of 146 million available subimages. At detection time, overlapping detections are merged, and several networks vote. The upright detector found 90.5% of the faces in a test set of 130 complex images.

The rotation-invariant version adds a router network in front of the detector. It has 400 inputs, 15 hidden units, and 36 outputs. Output unit iii stands for an angle of i⋅10∘i \cdot 10^\circi⋅10∘, and to signal a face at angle θ\thetaθ, unit iii is trained to produce cos⁡(θ−i⋅10∘)\cos(\theta - i \cdot 10^\circ)cos(θ−i⋅10∘). The window is rotated upright by the estimated angle before it enters preprocessing and detection. This system found 79.6% of the faces in 180 test images, with few false detections. Figure 2 shows results. The label in the upper left corner of each image gives the number of detected faces, the number of faces in the image, and the number of false detections; the label in the lower right gives the image size in pixels. The eye marks inside the boxes only indicate the estimated orientation; the detector does not locate the eyes.

Every step except the final decision was designed by hand: the window size, the lighting correction, the histogram equalization, the derotation, and the sliding-window search. This made the method hard to reuse. A detector for cats or dogs would need its own preprocessing, its own receptive fields, and its own training set. Neural classifiers of this generation were optimized for one task and did not adapt to a new one without human work.

LeNet¶

Yann LeCun and colleagues at AT&T Bell Labs developed LeNet in several versions between 1989 and 1998, first to read zip codes and later bank checks. LeNet-5, described in full by LeCun and colleagues in 1998, is the version usually meant. Its test bed was the MNIST database with 60,000 training and 10,000 test images of handwritten digits. The digits come from NIST collections written by Census Bureau employees and high school students on forms like the one in Figure 3. Each digit was scaled to fit into a 20 by 20 pixel box while keeping its aspect ratio, which introduced gray levels through anti-aliasing, and then placed into a 28 by 28 image with its center of mass in the center.

LeNet-5 alternates convolution and subsampling and ends with dense layers. Figure 4 shows the layers:

  • C1 applies six 5×55 \times 55×5 convolution kernels and produces six feature maps of 28×2828 \times 2828×28.

  • S2 subsamples each map by combining 2×22 \times 22×2 neighborhoods, which gives six maps of 14×1414 \times 1414×14.

  • C3 applies sixteen 5×55 \times 55×5 kernels and produces sixteen maps of 10×1010 \times 1010×10. In the original network, each C3 map is connected to only a subset of the six S2 maps.

  • S4 subsamples to sixteen maps of 5×55 \times 55×5, 400 values in total.

  • Three dense layers with 120, 84, and 10 units produce the class scores.

The original network takes a 32×3232 \times 3232×32 input, the 28×2828 \times 2828×28 digit surrounded by a border, and uses sigmoid-shaped activation functions. The figure shows the variant of the book Dive into Deep Learning, which takes the 28×2828 \times 2828×28 image directly and pads it by two pixels in C1. Weight sharing keeps the network small. Counting the layers of LeNet-5 gives 60,000 trainable parameters: 156 in C1, 12 in S2, 1,516 in C3, 32 in S4, 48,120 in the layer with 120 units, and 10,164 in the layer with 84 units. Four fifths of them sit in the first dense layer, which connects all 400 values of S4 to 120 units. The original output layer, which compares the 84 values with hand-chosen prototypes of the ten digits, is not counted. The fully connected variant in the figure has 61,706 parameters.

LeNet-5 reached a test error of 0.95% on MNIST, about 99% accuracy, and 0.8% when the training set was enlarged with distorted copies of the digits. The network learned its features from the pixels; apart from the size normalization of the digits, nobody designed a feature. Yet a support vector machine reached a comparable 0.8% in the same comparison, and convolutional networks remained a specialist tool for more than a decade. Today, ensembles of convolutional networks with data augmentation reach error rates below 0.1% on MNIST. The ConvNetJS MNIST demo trains a small convolutional network in the browser and visualizes the network while it learns.

AlexNet¶

AlexNet, by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton, won the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC) of 2012. The challenge asks for one of 1000 classes per image, a large step from the ten classes of digit recognition, and provides about 1.2 million training images, 50,000 validation images, and 150,000 test images. AlexNet follows LeNet’s pattern of convolution, pooling, and dense layers, but at a much larger scale: it has eight layers with parameters, five convolutional and three dense, and it processes images of 224×224224 \times 224224×224 pixels instead of 28×2828 \times 2828×28. The left column of Figure 5 shows its layers:

  • The first convolution applies 96 kernels of 11×11×311 \times 11 \times 311×11×3 with stride 4 to the 224×224×3224 \times 224 \times 3224×224×3 input.

  • The second convolution applies 256 kernels of 5×55 \times 55×5.

  • The third and fourth convolutions apply 384 kernels of 3×33 \times 33×3 each, the fifth 256 kernels of 3×33 \times 33×3.

  • Max pooling with 3×33 \times 33×3 windows and stride 2 follows the first, second, and fifth convolution.

  • Two dense layers with 4096 units each and a dense output layer with 1000 units and softmax classify the image.

Several design choices made the training of such a network possible. The ReLU activation replaced the saturating tanh: a four-layer network with ReLUs reached a training error of 25% on CIFAR-10 six times faster than the same network with tanh units. A single GTX 580 GPU with 3 GB of memory limited the size of the network, so the authors split it across two GPUs. The second, fourth, and fifth convolutions connect only to the feature maps on the same GPU, and training took five to six days. Overlapping pooling, with windows of 3 and stride 2, reduced the top-1 and top-5 error rates by 0.4% and 0.3% compared with non-overlapping pooling. Local response normalization follows the first two convolutions. Against overfitting, the authors used data augmentation, with random 224×224224 \times 224224×224 crops, horizontal reflections, and color changes along the principal components of the RGB values, and dropout in the first two dense layers. Without dropout, the network overfitted substantially.

AlexNet has about 60 million parameters and 650,000 neurons. Counting the layers gives 60,965,224 parameters. Of these, 58.6 million, or 96%, belong to the three dense layers; the first dense layer alone connects 6×6×256=92166 \times 6 \times 256 = 92166×6×256=9216 values to 4096 units. In 32-bit floating point, the dense layers take about 235 MB; some descriptions quote nearly 1 GB for them, about four times too much. The figure shows the single-stream variant of Dive into Deep Learning, whose first convolution uses padding 1 and produces 54×5454 \times 5454×54 maps, and whose last pooling layer outputs 5×5×256=64005 \times 5 \times 256 = 64005×5×256=6400 values; this variant has 50.8 million parameters.

A single AlexNet reached a top-5 error of 18.2% on the validation data, and the average of five networks 16.4%. The winning entry averaged seven networks, two of which had been pretrained on the full ImageNet release of fall 2011 with 15 million images in 22,000 categories, and reached a top-5 test error of 15.3%. The second-best entry, which classified Fisher vectors of hand-crafted features, reached 26.2%.

VGG¶

Karen Simonyan and Andrew Zisserman of the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) at Oxford asked how depth alone affects accuracy. Their networks use only 3×33 \times 33×3 convolutions with stride 1 and padding 1, which keep the spatial size, and 2×22 \times 22×2 max pooling with stride 2, which halves it. A VGG block is a sequence of 3×33 \times 33×3 convolutions followed by one pooling layer. Five blocks with 64, 128, 256, 512, and 512 channels are followed by the same three dense layers as in AlexNet. VGG-11, on the right of Figure 5, has one convolution in each of the first two blocks and two in each of the last three: 8 convolutions plus 3 dense layers make 11 weight layers. VGG-16 has 2, 2, 3, 3, and 3 convolutions per block, 13 in total.

Small kernels lose nothing in reach. Two stacked 3×33 \times 33×3 convolutions see a 5×55 \times 55×5 region of their input, and three see a 7×77 \times 77×7 region. A stack of three 3×33 \times 33×3 layers with CCC input and output channels has 3⋅32C2=27C23 \cdot 3^2 C^2 = 27C^23⋅32C2=27C2 weights, a single 7×77 \times 77×7 layer 49C249C^249C2, which is 81% more. For C=256C = 256C=256, these are 1,769,472 against 3,211,264 weights. The stack also applies three ReLUs instead of one, which makes the function more expressive.

The paper reports 133 million parameters for VGG-11, 138 million for VGG-16, and 144 million for VGG-19. Counting the layers of VGG-16 gives 138,357,544 parameters. Of these, 123.6 million, or 89%, sit in the dense layers, and 102.8 million in the first dense layer alone, which connects 7×7×512=25,0887 \times 7 \times 512 = 25{,}0887×7×512=25,088 values to 4096 units. Training one network took two to three weeks on four GPUs. VGG placed second in the classification task of ILSVRC 2014, with a top-5 test error of 7.3% for an ensemble of seven networks, and first in localization. After the competition, an ensemble of two networks reached 6.8%, and the best single network 7.0%. The simple, uniform design made VGG a popular feature extractor for years.

GoogLeNet and the Inception Module¶

GoogLeNet, by Christian Szegedy and colleagues at Google, won the classification task of ILSVRC 2014. Its name is a homage to LeNet. The task had the same format as in 2012: 1000 classes, about 1.2 million training images, 50,000 validation images, and 100,000 test images. The challenge with 200 object classes is the separate detection task. GoogLeNet was designed for efficiency, with a budget of 1.5 billion multiply-adds per image at inference.

Its building block is the inception module in Figure 6. It applies four branches in parallel to the same input: a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution; a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution followed by a 3×33 \times 33×3 convolution; a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution followed by a 5×55 \times 55×5 convolution; and 3×33 \times 33×3 max pooling followed by a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution. The outputs are concatenated along the channel dimension. The module looks at its input at several scales at once, and the following layers learn which scale matters, so the designer no longer has to choose one kernel size per layer. Stacking the module repeatedly creates many paths through different sequences of these basic convolutions. The 1×11 \times 11×1 convolutions in front of the 3×33 \times 33×3 and 5×55 \times 55×5 convolutions reduce the number of channels and with it the cost, the same idea as in the 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution example of the previous subsection.

Example

The first inception module of GoogLeNet receives 28×28×19228 \times 28 \times 19228×28×192 values, and its 5×55 \times 55×5 branch produces 32 channels. Applied directly, the 5×55 \times 55×5 convolution needs 5⋅5⋅192⋅32=153,6005 \cdot 5 \cdot 192 \cdot 32 = 153{,}6005⋅5⋅192⋅32=153,600 weights and 153,600⋅28⋅28=120.4153{,}600 \cdot 28 \cdot 28 = 120.4153,600⋅28⋅28=120.4 million multiply-adds. With a 1×11 \times 11×1 reduction to 16 channels first, it needs 192⋅16+5⋅5⋅16⋅32=15,872192 \cdot 16 + 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 16 \cdot 32 = 15{,}872192⋅16+5⋅5⋅16⋅32=15,872 weights and 12.4 million multiply-adds, almost ten times less.

The full network in Figure 7 has three parts. The stem takes the 224×224×3224 \times 224 \times 3224×224×3 image and applies a 7×77 \times 77×7 convolution with stride 2, max pooling, a 1×11 \times 11×1 and a 3×33 \times 33×3 convolution, and max pooling again, and delivers 28×28×19228 \times 28 \times 19228×28×192 values. The body stacks nine inception modules, with max pooling between groups of modules. The head averages each of the final 7×77 \times 77×7 maps into one value, which gives 1024 values, applies dropout of 40%, and classifies with one linear layer and softmax. Replacing dense layers by this average pooling improved the top-1 accuracy by about 0.6%. The network is 22 layers deep when counting layers with parameters, and 27 layers deep when counting pooling as well. The layer table of the paper adds up to about 6.8 million parameters, roughly a ninth of AlexNet; the text of the paper speaks of twelve times fewer.

Two auxiliary classifiers sit on top of intermediate modules, the third and the sixth of the nine. Each is a small network with average pooling, a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution with 128 filters, a dense layer with 1024 units, dropout of 70%, and a softmax over the 1000 classes. During training, their losses are added to the total loss with a weight of 0.3, to strengthen the gradient signal that reaches the lower layers and to regularize the network. At inference, they are discarded. Later networks with batch normalization and residual connections train without them. An ensemble of seven GoogLeNets, each evaluated on 144 crops per image, reached a top-5 error of 6.67%; a single network on a single crop reached 10.07%.

ResNet¶

Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren, and Jian Sun at Microsoft Research addressed a puzzle. On CIFAR-10, a plain network with 56 layers had a higher training error, and thus a higher test error, than a network with 20 layers. This degradation is not overfitting, since the training error itself gets worse. A deeper network could in principle copy the shallower one and turn its extra layers into the identity, but the training procedure does not find this solution.

The residual block makes the identity easy. Instead of learning the desired mapping H(x)H(\mathbf{x})H(x) directly, the block learns the residual F(x)=H(x)−xF(\mathbf{x}) = H(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{x}F(x)=H(x)−x and adds its input back:

y=F(x)+x\mathbf{y} = F(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}y=F(x)+x
(1)

If the identity is the best mapping, the block only has to push FFF toward zero. The shortcut adds neither parameters nor computation, and during backpropagation the gradient flows back through the addition unchanged, so the early layers keep receiving a useful signal.

The basic block on the left of Figure 8 has two 3×33 \times 33×3 convolutions, each followed by batch normalization, a ReLU between them, and a final ReLU after the addition. The addition requires that F(x)F(\mathbf{x})F(x) and x\mathbf{x}x have the same shape. When a block halves the resolution with stride 2 and doubles the number of channels, the shortcut applies a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution with stride 2, shown in the middle, to bring x\mathbf{x}x to the new shape. The right part shows ResNet-18 as drawn in Dive into Deep Learning: a 7×77 \times 77×7 convolution with stride 2, batch normalization, 3×33 \times 33×3 max pooling, four modules of two basic blocks each, global average pooling, and one dense layer, 11.7 million parameters in total.

Deeper ResNets use bottleneck blocks: a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution reduces the channels, for example from 256 to 64, a 3×33 \times 33×3 convolution works on the reduced channels, and a second 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution restores the 256 channels. ResNet-152 stacks 3, 8, 36, and 3 bottleneck blocks in four stages. Despite its 152 layers, it needs 11.3 billion multiply-adds per image, fewer than VGG-16 with 15.3 billion and VGG-19 with 19.6 billion. Counting its layers gives 60.2 million parameters; ResNet-50 has 25.6 million.

A single ResNet-152 reached a top-5 validation error of 4.49%, and an ensemble a top-5 test error of 3.57%, which won the classification task of ILSVRC 2015. ResNets also won ImageNet detection and localization and the COCO detection and segmentation tasks of that year. On CIFAR-10, the authors even trained a network with 1202 layers without optimization difficulty. Its test error of 7.93% was worse than the 6.43% of a 110-layer network, which the authors attribute to overfitting: 19.4 million parameters are too many for this small dataset.

DenseNet¶

Gao Huang, Zhuang Liu, Laurens van der Maaten, and Kilian Weinberger took shortcuts one step further. Inside a dense block, every layer receives the feature maps of all preceding layers, concatenated along the channel dimension, and passes its own maps to all following layers. A block with LLL layers thus has L(L+1)/2L(L+1)/2L(L+1)/2 direct connections instead of LLL. In a ResNet, layer ℓ\ellℓ adds its input to its output; in a DenseNet, it concatenates all earlier outputs:

ResNet: xℓ=Hℓ(xℓ−1)+xℓ−1DenseNet: xℓ=Hℓ([x0,x1,…,xℓ−1])\text{ResNet: } \mathbf{x}_\ell = H_\ell(\mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}) + \mathbf{x}_{\ell-1} \qquad \text{DenseNet: } \mathbf{x}_\ell = H_\ell\bigl([\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{\ell-1}]\bigr)ResNet: xℓ​=Hℓ​(xℓ−1​)+xℓ−1​DenseNet: xℓ​=Hℓ​([x0​,x1​,…,xℓ−1​])
(2)

Concatenation keeps the earlier features unchanged, so later layers can reuse them directly. Each layer adds only kkk new feature maps, the growth rate; the ImageNet models use k=32k = 32k=32. Layer ℓ\ellℓ of a block therefore receives k0+k(ℓ−1)k_0 + k(\ell-1)k0​+k(ℓ−1) maps, where k0k_0k0​ is the number of channels entering the block. Because this number keeps growing, each layer first reduces its input with a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution to 4k4k4k channels before its 3×33 \times 33×3 convolution. Between dense blocks, transition layers with batch normalization, a 1×11 \times 11×1 convolution, and 2×22 \times 22×2 average pooling with stride 2 halve the resolution and, with a compression factor of 0.5, also the number of channels.

Example

DenseNet-121 has four dense blocks with 6, 12, 24, and 16 layers. The first block starts with 64 channels and ends with 64+6⋅32=25664 + 6 \cdot 32 = 25664+6⋅32=256, which the transition halves to 128. The second block grows them to 128+12⋅32=512128 + 12 \cdot 32 = 512128+12⋅32=512, halved to 256; the third to 256+24⋅32=1024256 + 24 \cdot 32 = 1024256+24⋅32=1024, halved to 512; and the fourth to 512+16⋅32=1024512 + 16 \cdot 32 = 1024512+16⋅32=1024. Global average pooling turns these 1024 maps into the input of the final dense layer. Counting all layers gives 8.0 million parameters.

DenseNets reach the accuracy of ResNets with fewer parameters: a DenseNet-201 with 20 million parameters has about the same validation error as a ResNet-101 with more than 40 million.

Vision Transformer¶

Alexey Dosovitskiy and colleagues at Google Research removed the convolutions altogether. Figure 9 shows their Vision Transformer (ViT). It cuts the image into square patches of P×PP \times PP×P pixels; with P=16P = 16P=16, a 224×224224 \times 224224×224 image yields 2242/162=196224^2 / 16^2 = 1962242/162=196 patches. Each patch is flattened into 16⋅16⋅3=76816 \cdot 16 \cdot 3 = 76816⋅16⋅3=768 values and mapped by a trainable linear projection to a patch embedding with DDD dimensions. As in BERT, a learnable [class] embedding is placed in front of the sequence, and learnable position embeddings are added so that the model knows where each patch came from. The sequence passes through LLL transformer encoder layers. Each layer applies layer normalization, multi-head self-attention, layer normalization again, and a two-layer MLP, with a residual connection around the attention and around the MLP. The output state of the [class] token represents the image, and a classification head on top of it predicts the class. Transformer Embeddings explains self-attention and the encoder for text; the Vision Transformer uses them unchanged.

The paper defines three model sizes. A name such as ViT-L/16 denotes the Large model with patches of 16 by 16 pixels. Smaller patches give longer sequences, since the sequence length is inversely proportional to P2P^2P2, and thus more computation.

A convolution builds two assumptions about images into the network: nearby pixels belong together, and a pattern means the same wherever it appears. The Vision Transformer has far less of this image-specific bias. Apart from cutting the image into patches, it must learn spatial relations from data. Trained on ImageNet alone, with 1.3 million images, ViT models end a few percentage points below ResNets of comparable size. Pretrained on ImageNet-21k with 14 million images, or on Google’s internal JFT-300M with 303 million images, they match or beat the best convolutional networks: ViT-H/14 pretrained on JFT-300M reaches 88.55% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet, ViT-L/16 87.76%, and ViT-L/16 pretrained on ImageNet-21k 85.30%.

The right part of Figure 9 shows where the [class] token attends. Its attention concentrates on the dog, the airplane, and the snake, the regions that matter for the class, although the model never received the location of an object. Vision Transformers are also the image encoders of many multimodal models, such as the CLIP models of Multimodal Transformers.
