Summary

Key Takeaways¶

  1. Naive Bayes applies Bayes’ rule with the assumption that the features are independent given the class. The decision sums the log prior and the log likelihoods of the features, learning reduces to counting, and Laplace smoothing keeps unseen values from vetoing a class. Its decisions are often right even though its probabilities are too extreme.

  2. A decision tree classifies with a sequence of tests on single features. ID3 chooses the test with the largest information gain, C4.5 adds thresholds for continuous features and the gain ratio against features with many values, and pruning on a validation set keeps the tree from overfitting.

  3. Single trees have a high variance. Random forests average many randomized trees; gradient boosting adds small trees one at a time, each fitted to the negative gradient of the loss (for the squared error, the residuals of the current ensemble) and added as a correction, and is among the most accurate methods for tabular data, including the learning-to-rank models of web search.

  4. Logistic regression models the log-odds as a linear function and is trained with the cross-entropy loss; its gradient is the prediction minus the target, times the input. It is the discriminative counterpart of Naive Bayes.

  5. The support vector machine chooses the hyperplane with the largest margin; only the support vectors determine it, and the soft margin trades width against violations with the hyperparameter CCC. The kernel trick computes dot products in a high-dimensional feature space without ever constructing it.

  6. k-means minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares with Lloyd’s algorithm and finds a local optimum that depends on the initialization; k-means++ chooses good starting centroids. Expectation maximization fits Gaussian mixtures with soft assignments, and k-means is its hard-assignment special case.

  7. The number of clusters is a capacity parameter: the elbow method and the silhouette score choose it without labels.

  8. A hidden Markov model describes a sequence of hidden states that follows a Markov chain and emits one observation per step. The forward algorithm computes the probability of the observations, the Viterbi algorithm the most likely state sequence, both in N2TN^2 TN2T time, and Baum-Welch learns the parameters with expectation maximization.

Further Reading¶

Textbooks¶

  • Bishop, C. M. (2006). Pattern recognition and machine learning. Springer. Book page with free PDF. Chapter 4 covers logistic regression, chapter 7 support vector machines, chapter 9 k-means, mixtures, and expectation maximization, and chapter 13 hidden Markov models.

  • Hastie, T., Tibshirani, R., & Friedman, J. (2009). The elements of statistical learning: Data mining, inference, and prediction (2nd ed.). Springer. Book page with free PDF. Trees, boosting, random forests, logistic regression, and support vector machines from a statistical perspective.

Naive Bayes¶

  • Manning, C. D., Raghavan, P., & Schütze, H. (2008). Introduction to information retrieval, Chapter 13: Text classification and Naive Bayes. Cambridge University Press. Read online. The multinomial and Bernoulli models for text, with smoothing and feature selection by mutual information and chi-square.

  • Ng, A. Y., & Jordan, M. I. (2002). On discriminative vs. generative classifiers: A comparison of logistic regression and naive Bayes. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 14. NeurIPS proceedings. Shows that Naive Bayes approaches its best accuracy with fewer examples, while logistic regression wins with many.

  • Niculescu-Mizil, A., & Caruana, R. (2005). Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning. Proceedings of the 22nd International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). PDF. Measures how well classifiers are calibrated and shows that Naive Bayes pushes its probabilities toward 0 and 1.

Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting¶

  • Quinlan, J. R. (1986). Induction of decision trees. Machine Learning, 1(1), 81-106. PDF. The ID3 algorithm with the information gain and the weather example.

  • Wu, X., Kumar, V., Quinlan, J. R., et al. (2008). Top 10 algorithms in data mining. Knowledge and Information Systems, 14, 1-37. PDF. A survey that ranks C4.5 first and describes it together with k-means, SVM, EM, and Naive Bayes.

  • Breiman, L. (2001). Random forests. Machine Learning, 45, 5-32. PDF. Bootstrap samples and random feature subsets for ensembles of trees.

  • Friedman, J. H. (2001). Greedy function approximation: A gradient boosting machine. The Annals of Statistics, 29(5), 1189-1232. JSTOR. The general gradient boosting framework with arbitrary differentiable losses.

  • Natekin, A., & Knoll, A. (2013). Gradient boosting machines, a tutorial. Frontiers in Neurorobotics, 7, 21. PubMed Central. A readable introduction to gradient boosting with examples and practical advice.

  • Chen, T., & Guestrin, C. (2016). XGBoost: A scalable tree boosting system. Proceedings of the 22nd ACM SIGKDD International Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining. arXiv. The widely used library and the regularized objective behind it.

  • Burges, C. J. C. (2010). From RankNet to LambdaRank to LambdaMART: An overview. Microsoft Research Technical Report MSR-TR-2010-82. Microsoft Research. How gradient-boosted trees learn rankings, the model behind the learning-to-rank systems of web search.

Linear Classifiers¶

  • Burges, C. J. C. (1998). A tutorial on support vector machines for pattern recognition. Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, 2, 121-167. Microsoft Research. The margin, the dual problem, and kernels, derived step by step.

  • Schölkopf, B., & Smola, A. J. (2002). Learning with kernels. MIT Press. MIT Press. The standard textbook on support vector machines and kernel methods.

Clustering¶

  • MacQueen, J. (1967). Some methods for classification and analysis of multivariate observations. Proceedings of the Fifth Berkeley Symposium on Mathematical Statistics and Probability, 1, 281-297. Project Euclid. The paper that named k-means.

  • Arthur, D., & Vassilvitskii, S. (2007). k-means++: The advantages of careful seeding. Proceedings of the 18th ACM-SIAM Symposium on Discrete Algorithms (SODA), 1027-1035. PDF. The seeding with probabilities proportional to the squared distance.

  • Dempster, A. P., Laird, N. M., & Rubin, D. B. (1977). Maximum likelihood from incomplete data via the EM algorithm. Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series B, 39(1), 1-38. PDF. The general EM algorithm and the proof that each iteration does not decrease the likelihood.

  • Rousseeuw, P. J. (1987). Silhouettes: A graphical aid to the interpretation and validation of cluster analysis. Journal of Computational and Applied Mathematics, 20, 53-65. ScienceDirect. The silhouette score for choosing the number of clusters.

Hidden Markov Models¶

  • Rabiner, L. R. (1989). A tutorial on hidden Markov models and selected applications in speech recognition. Proceedings of the IEEE, 77(2), 257-286. PDF. The classic introduction to the three problems, the forward-backward algorithm, Viterbi, and Baum-Welch.

  • Jurafsky, D., & Martin, J. H. Speech and language processing (3rd ed. draft), Appendix A: Hidden Markov models. PDF. A modern treatment with part-of-speech tagging as the running example.
