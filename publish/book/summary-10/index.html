All representations start from the same time-frequency picture and differ in what they keep. Perceptual features and MFCCs condense each frame and segment into a few numbers that model how the ear hears; musical features add the structure that listeners recognize in music. Classifiers turn features into labels, first with hand-crafted features and small trees for two classes, then with networks that learn their features for hundreds of classes, and finally with CLAP, which labels and searches with free text. Transcription is the special case for speech: it turns audio into the richest description of all, text, but its errors fall on the names that users search for. Fingerprinting keeps the least, a sparse set of peak pairs, and is the only representation that names one exact recording under heavy distortion. The learned models did not make the hand-crafted ones obsolete: cost, explainability, small devices, and exact identification still favor them.

Key Takeaways¶

  1. The spectrogram is the common basis of audio features. The frequency resolution of a frame is Δf=1/T\Delta f = 1/TΔf=1/T, so a finer frequency grid always costs time resolution, and the sampling rate does not change it. The task decides the frame length.

  2. Perceptual features measure loudness, brightness, noisiness, and change per frame; segment statistics capture how these values vary. LSTER and HZCRR separate speech from music where the mean ZCR does not.

  3. MFCCs model the ear in three steps: the mel filter bank follows its frequency resolution, the logarithm its loudness perception, and the cosine transform keeps the smooth spectral envelope in 13 coefficients, extended by their changes to 39 values.

  4. Chroma describes pitch classes and the key independent of the octave; tempo comes from the periodicity of the onset strength, with octave errors as the typical failure.

  5. Hand-crafted features with a small tree solve a two-class problem cheaply and transparently. Pretrained networks recognize hundreds of classes, and CLAP assigns labels and answers text queries without a classifier trained for them, but only among the prompts it is given. Classifiers must be evaluated on held-out recordings.

  6. Transcription makes speech searchable as text. Weakly supervised encoder-decoder models such as Whisper and LLM-based recognizers transcribe many languages, but quality follows the amount of training data per language, dialects become translations, and names and rare terms fail most often. Hotwords at indexing time and phonetic matching at query time address these errors.

  7. A fingerprint pairs spectrogram peaks into hashes, looks them up in an inverted file, and accepts a recording only if many matches agree on one time offset. It identifies recordings under heavy noise, but not songs in other performances.

Key Formulas¶

Key Formula: Frequency Resolution of the STFT
Δf=fsN=1T\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{T}Δf=Nfs​​=T1​
(1)

A frame of NNN samples, lasting TTT seconds, resolves frequencies 1/T1/T1/T apart: 25 Hz for 40 ms, at any sampling rate.

Key Formula: Mel Scale
mel(f)=1125⋅ln⁡(1+f700)\text{mel}(f) = 1125 \cdot \ln\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right)mel(f)=1125⋅ln(1+700f​)
(2)

Equal steps in mel sound like equal steps in pitch; the bands of the mel filter bank are equally spaced on this scale and widen with frequency.

Key Formula: Word Error Rate
WER=S+D+IN\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}WER=NS+D+I​
(3)

Substitutions, deletions, and insertions in the best alignment with the reference, divided by the number of reference words; the CER does the same on characters.

Exam focus

  • The time-frequency trade-off: computing NNN and Δf\Delta fΔf for a frame, deciding whether neighboring notes can be separated, and why a higher sampling rate does not help.

  • Why LSTER and HZCRR separate speech from music although the mean ZCR does not, and why LSTER uses the linear energy.

  • The steps of the MFCC computation and the purpose of each: window, mel filter bank, logarithm, cosine transform, derivatives.

  • Hand-crafted tree versus pretrained classifier versus CLAP: labels, training data, cost, explainability, and the limits of zero-shot labels; evaluation on held-out recordings.

  • Computing WER and CER, why a low WER can still miss the names users search for, and the difference between hotwords and phonetic matching.

  • Why fingerprints use peak pairs and an offset histogram, and which changes of a recording break them.

Self-Check Questions¶

  1. (Apply) A recording is sampled at 44.1 kHz and analyzed with frames of 50 ms. How many samples does a frame hold, what is the frequency resolution, and can it separate A4 (440 Hz) from A♯4 (466.16 Hz)? What changes if the same recording is resampled to 16 kHz?

  2. (Understand) The Brahms clip has a higher mean zero-crossing rate than the speech clip, but a lower HZCRR. Explain how both can be true, and why LSTER is computed on the linear energy rather than on decibels.

  3. (Understand) Explain what the logarithm and the cosine transform contribute to MFCCs, and why dropping the coefficients above 13 removes most of the pitch information.

  4. (Analyze) A tempo estimator reports 184.6 bpm for a loop recorded at 90 bpm. Explain the error with the onset autocorrelation, and describe one way to avoid it.

  5. (Apply) The reference transcript is “the mayor of Uster spoke” and the recognizer writes “the mayor of oyster spoke today”. Compute the WER, and explain why a search for “Uster” fails although four of the five reference words are correct.

  6. (Evaluate) A radio archive wants to label its recordings as speech or music. Compare the decision tree, AST, and CLAP for a recorder with a small processor that labels the live stream, and for the archive server that labels 50 years of recordings.

  7. (Evaluate) A music publisher wants to find every broadcast in which one of its songs was played, including live versions and covers. Can audio fingerprinting do this? Which other representation of this chapter would you combine it with, and why?

Further Reading¶

  • Davis, S. B., & Mermelstein, P. (1980). Comparison of parametric representations for monosyllabic word recognition in continuously spoken sentences. IEEE Transactions on Acoustics, Speech, and Signal Processing, 28(4), 357-366. IEEE Xplore. The paper that introduced MFCCs and showed that they outperform the other speech features of the time.

  • Lu, L., Zhang, H.-J., & Jiang, H. (2002). Content analysis for audio classification and segmentation. IEEE Transactions on Speech and Audio Processing, 10(7), 504-516. PDF (IEEE Xplore). Classifies and segments audio streams into speech, music, environmental sound, and silence; defines HZCRR and LSTER with the thresholds of 1.5 times and half the mean over 1-second windows.

  • Ellis, D. P. W. (2007). Beat tracking by dynamic programming. Journal of New Music Research, 36(1), 51-60. PDF. Onset strength, tempo estimation with a tempo prior, and the dynamic program behind the beats of Figure 3.

  • Müller, M. (2021). Fundamentals of music processing (2nd ed.). Springer. FMP notebooks. A textbook on chroma, tempo, beat tracking, and music retrieval, with free Python notebooks for every chapter.

  • Castán, D., Ortega, A., & Lleida, E. (2010). Speech/music classification by using the C4.5 decision tree algorithm. FALA 2010: VI Jornadas en Tecnología del Habla and II Iberian SLTech Workshop. Semantic Scholar. The six segment features and the C4.5 rule set of the speech/music classifier in Classifying Audio.

  • Gemmeke, J. F., Ellis, D. P. W., Freedman, D., Jansen, A., Lawrence, W., Moore, R. C., Plakal, M., & Ritter, M. (2017). Audio Set: An ontology and human-labeled dataset for audio events. IEEE International Conference on Acoustics, Speech and Signal Processing, 776-780. PDF. The dataset of about two million labeled YouTube clips on which the pretrained classifiers of Classifying Audio are trained.

  • Kong, Q., Cao, Y., Iqbal, T., Wang, Y., Wang, W., & Plumbley, M. D. (2020). PANNs: Large-scale pretrained audio neural networks for audio pattern recognition. IEEE/ACM Transactions on Audio, Speech, and Language Processing, 28, 2880-2894. arXiv. Convolutional networks on log mel spectrograms, trained on AudioSet and transferred to other audio tasks.

  • Gong, Y., Chung, Y.-A., & Glass, J. (2021). AST: Audio Spectrogram Transformer. Interspeech 2021, 571-575. arXiv. The Vision Transformer applied to spectrogram patches; the classifier of the then-versus-now example.

  • Wu, Y., Chen, K., Zhang, T., Hui, Y., Nezhurina, M., Berg-Kirkpatrick, T., & Dubnov, S. (2023). Large-scale contrastive language-audio pretraining with feature fusion and keyword-to-caption augmentation. IEEE International Conference on Acoustics, Speech and Signal Processing. arXiv. The LAION CLAP model used for the zero-shot labels and text-to-audio search of Classifying Audio.

  • Radford, A., Kim, J. W., Xu, T., Brockman, G., McLeavey, C., & Sutskever, I. (2023). Robust speech recognition via large-scale weak supervision. International Conference on Machine Learning, 28492-28518. arXiv. Whisper: 680,000 hours of weakly supervised training, the multitask token format, and the relation between training hours and error rate per language.

  • Shi, X., et al. (2026). Qwen3-ASR technical report. arXiv. Open LLM-based recognizers for 52 languages and dialects with a separate forced aligner, and the observation that benchmark scores hide real-world differences.

  • Bian, C., et al. (2026). Qwen-Audio-3.0-ASR technical report. arXiv. A production LLM-based recognizer with hotword customization, long-audio context, and streaming; its successor Qwen-Audio-3.1-ASR (arXiv) adds speaker-diarized transcripts.

  • Bäckström, T., et al. (2022). Introduction to speech processing (2nd ed.). Aalto University. Online book. An open textbook on speech signals, features, recognition, and evaluation.

  • Wang, A. (2003). An industrial-strength audio search algorithm. International Conference on Music Information Retrieval. PDF. The Shazam paper: constellation maps, combinatorial hashing, and the offset histogram of Audio Fingerprinting.

  • MacLeod, C. (2022). abracadabra: How does Shazam work? Blog post. A readable walk through Wang’s algorithm with a working Python implementation.

  • Drevo, W. (2013). Audio fingerprinting. Blog post. Peak finding and pair hashing step by step, the source of the constellation-map figures in Audio Fingerprinting.

  • McFee, B., Raffel, C., Liang, D., Ellis, D. P. W., McVicar, M., Battenberg, E., & Nieto, O. (2015). librosa: Audio and music signal analysis in Python. Proceedings of the 14th Python in Science Conference, 18-25. SciPy proceedings. The Python library for spectrograms, MFCCs, chroma, onsets, and beats; documentation.
