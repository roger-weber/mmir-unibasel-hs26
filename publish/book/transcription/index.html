Transcription

A journalist searches the radio archive for the interview in which a guest mentioned a local politician by name. Labels such as “speech” narrow the search to the talk, but only the words themselves answer the question. Speech is the richest audio signal for retrieval because it can become text: once transcribed, an hour of broadcast enters the inverted index, ranks with BM25, and splits into passages for semantic search like any document. This section explains how transcription works, why many languages, dialects, and names remain hard, and how a search system copes with the errors. Transcription is the describe-then-search-text path for audio: a model writes a text description of the media, here the spoken words, and the text index does the rest (Multimodal Transformers).

The Classical Pipeline¶

Before 2010, and in many systems long after, speech recognition was a chain of separately trained components. Figure 1 shows its front end: the signal is windowed and transformed, and every 10 ms a vector of MFCCs, the 39 values that describe the spectral envelope of a frame and its changes, summarizes the current sound (Perceptual Features). An acoustic model maps sequences of these vectors to phonemes, the smallest sound units of a language, such as /m/, /aɪ/, or /ʃ/. Each phoneme was modeled by a hidden Markov model with a few states that the signal passes from left to right, which accommodates slower and faster speech. A pronunciation dictionary assembles phonemes into words, and a language model of word n-grams prefers likely word sequences: “recognize speech” over “wreck a nice beach”, which sounds almost the same. A search over all combinations returns the most probable transcript. The components needed expert work, and the dictionary limited the vocabulary: a name that was not in it could not be recognized at all. The hidden Markov models are described in Hidden Markov Models, and the recognizer built from them, with its training and its handling of speaking rate, speakers, and microphones, in Speech Recognition with HMMs.

Recurrent neural networks, which carry a state from one time step to the next, replaced the acoustic models in the 2010s. Transformers then replaced the whole chain with a single network that maps audio to text. How recurrent networks process sequences is described in Recurrent Networks.

Whisper¶

Whisper, released by OpenAI in 2022 (Radford et al., 2023), is an encoder-decoder transformer for speech recognition, shown in Figure 2. Its input is a log mel spectrogram: the audio is resampled to 16 kHz and cut into windows of 30 seconds, and each window becomes 80 mel bands computed from frames of 25 ms with a hop of 10 ms. The log mel spectrogram plays the role that resizing plays for images: it brings recordings with different sampling rates into one format and shrinks 480,000 samples per window to 3,000 frames of 80 values. The encoder turns these frames into a sequence of vectors. The decoder generates the transcript token by token, as a language model does, and attends to the encoder output at every step.

Whisper was trained on 680,000 hours of audio with transcripts collected from the web. The transcripts were not produced for training and contain errors. The authors call this weak supervision: a huge amount of imperfect data instead of a small amount of carefully transcribed speech. Of the 680,000 hours, 438,000 are English, 117,000 cover 96 other languages, and 125,000 pair speech in another language with an English translation. Special tokens at the start of the decoder input select the task: the language, transcription or translation into English, and whether to predict timestamps. The same model therefore identifies the language, transcribes, translates, and marks when each phrase was spoken. The models range from 39 million parameters (“tiny”) to 1.55 billion (“large”). The later version large-v3 uses 128 mel bands and was trained on 1 million hours of weakly labeled audio and 4 million hours labeled by an earlier Whisper model.

Whisper made accurate transcription available to everyone, but its decoder is a language model and shares the weakness of language models: on silence, music, or noise it sometimes produces fluent sentences that nobody said, or repeats a phrase many times. A transcript is a description of the audio, not the audio itself, and like every generated description it can contain things that are not there (Multimodal Transformers).

LLM-Based Speech Recognition¶

The newest recognizers connect an audio encoder to a large language model. The encoder turns the log mel spectrogram into vectors, a small adapter network projects them into the token embedding space of the language model, and the language model writes the transcript. Two things change compared with Whisper. The language model brings broad knowledge of which words and names are likely in a context. And it accepts text instructions next to the audio: a language hint, a list of names that are likely to occur, or the transcript of the previous minutes. Figure 3 compares this design with Whisper.

The Qwen3-ASR models (January 2026) are an open example. Built on the multimodal model Qwen3-Omni, the two models with 1.7 and 0.6 billion parameters identify the language and transcribe 52 languages and dialects, and they are released under the Apache 2.0 license. Word timestamps come from a separate model, a forced aligner: given the audio and its transcript, it determines when each word was spoken, for 11 languages. The Qwen-Audio-3.0-ASR system (September 2026) shows the direction of production systems: a mixture-of-experts language model trained on tens of millions of hours of speech, 30 languages and 16 Chinese dialects, customizable lists of hotwords, context over long recordings, and a streaming variant that transcribes while the speaker is still talking. Its successor, Qwen-Audio-3.1-ASR (October 2026), adds speaker diarization, a transcript that also says who spoke when, with speaker labels and timestamps.

The Many-Languages Challenge¶

“Supports 99 languages” sounds as if the problem were solved. It is not, for four reasons.

The long tail of training data. Of Whisper’s 117,000 non-English hours, a few large languages take most, and many languages have only a few hundred hours or less. The Whisper paper shows that the error rate of a language falls steadily with the amount of its training data. A claim of “N supported languages” therefore says little about the quality in any one of them. The Qwen3-ASR report adds that models with similar scores on public benchmarks can differ substantially on real-world recordings.

Language identification. A recognizer must first decide which language it hears. On a clip of a few seconds, this decision is unreliable, and it fails when speakers switch languages within a sentence, which is common in multilingual regions. A Swiss guest may speak Swiss German, quote a Standard German headline, and use English technical terms in a single answer.

Dialects without a standard spelling. Swiss German has no standard orthography, and its dialects differ from valley to valley. Recognizers therefore transcribe Swiss German into Standard German, which is in effect a translation: word order, vocabulary, and grammatical forms change. Whether a transcript is correct then depends on which of several valid Standard German renderings the reference chose.

The error measure. Transcription quality is measured by comparing the output, the hypothesis, with a reference transcript. The two word sequences are aligned with the minimal number of edits: the edit distance, which counts the insertions, deletions, and substitutions needed to turn one sequence into the other, applied to words instead of letters (Query Understanding).

Key Formula: Word Error Rate
WER=S+D+IN\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}WER=NS+D+I​
(1)

SSS, DDD, and III are the numbers of substituted, deleted, and inserted words in the best alignment of the hypothesis with the reference, and NNN is the number of words in the reference. The character error rate (CER) applies the same formula to characters.

The WER can exceed 100% when the hypothesis contains many insertions. Languages written without spaces between words, such as Chinese and Japanese, have no reliable word boundaries, so their results are reported as CER.

Example

A guest says in Swiss German: “Mir händ geschter mit em Gmeindspräsident Hürlimaa gredt.” The reference transcript renders it in Standard German as “Wir haben gestern mit dem Gemeindepräsidenten Hürlimann gesprochen” (N=8N = 8N=8 words). A recognizer writes “Wir haben gestern mit dem Gemeinde Präsidenten Hürliman geredet”.

The alignment finds three substitutions: “Gemeindepräsidenten” becomes “Gemeinde”, “Hürlimann” becomes “Hürliman”, and “gesprochen” becomes “geredet”. The extra word “Präsidenten” is one insertion. The WER is (3+0+1)/8=50%(3 + 0 + 1)/8 = 50\%(3+0+1)/8=50%. On characters, the same pair has 4 substitutions, 4 deletions, and 1 insertion over 66 reference characters, a CER of 9/66=13.6%9/66 = 13.6\%9/66=13.6%.

A reader understands the hypothesis perfectly: “geredet” is an equally valid rendering of “gredt”, and the split compound only changes the spelling. Yet half the words count as errors. The one error that matters for retrieval, the missing “n” in the name, counts no more than the harmless ones.

Searching Transcripts¶

A transcript with timestamps turns audio search into text search with a pointer into the recording. Each passage of the transcript is indexed together with its start time, and a hit leads the user to the moment in the broadcast, not only to the file. Speaker labels from diarization add a facet: only the passages of the guest, not those of the presenter.

The errors of a recognizer are not spread evenly. Frequent words are recognized well because the training data contains them thousands of times. Names of people, places, and products, and rare technical terms, are recognized worst, because the model has seen them rarely or never and falls back to a similar-sounding common word or spelling. These are exactly the words users search for: the journalist types “Hürlimann”, not “gestern”. A transcript with a low WER can still miss most of the names. There are two answers, one at indexing time and one at query time.

Hotwords at indexing time. If the system knows which names are likely, it can tell the recognizer before transcribing. A radio archive knows the names of its presenters, the local politicians, and the places of its region. LLM-based recognizers accept such a list as text context, and Qwen-Audio-3.0-ASR offers it as a feature called hotword customization. Whisper can be given a prompt with the expected spellings, which biases its decoder in the same direction. The cost is that the list must be maintained, and a name that is not on it gets no help.

Phonetic matching at query time. If the spelling in the transcript is wrong but the sound is right, the search can compare pronunciations instead of letters. The names Meier, Mayer, Maier, and Meyer are pronounced the same in German, and a recognizer can only guess which one was meant. Phonetic codes such as Soundex reduce a word to a short code of its rough pronunciation, so that such variants share one code. A finer variant indexes phoneme n-grams: the recognizer outputs, or a pronunciation dictionary derives, the phoneme sequence of the transcript, and overlapping sequences of three phonemes are indexed like the character n-grams of text. A query is converted into phonemes in the same way, and passages that share many phoneme trigrams with the query rank high, even if the spelling differs or the recognizer split a name into two words. Some systems also index the alternative hypotheses of the recognizer, not only the best one, to raise the recall for uncertain words. Phonetic codes and edit distances for the spelling variants of names are covered in Query Understanding.

A low word error rate does not guarantee findable names

The WER weighs every word equally, and most words in a transcript are frequent function words. For retrieval, what counts is whether the words that users search for, mostly names and rare terms, are transcribed correctly. Measure the recall of these query terms on a sample of the collection, not only the WER.
