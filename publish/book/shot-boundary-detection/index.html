Shot Boundary Detection

A viewer of “Tears of Steel”, the open film of the Blender Foundation that runs through this chapter, searches its subtitles for Celia’s line about her robot hand and finds it at 5:47. A result that starts playing at 5:47 drops the viewer into the middle of the scene, and a result that returns the whole film is useless. The natural edges of a result are the boundaries that the editor of the film already created: the cuts between shots. A shot is a continuous recording by one camera, so its frames share a place, a time, and a viewpoint, and this makes the shot the basic retrieval unit of video. This section shows how a system finds the shot boundaries without any help from the editor, by measuring how much consecutive frames differ.

Cuts and Transitions¶

Films join shots in two ways. A hard cut switches from the last frame of one shot to the first frame of the next. The two frames do not overlap, and the image changes abruptly from one frame to the next. Editors use hard cuts within a scene, for example to switch between the two speakers of a dialogue. A gradual transition, also called a soft cut, blends the two shots over several frames. In a fade, the old shot darkens to black and the new shot brightens from black; in a dissolve, the old shot fades out while the new shot fades in on top of it; in a wipe, a moving border pushes the old shot out of the frame. No single frame marks the boundary. A one-second dissolve at 24 frames per second spreads the change over 24 frames. Gradual transitions often signal a change of time or place, so they mark scene boundaries more often than hard cuts do.

The soundtrack is of little help. Music and background sound often continue across a cut, and a dialogue continues while the camera switches between the speakers. The boundary must be found in the images.

Figure 1 shows two dissolves in a row from “Tears of Steel”, 3:29.5 to 3:31.5, with the brightness histogram of every seventh frame. A dark scene with bright lights dissolves into a glowing orange grid, and the grid dissolves into a ruined street. The histograms change step by step: the peak in the dark bins flattens as the grid brightens the middle tones, and it returns as the dark street appears. The last histogram closely resembles the first, although the two frames show different shots.

The video file itself offers a hint. A video encoder stores most frames as differences to neighboring frames and only some frames as complete images, the I-frames. Encoders insert I-frames at regular intervals, so that a player can jump to any position, and many encoders insert an extra I-frame when they detect a scene change, because a difference to the previous frame would cost more than a complete image. I-frames are therefore cheap candidates for shot boundaries, but not the boundaries themselves: most I-frames lie inside shots, and the encoder’s own detection is tuned for compression, not for retrieval.

Measuring the Change Between Frames¶

The most direct measure compares the frames pixel by pixel. Let f(x,y,t)\mathbf{f}(x, y, t)f(x,y,t) be the vector of red, green, and blue values of the pixel at position (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) in frame ttt. The pixel difference sums the changes over all pixels:

The measure reacts to cuts, but it also reacts to everything else that moves. A person who walks through the frame, a camera that pans by a few pixels, or a zoom changes most pixel values while the shot continues. Within-shot distances then come close to the distances at cuts, and every threshold that finds most cuts also reports false ones. On the first three minutes of the film, the threshold that finds 30 of the 34 cuts reports six false cuts with the pixel difference and one with the histogram distance below.

A color histogram removes most of this problem. It divides the colors into a fixed set of bins, for example ranges of brightness, and records for each bin the share of the pixels whose color falls into it, regardless of where in the frame these pixels lie. When the camera pans or a person moves, the pixels keep their colors, so the histogram barely changes. When the shot changes, the distribution of colors usually changes too. A brightness histogram with a few dozen bins is often enough, because consecutive shots rarely have the same distribution of light and dark. We write h(t)\mathbf{h}(t)h(t) for the normalized histogram of frame ttt, whose entries sum to 1, and compare consecutive histograms with the L1 distance, the sum of the absolute differences of the bins.

Other histogram distances work as well. The color moments give an even more compact variant: the distance between the mean and standard deviation of the brightness in consecutive frames. Color histograms, the quadratic distance, and color moments are treated in depth in Color Features.

Histograms also have blind spots. Two shots of different persons in the same room and light can have nearly the same histogram, and the cut between them goes unnoticed. A camera flash, a lightning strike, or an explosion changes the histogram of a single frame completely and produces a cut where there is none.

Choosing the Threshold¶

A hard cut is declared when d(t)≥Td(t) \geq Td(t)≥T for a threshold TTT. Choosing TTT is a binary classification problem. The frame pairs within a shot form the negative class with distance distribution fnf_nfn​, and the frame pairs across a cut form the positive class with distribution fpf_pfp​. Because the two distributions overlap, every threshold trades missed cuts against false cuts: raising TTT removes false cuts and adds missed ones. To find a good threshold, we annotate the cuts in a sample of videos and compute the distances of all frame pairs. For every candidate threshold, the true positive rate is the share of the cuts that it detects, and the false positive rate is the share of the within-shot pairs that it reports as cuts. The ROC curve plots the two rates against each other, and we choose the threshold at the point that fits the application. Thresholds from score distributions and the ROC curve are treated in depth in Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves.

For retrieval, the two errors do not cost the same. A false cut splits one shot into two shorter ones. The index then holds a redundant keyframe, and a result may end a few seconds early, but nothing becomes unsearchable. A missed cut merges two shots into one. If the merged shot is represented by a keyframe of the first part, the content of the second part disappears from the index. Shot detectors for retrieval therefore usually favor a high true positive rate and accept some false cuts.

One threshold does not fit all videos

A threshold chosen on news broadcasts fails on music videos, and a threshold chosen on bright sports footage fails on a dark thriller. The distance distributions depend on the genre, the lighting, and the editing style. Either choose the threshold on a sample of the target collection, or use an adaptive threshold that compares d(t)d(t)d(t) with the average distance of the surrounding frames, so that a cut must stand out from its local neighborhood.

Gradual Transitions and Twin Thresholds¶

A gradual transition spreads the change over many frames. Suppose the cut from day to night of the example above is a dissolve of five frames, and, to keep the numbers simple, the histogram moves in five equal steps from h(t)\mathbf{h}(t)h(t) to the night histogram. Each step then has a distance of 1.06/5=0.2121.06 / 5 = 0.2121.06/5=0.212. With a cut threshold of Tc=0.8T_c = 0.8Tc​=0.8, no single step comes close. Lowering the threshold to 0.2 would detect the dissolve, but it would also turn every larger movement within a shot into a cut.

One way out is to model the transition effects. A fade passes through black frames, and a wipe divides the frame into an old and a new region separated by a moving border. Detectors for such effects work well, but every new effect requires a new model, and editing software offers many of them.

Twin thresholding, introduced by Zhang, Kankanhalli, and Smoliar in 1993 as the twin-comparison method, needs no model of the effect. It adds a second, lower threshold TsT_sTs​ that marks the possible start of a gradual transition, and compares frames over a longer distance:

  1. If d(t)≥Tcd(t) \geq T_cd(t)≥Tc​, a hard cut lies between frames t−1t-1t−1 and ttt.

  2. If Ts≤d(t)<TcT_s \leq d(t) < T_cTs​≤d(t)<Tc​, a gradual transition may start. Frame r=t−1r = t - 1r=t−1, the last frame before the change, becomes the reference frame.

  3. As long as the consecutive distances stay at or above TsT_sTs​, the content keeps changing, and we follow it frame by frame.

  4. When the consecutive distance falls below TsT_sTs​ at frame e+1e + 1e+1, the change has stopped at frame eee. We compare the end frame with the reference frame.

Each step of a gradual transition is small, but the frames before and after the transition differ as much as the two sides of a hard cut. If the end frame is still close to the reference frame, the change was not a shot boundary.

The method then reports a soft cut in the middle of a shot. A practical detector therefore checks whether a gradual change comes from camera motion, using the motion estimation of Motion Detection.

Keyframes¶

Once the shots are known, each shot needs one or more keyframes that represent it in the index. The keyframes are the input for image features, from color histograms to the embeddings of an image encoder, for the text-to-video search of Video Understanding with Transformers, and for the thumbnails that a result list or a storyboard shows to the user. Three simple choices are common. The first frame of a shot is easy to find but may still belong to a transition. The middle frame avoids the transitions and works well for short shots. The most representative frame is the one whose histogram lies closest to the mean histogram of all frames in the shot.

A single keyframe is enough for a static shot, but not for a long shot in which the camera pans across a street or a person enters the room. Clustering the frame histograms of such a shot, for example with kkk-means, and taking the frame closest to each cluster center yields one keyframe per visually distinct part of the shot. An alternative is to sample frames at a fixed rate, for example one per second, and to combine their features, which Video Understanding with Transformers does for embeddings.

Then vs. Now: Learned Shot Detection¶

Histogram distances with twin thresholds remain a common baseline. They run in real time on any processor, every decision can be explained by a distance and a threshold, and open-source tools such as PySceneDetect offer threshold-based detectors of this kind. Their weaknesses are the ones discussed above: flashes and fast motion produce false cuts, similar shots hide true cuts, and unusual transitions escape both thresholds.

Learned detectors replace the hand-tuned thresholds with a network trained on annotated videos. TransNet V2, published by Souček and Lokoč in 2020, downscales every frame to 48 by 27 pixels, processes windows of 100 consecutive frames with convolutions over space and time, and outputs for each frame the probability that a transition takes place. Like other recent detectors, it is trained on annotated videos and on transitions that are created artificially by joining real shots. Because the network sees a whole window of frames, it can learn that a flash lasts a single frame and that a pan moves the content instead of replacing it. In the authors’ evaluation, TransNet V2 reaches F1 scores of 96.2% on the BBC Planet Earth documentaries, 93.9% on the RAI broadcasts, and 77.9% on ClipShots, a collection of user-generated videos with many difficult transitions.

We compared both detectors on “Tears of Steel”. The histograms have 32 brightness bins and are computed on frames reduced to 320 by 134 pixels. The first three minutes of the film, with 34 annotated cuts and one fade, serve to choose the thresholds. The ROC curve of this excerpt has an area of 0.987, and Tc=0.30T_c = 0.30Tc​=0.30 is the largest threshold that still finds 30 of the 34 cuts, with a single false cut among 4,235 frame pairs within shots. The four missed cuts lead into title cards with small text on black, whose histograms differ too little from the black frames around them. Ts=0.08T_s = 0.08Ts​=0.08 lies at the 99th percentile of the distances within shots. TransNet V2 reports a transition wherever its probability exceeds 0.5. The next three minutes, from 3:00 to 6:00, are the test excerpt. We annotated the transitions of both excerpts from contact sheets of the decoded frames: strips of eight frames around every candidate of both detectors, and for the test excerpt also three frames per second to catch misses. The test excerpt holds 39 hard cuts and 2 dissolves.

Of the 17 false detections of the twin thresholds, 16 come from light effects inside shots, the weakness named above for flashes: 7 from a bright glow that follows the cut at 3:35.5, 7 from a hologram that flickers every second frame at 5:08, and 2 false gradual transitions from a flicker at 3:43. Neither method finds the two dissolves of Figure 1, from 3:29.5 to 3:31.5. Inside these dissolves, the distance between consecutive frames swings between 0.04 and 0.12, around TsT_sTs​. The twin thresholds therefore end each candidate after a few frames and compare only a fragment of the transition with the reference frame. TransNet V2 never marks more than a single frame as a transition anywhere in the film. Gradual transitions remain the hard case for both generations.

The learned detector costs more computation, but the tiny input of 48 by 27 pixels per frame keeps it fast enough for large video archives, the use case its authors designed it for. On a processor without a graphics card, it processed the 17,620 frames of the film in 214 seconds, about 82 frames per second, more than three times faster than the film plays. Over the whole film, it finds 139 transitions and thus 140 shots, while the twin thresholds report 190 hard cuts and 43 gradual transitions. The learned detector does not remove the need for a threshold: the per-frame probabilities still have to be turned into decisions, and the trade-off between missed and false cuts remains the one of the histogram threshold.
