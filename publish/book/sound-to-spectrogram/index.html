From Sound to Spectrogram

A radio station that archives its broadcasts stores one hour as 172.8 million numbers per channel. Each number is the air pressure at the microphone at one moment, measured 48,000 times per second. Two recordings follow us through the first half of this chapter: a speech clip, 14.8 seconds of a LibriVox audiobook reading of The Ashiel Mystery, and a music clip, 45.8 seconds of Brahms’s Hungarian Dance No. 5 played by a string orchestra. Both are public recordings, stored with 22,050 samples per second. Played back, the first is clearly a man reading and the second clearly violins. As lists of numbers, they look alike: values that swing up and down around zero.

This section turns such a list into a picture that shows which frequencies sound at which moment. Almost every feature in the rest of the chapter, the input of modern speech recognizers, and the audio fingerprint are computed from this picture, the spectrogram.

Sound as a Signal¶

Sound is a pressure wave. A vibrating string or a loudspeaker membrane pushes the air molecules back and forth, and regions of higher and lower pressure travel outwards at about 343 meters per second. The number of oscillations per second is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). Humans hear frequencies between about 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz. Figure 1 shows how a microphone records such a wave: the amplitude, that is, the deviation of the pressure from its resting value, over time.

The ear handles pressures across an enormous range. The quietest audible sound has a pressure amplitude of about 20 micropascals, while a sound that hurts has about 20 pascals, a million times more. Sound levels are therefore measured on a logarithmic scale, the sound pressure level in decibels (dB):

Sampling¶

A computer stores the continuous wave as a sequence of samples x(n)x(n)x(n), taken at a fixed sampling rate fsf_sfs​. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem says that a sampled signal can represent frequencies up to half the sampling rate:

(2)

To cover the full range of human hearing up to 20 kHz, audio CDs sample at 44,100 Hz (fmax=22,050f_\text{max} = 22{,}050fmax​=22,050 Hz), and video, DVDs, and broadcasting use 48,000 Hz (fmax=24,000f_\text{max} = 24{,}000fmax​=24,000 Hz). Speech needs less: the telephone transmits frequencies between 300 and 3400 Hz with 8,000 samples per second, and speech recognizers such as Whisper work with 16,000 samples per second. The two running clips use 22,050 Hz and represent frequencies up to 11,025 Hz.

Frequencies above fs/2f_s / 2fs​/2 do not disappear when a signal is sampled. They fold back into the range below fs/2f_s / 2fs​/2 and appear as tones that were never there: a 30 kHz component sampled at 48 kHz shows up at 48−30=1848 - 30 = 1848−30=18 kHz. This effect is called aliasing, and recording devices remove such frequencies with a low-pass filter before sampling. Each sample is stored with a fixed number of bits, typically 16, which gives 65,536 amplitude levels and a dynamic range of about 96 dB.

From Waveform to Spectrum¶

A waveform shows how the pressure changes, but not which tones produce the change. A violin note at 440 Hz is not a single sine wave. It is a sum of sine waves at 440 Hz, 880 Hz, 1320 Hz, and further multiples, each with its own strength, and in the waveform these components blend into one curve. The Fourier transform separates them. It describes a signal as a sum of sine waves and returns, for each frequency, how strongly it is present.

For a sequence of NNN samples, the discrete Fourier transform computes NNN complex coefficients:

Applied to a whole recording, the Fourier transform returns one spectrum that averages over the entire duration. Everything that changes over time is lost: the spectrum of the Brahms clip contains all its notes but does not say in which order they were played. Speech is even more dynamic, since its sounds change every few tens of milliseconds.

The Short-Time Fourier Transform¶

The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) applies the Fourier transform to short pieces of the signal. It cuts the recording into frames of NNN samples, moves the start of each frame forward by a hop of HHH samples, and transforms each frame separately. Before the transform, each frame is multiplied with a window function w(n)w(n)w(n) that fades the signal in and out at the frame boundaries. Without the window, the abrupt cut at the frame edges would itself produce spurious frequencies, an effect called spectral leakage. Common choices are the Hann and the Hamming window, both bell-shaped curves. Figure 2 shows the procedure: the window slides along the signal, and each windowed frame yields one column of a time-frequency picture. Why the window reduces the leakage follows from the convolution theorem (Fourier Transform and Convolution).

The squared magnitudes ∣X(t,k)∣2|X(t,k)|^2∣X(t,k)∣2, usually in decibels, form the spectrogram: time runs to the right, frequency upwards, and the color shows the energy at each point.

The Time-Frequency Trade-off¶

A longer frame gives a finer frequency grid, but it also averages over a longer time. Two events within one frame merge into one spectrum, and a note that starts in the middle of a frame smears across it. Figure 3 shows four seconds near the start of the Brahms clip with three frame lengths. With 10 ms frames, each bin spans 100 Hz: the harmonics of the violins merge into broad bands, but every change of note appears as a sharp vertical edge. With 160 ms frames, each bin spans 6.25 Hz: the harmonics become thin horizontal lines, but the note changes blur over a sixth of a second. The 40 ms frames in the middle are a compromise in which both the harmonics and the note changes remain visible.

No frame length gives both a fine time and a fine frequency resolution, because Δf=1/T\Delta f = 1/TΔf=1/T ties them together. The task decides the compromise. Speech recognizers use frames of about 25 ms with a hop of 10 ms, short enough to capture consonants that last only a few tens of milliseconds. Pitch and chord analysis of music uses frames of 100 ms or more to resolve neighboring notes in the lower octaves. The fingerprint of Audio Fingerprinting uses frames of 93 ms. Frames always overlap, with a hop of a quarter to a half of the frame length, so that an event at the boundary of one frame lies in the middle of the next.

Frequency resolution is not set by the sampling rate

A higher sampling rate adds bins at the top of the spectrum, up to the new fs/2f_s / 2fs​/2, but it does not make the bins narrower. Only a longer frame does. A 40 ms frame resolves 25 Hz at 16 kHz, 22.05 kHz, and 48 kHz alike.

Frames and Segments¶

A single feature vector for an hour of broadcast would average away everything a user searches for, and a single 40 ms frame is too short to tell whether someone is speaking. Audio analysis therefore works with retrieval units on two levels, just as long texts are split into overlapping passages: overlapping frames of 40 ms with a hop of 20 ms, and overlapping segments of 4 seconds with a hop of 100 ms. Figure 4 shows how the two levels work together. Each frame yields a feature vector; each segment summarizes the (4000−40)/20+1=199(4000 - 40)/20 + 1 = 199(4000−40)/20+1=199 frames it contains with statistics such as the mean and the variance of each feature. Segments overlap for the same reason as frames and text passages, so that no event is cut in half at a boundary (Time-Based Media and Specialized Solutions).

The two lengths answer different questions. The frame length fixes the frequency resolution, as computed above: 40 ms frames give 25 Hz, enough for the spectral shape of speech and for the higher notes of music. The segment length fixes the unit that a classifier labels and a search returns. Distinguishing speech from music needs several seconds, because the typical rhythm of speech, syllables and short pauses, only shows over that time. Timbre, such as the difference between a guitar and a falling rock, shows within a fraction of a second. Speech for transcription is often cut at pauses, so that no word is split between two segments. Four seconds is a reasonable default for general audio analysis.

The Ear as a Frequency Analyzer¶

The ear performs a similar analysis before the brain ever sees the signal. In the cochlea, a fluid-filled spiral of the inner ear, the basilar membrane vibrates at different places for different frequencies: high frequencies near the entrance, low frequencies near the tip. Hair cells along the membrane convert the vibration into nerve signals, so the auditory nerve receives a kind of spectrogram rather than a waveform. The frequency map is not uniform. Low frequencies occupy a long stretch of the membrane and high frequencies a short one, so the ear resolves low frequencies finely and high frequencies coarsely: from 100 Hz to 200 Hz is an octave, while from 10,000 Hz to 10,100 Hz is a sixth of a semitone, although both steps are 100 Hz. Loudness is perceived roughly logarithmically, which is why decibels describe it well. These two properties, a frequency resolution that decreases with frequency and a logarithmic perception of energy, are built into the mel scale and the MFCC features of the next section. The anatomy of the ear and the auditory pathway to the cortex are described in Human Hearing and Sound Physics.
