Color Features

Look at the squares labeled A and B in Figure 1. Square A looks dark gray and square B looks light gray, yet both have exactly the same pixel values, as the connecting gray bar in the right panel shows. Our visual system does not report the light that reaches the eye. It estimates the color of the surface, and because it knows that B lies in the shadow of the cylinder, it corrects for the shadow. A camera makes no such correction. It records the light.

The illusion holds two lessons for color features. First, the numbers in an image file are measurements of light, not of perception, so we must transform them before their distances become meaningful. Second, a feature must decide which differences to ignore: a shadow changes the measured color of a surface, but not the surface itself. This section explains how three numbers come to describe a color, shows why the space in which we compare them decides whether distances make sense, and then builds the two classic color features, histograms and moments. Research has proposed many more, such as color correlograms and dominant-color descriptors, but most of them lost their relevance once learned features took over (Learned Features: CNNs). Histograms and moments are enough to show the ideas that all color features share.

From Light to Three Numbers¶

The light that reaches the eye from a point in the scene is the product of the illumination and the reflectance of the surface. Snow reflects almost all visible wavelengths, vegetation reflects mostly green, and water reflects little. The result is a spectrum: an energy value for every wavelength between about 380 and 780 nanometers. The eye does not measure this spectrum. The retina has three types of cones, and each type responds to a broad band of wavelengths (Figure 2). The S (short-wavelength) cones peak near 420 nm, the M (medium) cones near 534 nm, and the L (long) cones near 564 nm. They are often called the blue, green, and red cones, although the L cones peak in the yellow-green; they respond more strongly to red light than the other two types. Each cone type weights the spectrum with its sensitivity curve and sums the result, so the whole spectrum is reduced to three numbers.

Reducing a spectrum to three numbers loses information. Two different spectra can produce the same three cone responses, and then they look identical. This effect is called metamerism, and every display relies on it: a monitor mixes red, green, and blue light so that the cones respond as they would to the original scene, although the two spectra have little in common. Metamerism is also the reason why three channels per pixel are enough for an image file. A color is a point in a three-dimensional space, and color features describe how the pixels of an image are distributed in that space. The appendix covers the human visual system in The Human Visual System and the definitions of all color spaces in Color Systems.

Why the Color Space Matters¶

The obvious choice is the space the file already uses: sRGB, with three values from 0 to 255 per pixel. Figure 3 shows four colors where one channel changes by 100 units from one box to the next. In sRGB, all three steps have the same Euclidean distance of 100. To the eye, they are not equal. The step from the olive green (155, 200, 100) to the golden yellow (255, 200, 100) looks large, while the step from the salmon (255, 100, 100) to the crimson (255, 0, 100) looks much smaller. The sRGB scale was defined to match the response of monitors, not perception, so equal distances do not mean equal differences.

Perceptual color spaces fix this by construction. They describe a color by its brightness, how light or dark it appears, and its chromaticity, which has two parts: the hue (red, yellow, green, blue, and so on) and the saturation, how vivid the color is compared to a gray of the same brightness. Two such spaces matter for retrieval.

CIE Lab was defined in 1976 so that Euclidean distances approximate perceived differences. Its LLL axis is the lightness from 0 (black) to 100 (white). The aaa axis runs from green (negative) to red (positive), the bbb axis from blue (negative) to yellow (positive), and grays lie at a=b=0a = b = 0a=b=0. Figure 4 shows the sRGB colors in three slices of constant lightness. Converted to Lab, the three steps of Figure 3 have distances of 42.7, 59.8, and 27.7. The step from salmon to crimson is less than half as large as the step before it, which matches what we see. Lab is only approximately uniform, and refined difference formulas exist for color science, but its Euclidean distance is good enough for retrieval features.

HSV and its sibling HSL turn the RGB cube into a cylinder (Figure 5). The hue becomes an angle around the vertical axis, the saturation the distance from the axis, and the value VVV (or the lightness in HSL) the height. HSV is not perceptually uniform, but it separates the hue from the brightness. That is useful when the lighting should not matter. A red car in the sun and in the shade has nearly the same hue but a different value, so a feature that uses only the hue ignores the shadow.

Hue is an angle

Hue values wrap around: 350° and 10° are both red and only 20° apart, not 340°. Compare hues with min⁡(∣α−β∣,360°−∣α−β∣)\min(|\alpha - \beta|, 360° - |\alpha - \beta|)min(∣α−β∣,360°−∣α−β∣). Hue is also undefined for grays: a pixel with zero saturation has no meaningful hue, and very dark pixels have a noisy one. HSV-based features therefore treat pixels with low saturation or low value as gray levels instead of assigning them a hue.

The conversions from sRGB to Lab or HSV are fixed formulas that every image library provides. The conversion to Lab has one step that is easy to miss: sRGB values are gamma-encoded and must be linearized before they are converted to CIE XYZ and then to Lab. Color Systems gives all formulas and converts one color step by step.

Color Histograms¶

A color histogram describes how often each color occurs in an image. It needs a fixed set of reference colors, which play the role of the vocabulary in text retrieval. The system assigns each pixel to its nearest reference color, counts the pixels per reference color, and divides the counts by the number of pixels. The result is a vector h\mathbf{h}h with one entry hih_ihi​ per reference color cic_ici​; the entries lie between 0 and 1 and sum to 1. The division makes the histogram independent of the image size, so a thumbnail and the full-resolution photo have nearly the same histogram. Because the histogram ignores where a pixel lies, shifting, rotating, or mirroring the image does not change it either.

The choice of reference colors decides what the histogram can distinguish (Figure 6). The simplest choice divides each RGB channel into equal ranges: four ranges per channel give 43=644^3 = 6443=64 reference colors. Perceptual spaces give better vocabularies. In HSV, the hue circle can be split into sectors of similar-looking hues, for example seven sectors with boundaries at 22°, 45°, 70°, 155°, 186°, 278°, and 330°. Combined with nine ranges of the value VVV, which reflect that the eye is more sensitive to brightness than to hue, this gives 7×9=637 \times 9 = 637×9=63 reference colors. In Lab, equal ranges on all three axes give reference colors that are roughly equally far apart.

Figure 7 applies a Lab vocabulary to three photos: the Matterhorn photo, a photo of the Taj Mahal in Agra, and a photo of the tennis player Roger Federer at Wimbledon. We divide Lab into ranges of 20 units on each axis: five ranges for LLL from 0 to 100, and five ranges each for aaa and bbb from −50 to 50, where values beyond the outer ranges are assigned to them. This gives 5×5×5=1255 \times 5 \times 5 = 1255×5×5=125 reference colors. The middle column of the figure replaces each pixel by its reference color and thus shows what the histogram counts. The right column shows the three histograms. The order of the reference colors is the same in every row, grouped by lightness from dark to light, so a bar at the same position means the same color in all three photos.

The Matterhorn photo uses 40 of the 125 reference colors, and three of them cover almost two thirds of its pixels. The light sky blue (37.6%) covers the sky and the snow in shade, white (14.0%) the sunlit snow and the clouds, and a dark blue-gray (12.0%) the forest slopes in shadow. The Taj Mahal photo contains the same kinds of colors: blue sky, light marble, and dark shadows. They fall into different bins, though. Its sky is darker than the Matterhorn’s: it lands in the two blue bins one and two lightness ranges lower (19.4% and 10.0%), and at the position of the Matterhorn’s largest bin, the Taj Mahal histogram is almost empty (0.01%). The Federer photo is dominated by a single yellowish green, the grass, with 57.2% of the pixels.

Comparing Histograms¶

Given two histograms h\mathbf{h}h and g\mathbf{g}g over the same NNN reference colors, the simplest distances compare them bin by bin:

Both distances treat all reference colors as unrelated. If a hazier sky moves 60% of the pixels from “sky blue” into the neighboring “pale blue”, the L1 distance counts this as a change as large as if the sky had turned red. Small changes in lighting or camera settings move pixels across bin boundaries, and bin-by-bin distances react strongly to them. The quadratic distance adds a matrix A\mathbf{A}A that encodes how similar the reference colors are, so that moving pixels to a similar color costs little.

Perceptual similarity is not semantic similarity: a feature that measures how an image looks finds images that look alike, which is not the same as images that show the same thing.

Color Moments¶

A histogram with 125 bins is a long vector, and practical systems use up to a thousand bins. Color moments summarize the distribution of each channel with a few statistics instead. For a channel ccc of an image with N×MN \times MN×M pixels, such as the LLL, aaa, or bbb channel, the first four moments describe the position, the width, the asymmetry, and the tails of the distribution.

The moments have very different value ranges: standard deviations of 5 to 30, skewness values around 1, covariances of up to about 140. Before a Euclidean or Manhattan distance can compare two moment vectors, each dimension must be rescaled, for example with the Gaussian normalization of Distance and Similarity Measures, which divides each dimension by its standard deviation over the collection. Two variants reduce the vector further. Moments of the lightness alone give 4 values and ignore color. Moments of the two chromaticity channels aaa and bbb give 8 values plus one covariance, 9 in total, and are less sensitive to changes in lighting.

Compared with histograms, moments are compact: 15 values instead of 125 to 1000 bins, and no cross-bin matrix is needed. They share the robustness of histograms against shifts, rotations, and scaling. Their weakness is that a few statistics cannot describe a distribution with several peaks. The bright sky and the dark forest of the Matterhorn photo average to a lightness of 60.9, a value that few of its pixels actually have. The appendix shows how all moments are computed in a single pass over the pixels (Extended Image Features).

What Global Color Features Miss¶

Histograms and moments ignore where a color occurs. A blue lake at the bottom of a photo counts the same as a blue sky at the top, and shuffling the pixels of an image produces a meaningless image with exactly the same features. A simple remedy keeps a little layout information. Stricker and Dimai proposed in 1996 to split the image into five overlapping regions, an ellipse in the center and the four corners, to compute color moments per region, and to weight the center more strongly. Such features remain cheap and work well for photos with a typical composition, where the subject sits in the middle.

The deeper limitation is that color says nothing about structure. The pyramid shape of the Matterhorn, the sharp ridges, the grain of the forest: all of this lies in how the intensities are arranged, not in which colors occur. The next section turns to features that describe this arrangement.
