Video Understanding with Transformers

A viewer of “Tears of Steel”, the open film of this chapter, wants the fight against the robots, and the subtitles do not help, because the fight is shown, not said. The previous sections do not help either: shot boundaries tell where the fight might start and end, and motion features tell that a lot happens in some shots, but neither knows what a robot looks like. A sports editor faces the same gap with a different query. The 90-minute recording of last night’s match contains three goals, and the editor wants each of them as a clip for the evening news (Time-Based Media and Specialized Solutions).

Both queries need features that know concepts, and both are best answered by several modalities at once. This section first applies image embeddings, which place images and texts in one vector space, to the shots of a video, then looks at models that learn motion as well, aligns the modalities on the common time line, and works through the sports example. It ends with models that answer moment queries directly, and with the limits of all of these methods on long videos.

Searching Shots with Image Embeddings¶

The simplest video search engine reuses an image search engine. Shot detection splits the video into shots, and a keyframe represents each shot. CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder on 400 million web image-caption pairs so that matching pairs get nearby vectors. Its image encoder embeds every keyframe, and at query time its text encoder embeds the query, so that a vector index returns the keyframes nearest to the query. Each result is a shot with a start time and an end time, so the player can jump to its beginning. CLIP and its training are treated in depth in Transformer Embeddings for Image Search.

A single keyframe, however, represents a shot poorly when the shot changes. A shot of 20 seconds may show an empty bridge at first, and a robot that climbs onto it only at the end. A common remedy samples frames at a fixed rate, for example one per second, embeds every sampled frame, and combines the frame embeddings into one vector per shot. The simplest combination is the mean.

Real CLIP similarities between a text and an image are much lower than these toy values, typically between 0.1 and 0.4 even for good matches, but the comparison between the strategies is the same.

Luo and colleagues studied these choices systematically in CLIP4Clip (2022), using frames sampled at one per second. They concluded that a single image is far from enough to represent a video clip for text-to-video retrieval. Mean pooling of CLIP frame embeddings worked best when only little video data was available for training, while learned attention over the sequence of frames helped only on large training sets. MSR-VTT, a benchmark of 10,000 web videos of 10 to 32 seconds with 200,000 captions, tests on 1,000 clips with one caption each. CLIP without any training on video found the correct clip at the first rank for 31.2% of these captions, and fine-tuning with mean pooling raised this to 43.1%.

Long shots raise the same question as long documents, which retrieval-augmented generation splits into overlapping chunks before indexing (Chunking Strategies). A shot of three minutes, in which the camera follows a character through a building, is too coarse as a single unit. Splitting it into windows of a few seconds, with some overlap, gives units that match a query more precisely, and the shot boundaries still serve as natural edges where they exist.

Models That See Motion¶

Image embeddings describe what is visible, not what happens. A person opening a door and a person closing a door look alike in every single frame; only the order of the frames distinguishes them. The same holds for sitting down versus standing up, or for a ball that enters the goal versus a ball that bounces off the post. Queries about actions need models that process several frames together. Three families of such models developed one after the other.

Two-stream networks, proposed by Simonyan and Zisserman in 2014, process a video in two parallel networks. One network classifies single frames and captures the appearance. The other receives a stack of dense optical flow fields, computed with the methods of Motion Detection, and captures the motion. The two predictions are combined at the end. The motion stream alone recognized actions well, which confirmed that the flow carries information that the frames do not.

3D convolutional networks extend the convolution kernels from the image plane into time. In an image network, a kernel of, for example, 3 by 3 pixels slides over the image and computes at every position a weighted sum of the pixels under it. A 3D kernel also spans several consecutive frames, so that it responds to patterns that change over time. C3D (Tran and colleagues, 2015) used kernels of 3 by 3 pixels by 3 frames in all layers. I3D (Carreira and Zisserman, 2017) “inflated” the trained 2D filters of an image classifier into 3D, so that video models could start from what image models had already learned. I3D was trained on Kinetics, a dataset with 400 human action classes and more than 400 clips per class. Convolutional networks for images are covered in Learned Features: CNNs.

Video transformers treat a clip as a sequence of patches, just as the vision transformer cuts an image into patches of 16 by 16 pixels and processes them as a sequence of tokens. TimeSformer (Bertasius and colleagues, 2021) cuts every frame into patches and lets each patch attend to other patches in two steps within each block: first to the patches at the same position in the other frames, then to the patches of its own frame. VideoMAE (Tong and colleagues, 2022) pretrains such models without labels by hiding 90 to 95% of the patches and training the model to reconstruct them, which works because neighboring frames are highly redundant.

The two-step attention of TimeSformer addresses the cost of video. For retrieval, these models play the same role as the image encoders. They turn a clip into an embedding, and video-text models trained on clips with captions place clips and queries in one space, so that “a robot climbs onto a bridge” can be matched against what happens, not only against what is visible in one frame.

Aligning the Modalities on the Time Line¶

The images are only one of the tracks that a video holds. Speech recognition turns spoken words into text with timestamps, and text recognition (OCR) reads the words shown on screen. The audio track also contains events that are neither speech nor music, such as applause, crowd cheers, explosions, and sirens, and pretrained audio classifiers that know hundreds of sound classes detect them. All of these share the clock of the video. A subtitle from 5:47 to 5:50, a “crowd cheering” event from 63:14 to 63:31, and a scoreboard read by OCR at 63:35 can all be attached to the shots that cover these times. Speech recognition and audio classifiers are treated in Transcription and Classifying Audio.

A video retrieval system therefore builds an index of moments. Each entry covers a shot or a time window and stores its start and end time, one or more visual embeddings, the transcript and subtitle text, the OCR text, and the detected audio events. Figure 1 sketches the pipeline from the tracks of the video to the index and to a query.

A query searches each field with the method that fits it. Words that are spoken or shown are found with BM25 over the transcript and OCR text, visual concepts with a vector search over the embeddings, and event types or time ranges with filters. Reciprocal rank fusion combines the rankings: each moment receives the sum of 1/(k+rank)1 / (k + \text{rank})1/(k+rank) over the rankings in which it appears, with a constant such as k=60k = 60k=60, so that moments ranked high in several fields rise to the top (Building Semantic Search). Adjacent hits merge into one moment, and its edges are moved to the nearest shot boundaries, so that the clip neither starts in the middle of a shot nor cuts off its end.

The two queries of the chapter opener show why the fusion matters. Figure 2 shows both on “Tears of Steel”, split by TransNet V2 into 140 shots, with one frame per second embedded by CLIP. BM25 over the 76 subtitles finds “robot hand” in exactly two entries, at 0:30.8 and 5:47.0, and all other subtitles score 0. The shot boundaries turn each hit into a clip. The line at 0:30.8 lies inside a shot from 0:25.0 to 0:40.2. The line at 5:47.0 runs until 5:50.0 and crosses a cut at 5:48.4, so its clip joins two adjacent shots. Used as a CLIP query, the same line finds neither of these shots among its top five: what is said is not visible in the frames.

The query for the fight finds no matching subtitle and depends entirely on the visual embeddings. To measure the result, we marked the three fight sequences of the film, from 1:26 to 1:57, from 7:05 to 8:25, and from 9:32 to 9:48, and counted the fight shots among the five best shots. For the query “a battle against giant robots”, the middle keyframe and the best single frame each place three fight shots among their top five, and mean pooling places two; its best shot comes from the end credits, which show drawn robots. For “soldiers fight robots” and “a fight against a robot”, no strategy finds more than one fight shot. All of these scores lie between 0.26 and 0.30, so a fight shot and an unrelated shot differ by about 0.01 at most. CLIP recognizes robots but not a fight, and the wording of the query matters more than the pooling. Mean pooling does not help here, because the shots are short, 5.2 seconds on average, and leave it only a few frames to combine. Models that see motion, and the fusion with the other tracks, address exactly these weaknesses.

Modalities can disagree

Alignment on the time line does not mean that the tracks talk about the same thing. A song with lyrics in the background produces a transcript that has nothing to do with the scene, a news ticker brings OCR text about another story, and a commentator may talk about the previous goal while the replay of a different scene runs. A system that trusts one track blindly inherits its noise. Fusion helps, because a moment that several tracks agree on is more reliable than a moment found by one track alone.

Worked Example: Football Highlights¶

The sports editor wants the goals of the match as clips. A system that analyzes every frame of 90 minutes with a video model would be slow and expensive. The classic pipeline instead lets cheap detectors propose candidate moments and lets more expensive analysis confirm them.

Step 1: Candidates from the crowd. During ordinary play, the stadium sound has a steady, moderate energy and a stable spectrum. When an exciting play happens, the energy rises quickly, the sound shifts toward higher frequencies as the cheering becomes brighter, and the variation over time becomes erratic. A classifier on such audio features (Classifying Audio) marks the segments with strong audience reactions. The classical system used a decision tree on hand-crafted features; a current system uses a pretrained audio event classifier.

Step 2: The commentator. Commentators raise their pitch, speak faster, and use shorter phrases during key plays. These changes appear in the pitch and the short-term energy of the speech, and they strengthen or weaken the evidence from the crowd.

Step 3: Confirmation from text. Speech recognition transcribes the commentary of the candidate segments (Transcription). Words such as “goal”, the names of the players, or “what a save” confirm the moment and label the type of event. A goal changes the score, so OCR on the scoreboard in the corner of the image gives strong confirmation when the score changes from 1:0 to 2:0 shortly afterwards.

Step 4: Clip edges from the shots. The crowd reacts after the goal, not before it, so the reaction marks the end of the action, not its beginning. The clip should include the attack that led to the goal. Shot boundaries give the edges: the clip starts one shot before the reaction begins and ends at the first boundary after the reaction. Broadcasts often introduce replays with an animated logo transition, which twin thresholding (Shot Boundary Detection) detects as a gradual transition and which marks the end of the live action.

Example: Cutting the clip

The crowd energy exceeds its threshold from 63:14 to 63:31 in the recording, and the commentary in this segment contains “goal”. The shot boundaries near this segment lie at 62:51, 63:09, 63:21, and 63:40. The reaction starts in the shot from 63:09 to 63:21, so the clip starts one shot earlier, at 62:51, with the attack. The first boundary after the end of the reaction is 63:40. The clip runs from 62:51 to 63:40 and lasts 49 seconds.

The pipeline illustrates a pattern that recurs in video retrieval. Cheap signals, here the audio energy, scan the whole recording; more expensive analysis, here speech recognition and OCR, runs only on the candidates; and the shot structure turns a detection into a usable clip. Today the individual detectors are learned models, but the structure of the pipeline has stayed the same.

Moment Retrieval and Long Videos¶

The pipelines above combine separate components. Current research trains models that answer a moment query directly. Moment retrieval takes a video and a natural-language query and returns the start and end time of the matching moments. Lei, Berg, and Bansal introduced the QVHighlights dataset for this task in 2021: more than 10,000 YouTube videos, each annotated with a free-form query, the moments that match it, and a five-point relevance score for the short clips within these moments. Their baseline, Moment-DETR, is a transformer that predicts the moments from the query and the clip features in one step.

Video-language models go one step further. They feed frame embeddings, often together with the transcript, into a large language model, which then answers questions about the video in text: what Thom says to Celia when the breakup is replayed, or what the scientists are trying to achieve. Benchmarks such as Video-MME (2025) evaluate such models on videos from 11 seconds to one hour, with questions that may also require the subtitles and the audio track.

Long videos remain the hard case. One hour sampled at one frame per second gives 3,600 frames; at 196 patches each, that is 705,600 visual tokens, beyond the context of most models and far beyond what attention over all pairs can afford. Models therefore sample few frames, compress the patches of each frame into a few tokens, or process the video in chunks, and each of these choices can miss the one moment that matters. Fusion can fail when a track is noisy, and the benchmarks cover only parts of the tasks that real collections pose, from surveillance footage to recorded talks.

The practical consequence is the architecture of retrieval-augmented generation applied to video, in which a retriever finds a few relevant passages and a language model reads only those (7 - Retrieval Augmented Generation). The index of moments finds a few candidate segments in a collection of thousands of hours, quickly and cheaply. A video-language model then inspects only these minutes, verifies the match, and answers the question. Each component does what it does best: the index scales, and the model understands.
