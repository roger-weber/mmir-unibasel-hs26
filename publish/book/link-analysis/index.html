Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS

Return to the two candidate pages for “uni basel” in Figure 1. Content signals and anchor texts already favour the home page, but there is a stronger and more general clue: the structure of the web itself. Thousands of pages link to the university home page, while only a handful link to a single course page. A hyperlink is an implicit recommendation: the author of the linking page considered the target worth visiting. The web is a directed graph with pages as nodes and links as edges, and its topology tells us which pages the web itself considers important.

This section develops two classic ways to read that graph. PageRank computes one global importance score for every page, independent of any query. It was the key innovation of Google in 1998 and is still the reference model for ranking nodes in a graph. HITS distinguishes two roles that a page can play for a topic, hub or authority, and computes them on a small, query-specific subgraph. Both turn out to be the same mathematical idea: an eigenvector of a link matrix, computed by repeated matrix-vector multiplication.

Counting Links Is Not Enough¶

The simplest link-based score counts the incoming links of a page, its in-degree. Figure 1 shows a small web of seven pages. Page E receives six links and ranks first. B and G follow with four links each, C has two, A and D one each, and F none.

This ranking matches our first intuition: E is clearly the centre of this graph. But look at B and G, which tie with four incoming links. G receives a link from E, the most important page. B receives its links from A, C, D, and F, which are all minor pages themselves. G should rank above B, because a recommendation from an important page should count more than a recommendation from an unimportant one. The in-degree treats all links as equal votes.

There is a second, more practical problem. In-degree is easy to manipulate. Anyone can create a thousand pages that all link to a target page, a practice called link farming, and inflate its in-degree at almost no cost. We need a measure in which a link is worth only as much as the page it comes from. Then a thousand links from a thousand worthless pages add up to very little.

The Random Surfer¶

PageRank, developed by Larry Page and Sergey Brin at Stanford, defines importance through a thought experiment. A random surfer starts on some page and browses the web forever. On each page, the surfer does one of two things:

  1. With probability α\alphaα, the surfer clicks one of the links on the page, choosing each link with equal probability.

  2. With probability 1−α1 - \alpha1−α, the surfer gets bored and jumps to a random page, for example by typing a URL or opening a bookmark. Every page is equally likely as the jump target.

If a page has no outgoing links (a dangling node), the surfer always jumps to a random page. The PageRank of a page is the long-run fraction of time the surfer spends on it. In terms of probability theory, the surfer performs a Markov chain with pages as states, and PageRank is the stationary distribution of this chain. A page with high PageRank is a page that people browsing the web would visit often, which makes it a good candidate for the top of a result list.

To compute the PageRank of a page ppp, we ask how the surfer can arrive there. Either they follow a link from some page qqq that links to ppp (written q→pq \rightarrow pq→p), or they jump to ppp at random. Let L(q)L(q)L(q) denote the number of outgoing links of qqq, and let P\mathbb{P}P be the set of all pages with N=∣P∣N = |\mathbb{P}|N=∣P∣.

A page is important if important pages link to it. Each page passes its PageRank in equal shares along its outgoing links; the random jump term 1−αN\frac{1-\alpha}{N}N1−α​ gives every page a small base score. A common choice is α=0.85\alpha = 0.85α=0.85.

The formula captures both problems with in-degree. A link from qqq contributes PR(q)\text{PR}(q)PR(q), so links from important pages count more. And the contribution is divided by L(q)L(q)L(q), so a page that links to a thousand targets gives each of them only a thousandth of its vote. A link farm of a thousand pages that nobody links to has a combined PageRank of only about 1000⋅1−αN1000 \cdot \frac{1-\alpha}{N}1000⋅N1−α​, a negligible amount on a web of billions of pages. To inflate a target’s PageRank, a spammer needs links from pages that already have a high PageRank, and those are much harder to get.

The random jump also matters. Without it, a group of pages that link only to each other, a rank sink, would absorb the surfer forever and collect all PageRank. The jump guarantees that the surfer can always leave, and it makes the Markov chain irreducible and aperiodic. These two properties guarantee that a unique stationary distribution exists.

Each iteration is one multiplication of a sparse matrix with a vector. The web graph is extremely sparse: a page has a few dozen outgoing links, not billions. One iteration therefore costs time proportional to the number of links, and it parallelizes well across many machines. The number of iterations grows only slowly with the size of the graph. Page et al. reported that PageRank on a database of 322 million links converged in roughly 52 iterations, and on half of the data in roughly 45.

PageRank is not a relevance score

PageRank measures how often a random surfer visits a page. It does not know the query. A search engine computes PageRank once after each crawl and uses it as one feature among many, combined with content signals such as BM25F and proximity. Ranking by PageRank alone would return the same famous pages for every query. PageRank is therefore not “how relevant a page is”, but “how prominent a page is in the web graph”.

PageRank also favours old pages. A new page, however good, has not yet collected incoming links, and links from important pages take time to appear. This is one reason why search engines add freshness signals for news and events, where the best page may be only a few minutes old.

Personalized PageRank¶

The random jump in PageRank picks every page with the same probability. Nothing forces this choice. If the surfer always jumps back to a fixed set of pages S\mathbb{S}S, the stationary distribution measures importance from the point of view of S\mathbb{S}S: pages that are close to S\mathbb{S}S in the link graph and reachable through many paths receive high scores. This variant is called Personalized PageRank. Formally, we replace the uniform jump vector 1N⋅1\frac{1}{N} \cdot \mathbf{1}N1​⋅1 with a jump distribution s\mathbf{s}s that is non-zero only on S\mathbb{S}S:

r=(1−α)⋅s+α⋅Mr\mathbf{r} = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \mathbf{s} + \alpha \cdot \mathbf{M} \mathbf{r}r=(1−α)⋅s+α⋅Mr
(5)

With S\mathbb{S}S chosen as the pages of one topic, for example all pages that a web directory lists under “sports”, we obtain a topic-specific importance score. Haveliwala’s topic-sensitive PageRank precomputes one such vector per topic and mixes them at query time according to the topic of the query. With S\mathbb{S}S chosen as a single node, for example one user in a social network, Personalized PageRank ranks all other nodes by how closely connected they are to that user. This is the basis for many “people you may know” and “who to follow” recommendations, which we revisit in Ranking Without a Query: Feeds.

Hubs and Authorities: HITS¶

In 1998, Jon Kleinberg proposed a different reading of the link graph. He observed that pages play two distinct roles for a topic, shown in Figure 3:

  • An authority is a page that the community recognizes as a definitive source on a topic, such as Wikipedia, IMDb, or the official site of an organization. Good authorities receive many links.

  • A hub is a page that collects links to the authorities of a topic without providing much content itself, such as a curated list of resources or a directory. Good hubs have many outgoing links.

The two roles reinforce each other. A good hub links to good authorities, and a good authority is linked from good hubs. Crucially, both are defined for a topic: a page that is a great authority on Basel is not an authority on jaguars. HITS (Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search) therefore does not run on the whole web, but on a small graph built for each query QQQ, as shown in Figure 4:

  1. Root set: take the top results for QQQ from a text search engine, for example the top 200. This set already contains relevant pages, but not necessarily the best hubs and authorities.

  2. Base set: extend the root set with all pages that a root page links to, and with pages that link to a root page (limited to, say, 50 per root page). Links within the same domain are usually dropped, since they are mostly navigation menus.

The outgoing links of a root page are found by parsing it. The incoming links require a link index of the web, which a search engine builds during the crawl anyway. For each page ppp in the base set, HITS computes an authority value a(p)a(p)a(p) and a hub value h(p)h(p)h(p).

  • E (a=0.31a = 0.31a=0.31) is a slightly better authority than C (a=0.29a = 0.29a=0.29), even though E has two incoming links and C has three. E is linked by the two strong hubs A and F. C is linked by A, by the average hub G, and by the weak hub D.

  • Roles mix. B is a weak authority and an average hub; G is a top authority but also has some hub value. C and H are pure types: C has no outgoing links, and nobody links to H.

PageRank and HITS answer different questions. PageRank is computed once, offline, for the whole web, and measures global prominence regardless of the query. HITS is computed at query time on a few thousand pages and finds the best sources for one topic. This is also HITS’s main weakness: building and iterating a base graph for every query is expensive, and the topic focus is fragile, since a few strongly linked off-topic pages in the base set can take over the result. Web search engines adopted PageRank-style global scores. The hub and authority idea survived elsewhere. SALSA, a random-walk variant of HITS by Lempel and Moran, powered Twitter’s “Who to Follow” service, where the accounts closest to a user act as hubs and the accounts they follow act as authorities (Ranking Without a Query: Feeds).

SALSA: hubs and authorities as a random walk (optional reading)

HITS sums the hub values of all linking pages and then normalizes. A hub passes its full value to every page it links to, however many links it has, and the principal eigenvector concentrates on the most densely linked group of pages in the base set. Lempel and Moran called this the tightly knit community effect: a small group of pages that all link to each other can take over the result, even if the group is off topic. SALSA (Stochastic Approach for Link-Structure Analysis) replaces the sums with a random walk, in the spirit of PageRank.

The walk alternates between the two roles. From an authority ppp, the surfer follows one of the incoming links of ppp backwards to a hub, each with equal probability. From a hub qqq, the surfer follows one of the outgoing links of qqq forwards to an authority, again with equal probability. With in(p)\text{in}(p)in(p) the number of incoming and out(q)\text{out}(q)out(q) the number of outgoing links, the updates are

a(t+1)(p)=∑q→ph(t)(q)out(q)h(t+1)(q)=∑q→pa(t)(p)in(p)a^{(t+1)}(p) = \sum_{q \rightarrow p} \frac{h^{(t)}(q)}{\text{out}(q)} \qquad h^{(t+1)}(q) = \sum_{q \rightarrow p} \frac{a^{(t)}(p)}{\text{in}(p)}a(t+1)(p)=q→p∑​out(q)h(t)(q)​h(t+1)(q)=q→p∑​in(p)a(t)(p)​
(7)

Each hub splits its value among its outgoing links, and each authority splits its value among its incoming links. Both vectors remain probability distributions, so no normalization step is needed.

Lempel and Moran showed that the stationary distribution of this walk has a closed form. Within each connected component of the graph, the authority value of a page is proportional to its in-degree, and the hub value to its out-degree. A dense community therefore gains only as much as it has links, and the iteration is not even necessary. The price is the mutual reinforcement of HITS: within a component, a link from a strong hub counts as much as a link from a weak one. SALSA is in effect an in-degree count on the query-specific base set. Its topic focus comes from the base set, not from the link weights.

On the eight-page base set of Figure 5, all pages form one component with 17 links, so each authority value is its in-degree divided by 17: D 35%, G 24%, C 18%, E 12%, B and F 6% each. The hub values follow the out-degrees: A 29%, F and H 18% each, B and G 12% each, D and E 6% each. The top two authorities agree with HITS, but C and E swap places. HITS ranks E above C because the strong hubs A and F link to E. SALSA ignores hub strength and ranks C above E, since it has three incoming links and E only two.

Early experiments with HITS revealed three problems. An entire domain linking to one external page creates an overpowered authority. Automatically generated links, such as advertisements or “site by” credits, grant authority to off-topic pages. And for a query such as “jaguar car”, the more frequent term dominates, so the top authorities are about cars in general rather than about Jaguar.

Making HITS robust: the Bharat and Henzinger improvements (optional reading)

Krishna Bharat and Monika Henzinger proposed two refinements of HITS in 1998 that address these problems.

The first refinement fixes overpowered hubs and authorities by letting each domain cast one vote instead of each page. If kkk pages of one domain link to a page ppp, each of these links receives the weight aw(q,p)=1kaw(q, p) = \frac{1}{k}aw(q,p)=k1​ in the authority update of ppp. If a page ppp links to lll pages of one domain, each of these links receives the weight hw(p,q)=1lhw(p, q) = \frac{1}{l}hw(p,q)=l1​ in the hub update of ppp. Figure 6 shows the case of three pages on the example domain a.com linking to one page, and of one page linking to four pages on b.com. The updates become

(8)

The second refinement keeps the computation on topic. Bharat and Henzinger build a reference document CCC from the terms with the highest tf⋅idf\text{tf} \cdot \text{idf}tf⋅idf weights in the root set, so rare and specific terms such as “jaguar” are represented more strongly. For each page ppp in the base set, they compute the cosine similarity s(p)=c⊤p∥c∥⋅∥p∥s(p) = \frac{\mathbf{c}^{\top} \mathbf{p}}{\lVert \mathbf{c} \rVert \cdot \lVert \mathbf{p} \rVert}s(p)=∥c∥⋅∥p∥c⊤p​ between the tf⋅idf\text{tf} \cdot \text{idf}tf⋅idf vectors of CCC and ppp (Vector Space Retrieval). Pages with a similarity below a threshold, for example the median of all s(p)s(p)s(p) values, are removed from the base set, which drops about half of the pages.

The remaining similarity values weight the propagation, so that relevant pages pass on more of their hub and authority value:

In their evaluation on 28 topics, the combination of both refinements improved the precision of the top ten results by at least 45% over the original HITS algorithm. The same two ideas, counting votes per domain and weighting links by topical similarity, reappear in many later link-analysis methods, including the per-domain anchor votes of Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts.
