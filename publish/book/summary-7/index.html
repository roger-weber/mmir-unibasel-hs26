The link-analysis models all compute an eigenvector of a link matrix by power iteration; they differ in where the random walk restarts (everywhere, at a fixed set, or nowhere) and in which graph they walk (the whole web or a query-specific base set). The learning-to-rank families can use the same model and differ in the unit of training: one document, one pair, or one list. A modern search engine uses link analysis to produce features and learning to rank to combine them, and a recommendation feed reuses both: random walks and embeddings to generate candidates, and a learned multi-task ranker to order them.

Key Takeaways¶

  1. Web search differs from classical retrieval in an uncurated, adversarial collection, short and ambiguous queries, huge result sets with nearly equal term scores, and a link graph that reveals which pages people consider important. The engine serves the intent most users mean, composes the result page for that intent, and relies on a crawler that collects the evidence about each page that the page itself does not provide.

  2. Content signals go beyond term counts: proximity rewards query terms that appear close together, BM25F weights fields before a single saturation, anchor text describes a page in other people’s words, counted with one vote per domain, and location, language, and site quality adapt the ranking to the user and the source.

  3. PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random surfer and measures global prominence, not relevance; HITS computes topic-specific hubs and authorities on a query-dependent subgraph.

  4. Learning to rank combines hundreds of signals; pairwise and listwise objectives target the order of results, and LambdaRank weights each pair by its effect on nDCG. Clicks are abundant training labels but must be corrected for position bias.

  5. Feeds rank without a query, with the same staged design: embeddings and the social graph supply candidates, a learned ranker predicts the user’s reactions, and the weights on these predictions decide what the platform optimizes.

Exam focus

Beyond the formulas above, be able to explain:

  • Why in-degree is a poor and manipulable importance measure, and how PageRank fixes both problems (weighting by source importance, dividing by out-degree).

  • The role of the damping factor α\alphaα and the random jump: dangling nodes, rank sinks, uniqueness of the solution, and the effect on convergence.

  • The difference between PageRank and HITS: global versus query-specific, offline versus at query time, one score versus two roles.

  • Why BM25F saturates after combining fields, and why anchor votes are counted per domain.

  • The difference between pointwise, pairwise, and listwise learning to rank, and why position bias makes raw clicks unreliable labels.

  • How a feed mirrors the web ranking stack, and why optimizing engagement is not the same as optimizing value.

Self-Check Questions¶

  1. (Understand) Explain the random surfer model. What does the PageRank of a page represent, and why is it not a measure of relevance to a query?

  4. (Analyze) A spammer repeats a keyword ten times in the title, every heading, and the body of a page. Compare the effect under per-field saturation and under BM25F.

  5. (Evaluate) A new ranker improves nDCG on the judged test set but reduces the click-through rate in an A/B test. Which result do you trust, and what further evidence would you collect?

  6. (Evaluate) A short-video platform raises the weight of shares in its engagement score. Discuss the likely effects on what users see, and propose one safeguard.

Further Reading¶

  • Brin, S., & Page, L. (1998). The anatomy of a large-scale hypertextual web search engine. Computer Networks and ISDN Systems, 30(1-7), 107-117. PDF (archived). The original Google paper: crawler, index, anchor text, proximity hit lists, and PageRank in one system.

  • Page, L., Brin, S., Motwani, R., & Winograd, T. (1999). The PageRank citation ranking: Bringing order to the web. Technical Report 1999-66, Stanford InfoLab. PDF. Defines PageRank, the random surfer, and power iteration, with the convergence results quoted in Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS.

  • Kleinberg, J. M. (1999). Authoritative sources in a hyperlinked environment. Journal of the ACM, 46(5), 604-632. PDF. Introduces hubs, authorities, and the HITS iteration.

  • Bharat, K., & Henzinger, M. R. (1998). Improved algorithms for topic distillation in a hyperlinked environment. Proceedings of SIGIR 1998, 104-111. PDF. The domain weights and similarity weighting of the HITS dropdown.

  • Lempel, R., & Moran, S. (2001). SALSA: The stochastic approach for link-structure analysis. ACM Transactions on Information Systems, 19(2), 131-160. The random-walk variant of HITS in the optional reading of Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS, including the tightly knit community effect and the in-degree closed form.

  • Haveliwala, T. H. (2002). Topic-sensitive PageRank. Proceedings of WWW 2002, 517-526. PDF (archived). Personalized PageRank with one precomputed vector per topic.

  • Büttcher, S., Clarke, C. L. A., & Lushman, B. (2006). Term proximity scoring for ad-hoc retrieval on very large text collections. Proceedings of SIGIR 2006, 621-622. DOI. Pairs neighbouring hits of different query terms in the merged hit list and adds a proximity score to BM25; the pairing rule of Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts.

  • Robertson, S., Zaragoza, H., & Taylor, M. (2004). Simple BM25 extension to multiple weighted fields. Proceedings of CIKM 2004, 42-49. Argues for combining field counts before saturation and defines BM25F. A free treatment is in Robertson and Zaragoza’s survey The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond (Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval, 3(4), 2009), listed in the Chapter 4 summary.

  • Broder, A. (2002). A taxonomy of web search. ACM SIGIR Forum, 36(2), 3-10. PDF. The navigational, informational, and transactional query types.

  • Olston, C., & Najork, M. (2010). Web crawling. Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval, 4(3), 175-246. PDF. A survey of crawler architecture, politeness, freshness, and duplicate detection.

  • Liu, T.-Y. (2009). Learning to rank for information retrieval. Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval, 3(3), 225-331. PDF (archived). The standard survey of pointwise, pairwise, and listwise methods.

  • Burges, C., Shaked, T., Renshaw, E., Lazier, A., Deeds, M., Hamilton, N., & Hullender, G. (2005). Learning to rank using gradient descent. Proceedings of ICML 2005, 89-96. PDF. Introduces RankNet and the pairwise logistic loss of Learning to Rank.

  • Burges, C. J. C. (2010). From RankNet to LambdaRank to LambdaMART: An overview. Microsoft Research Technical Report MSR-TR-2010-82. PDF. The pairwise loss, the lambda weights, and boosted trees, explained by their author.

  • Chapelle, O., & Chang, Y. (2011). Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge overview. JMLR Workshop and Conference Proceedings, 14, 1-24. PDF. The 2010 challenge on real web-search features; the winning team of track 1 combined twelve rankers, eight of them LambdaMART models.

  • Joachims, T. (2002). Optimizing search engines using clickthrough data. Proceedings of KDD 2002, 133-142. PDF. Turns clicks into pairwise preferences for learning to rank.

  • Joachims, T., Granka, L., Pan, B., Hembrooke, H., & Gay, G. (2005). Accurately interpreting clickthrough data as implicit feedback. Proceedings of SIGIR 2005, 154-161. PDF. The eye-tracking study behind position bias: clicks are informative but biased.

  • Joachims, T., Swaminathan, A., & Schnabel, T. (2017). Unbiased learning-to-rank with biased feedback. Proceedings of WSDM 2017, 781-789. arXiv. Inverse propensity weighting to remove position bias from click data.

  • Covington, P., Adams, J., & Sargin, E. (2016). Deep neural networks for YouTube recommendations. Proceedings of RecSys 2016, 191-198. PDF. The two-stage candidate generation and ranking design of a large video feed.

  • Yi, X., Yang, J., Hong, L., Cheng, D. Z., Heldt, L., Kumthekar, A., Zhao, Z., Wei, L., & Chi, E. (2019). Sampling-bias-corrected neural modeling for large corpus item recommendations. Proceedings of RecSys 2019, 269-277. PDF. Training two-tower retrieval models with in-batch negatives at YouTube scale.

  • Gupta, P., Goel, A., Lin, J., Sharma, A., Wang, D., & Zadeh, R. (2013). WTF: The who to follow service at Twitter. Proceedings of WWW 2013, 505-514. PDF. The circle of trust (Personalized PageRank) and SALSA on the follow graph.

  • Aggarwal, P., Murahari, V., Rajpurohit, T., Kalyan, A., Narasimhan, K., & Deshpande, A. (2024). GEO: Generative engine optimization. Proceedings of KDD 2024, 5-16. arXiv. How page content can be tuned to be cited in generated answers.

  • Twitter Engineering (2023). Twitter’s recommendation algorithm. Blog post (archived), source code, and heavy ranker weights. The candidate sources, heavy ranker, and filters of a production feed.
