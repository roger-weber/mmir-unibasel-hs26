Learned Features: CNNs

Each hand-designed feature of this chapter was built with one task in mind, and it solves that task well. SIFT finds the same object in another photo, HOG with a linear classifier finds pedestrians, and other hand-designed detectors find faces. Moved to a new task, they rarely work as well: SIFT recognizes this particular Matterhorn, but not mountains in general, and a detector built for pedestrians does not find mountains. Color and texture features are more general, but they measure only how an image looks. They can filter a collection for images that look alike, but they cannot close the gap to what an image shows. No hand-designed feature separates “snow-covered peak” from “white building under a blue sky”.

Neural networks changed how features are made. In 2012, the convolutional network AlexNet won the ImageNet challenge, which asks for one of 1,000 classes per photo, with a top-5 error of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the best entry based on hand-designed features. Since then, image classifiers learn their features from labeled examples instead of relying on features designed by experts. A designer tries the patterns that seem promising, such as gradients in a few directions or a fixed set of filter scales. Training searches a far larger space of possible filters and keeps whatever helps the task, without the designer’s assumptions about what should matter. Learned features have limits too: a network trained on everyday photos does not transfer completely to X-ray or satellite images. They do, however, provide a framework for transfer. Given labeled examples of the new domain, we adapt the same network instead of designing a new feature.

The architectures that followed, from VGG and GoogLeNet to residual networks and Vision Transformers, are described in CNN Architectures. This section opens one trained network, ResNet-18, and asks three practical questions: what did it learn, how do we turn it into a feature extractor for retrieval, and how do we reuse it for a task it was never trained on?

ResNet-18 was trained to sort the 1.28 million photos of ImageNet into 1,000 classes such as “alp”, “mosque”, and “racket”. It has 11.7 million parameters and takes an image of 224 by 224 pixels as input. Nothing in its training tells it about edges, colors, or textures. It only receives a photo and the correct class, and it adjusts its parameters to make fewer mistakes. How convolution, pooling, and training work is explained in C - Neural Networks; here we look at the result.

What the First Layer Learns¶

The first layer of ResNet-18 consists of 64 filters of 7 by 7 pixels with three color channels each. It applies them exactly like the Sobel kernels of Edges, Texture, and Shape: each filter slides over the image, and at each position the weighted sum of the pixels under it becomes one value of a new image. The difference is that nobody chose the weights. Figure 1 shows the 64 filters after training, next to the Sobel and Gabor kernels of the previous section.

The resemblance is striking. Many learned filters are oriented edge detectors: a dark and a bright half at some angle, like a Sobel kernel. Others are stripe patterns of several orientations and widths, like Gabor filters. A third group responds to color: blobs of one color, and filters that compare two opposing colors, such as orange against blue or magenta against green. These opponent pairs echo the aaa and bbb axes of CIE Lab from Color Features. Trained only to name objects, the network rediscovered the building blocks that decades of research on human vision and image processing had designed by hand. The receptive fields of the retina and the primary visual cortex (The Human Visual System) inspired the architecture of convolutional networks, and the filters the networks learn look much like those receptive fields.

Feature Maps: From Edges to Objects¶

Each filter produces a feature map: an image that is bright where the filter’s pattern occurs. The next layer applies its own filters to the stack of feature maps, so it detects combinations of first-layer patterns, such as two edges that meet in a corner or a stripe pattern of a certain color. Every few layers, the network halves the resolution and increases the number of feature maps. Figure 2 shows this structure for LeNet, the compact network for handwritten digits from the 1990s: 6 feature maps of 28 by 28, then 16 maps of 10 by 10, then fully connected layers down to 10 output classes. ResNet-18 follows the same principle on a larger scale. Its four stages produce 64 feature maps of 56 by 56, then 128 maps of 28 by 28, 256 maps of 14 by 14, and finally 512 maps of 7 by 7.

Because each layer looks at a neighborhood of the previous one, the part of the image that influences one value grows from layer to layer. A first-layer value depends on 7 by 7 pixels; a value in the last stage of ResNet-18 depends on most of the image. Visualizations of trained networks show what this means: early layers respond to edges and colors, middle layers to textures and object parts such as eyes, wheels, or windows, and the last layers to whole objects and scenes. The layers form an abstraction ladder of their own, from what an image looks like to what it shows.

At the top, ResNet-18 averages each of the 512 final feature maps into a single number, and a fully connected layer maps the resulting 512 values to 1,000 class scores, which a softmax turns into probabilities. For the Matterhorn photo, the network answers “alp” with 77.9% and “valley” with 21.3%. For the Taj Mahal, it answers “mosque” with 98.8%. That is wrong, but understandable: the Taj Mahal is a mausoleum, ImageNet has no such class, and the network can only choose among the classes it was trained on. For the Federer photo, the best class is “racket” with 34.5%.

The Second-to-Last Layer as an Embedding¶

For retrieval, the 1,000 class probabilities are too narrow. They describe an image only in terms of ImageNet classes, and a photo of a class the network never saw gets a distribution over unrelated classes. The 512 values before the last layer are more useful. They are the network’s description of the image, from which it computes all class scores with a single linear layer. Removing that layer turns the classifier into a feature extractor, and its output is an image embedding.

Example: What the embedding finds

We compare the Matterhorn photo with the 45 photos of the collection in Figure 10: Matterhorn views, other peaks, scenes with the query’s colors, and other subjects. Figure 3 shows five of them, each with the cosine similarity of its ResNet-18 embedding to the query, its rank among the 45 photos, and its rank under the color histogram of Color Features.

The embedding ranks Aoraki / Mount Cook in New Zealand first, with a cosine similarity of 0.924. The Matterhorn seen from the Riffelberg above Zermatt (0.910) and from nearly the same spot in Zermatt (0.883) follow at ranks 3 and 6. The pond that reflects white clouds (0.527, rank 27) and the Taj Mahal (0.487, rank 31) are far away.

The color histogram orders the same photos very differently. With the quadratic distance, the pond is at rank 5, the Taj Mahal at rank 13, the Riffelberg photo at rank 18, Aoraki at rank 21, and the photo from Zermatt, whose sky is a deeper blue than the pale sky of the query, only at rank 26. The embedding ignores the shade of the sky and responds to the mountain. It does not respond to this particular mountain, though: its best match is a peak on the other side of the world.

The example shows both the strength and the limit of this embedding. ResNet-18 was trained to sort photos into 1,000 classes, not to compare two photos. Its last layer separates the classes well, so a mountain lands near other mountains. The distances within a class, however, were never part of the training. For the classifier, Aoraki and the Matterhorn are both an “alp”, and nothing in its training rewarded telling them apart. That is why a peak in New Zealand outranks a photo of the same mountain from nearly the same spot.

Text embeddings had the same problem. The [CLS] vector of BERT reflects its pre-training tasks, not the similarity of two sentences, and Sentence-BERT fixed this with a bi-encoder: the same network encodes both sentences, and the training compares their two embeddings directly (Semantic Similarity with Transformers). Image retrieval needs the same step. An encoder that is trained on pairs or triplets of images, with the similarity of their embeddings as the training objective, learns what “similar” should mean for the task. This approach is called metric learning. Face embeddings, at the end of this section, are its classic application, and the transformer encoders of Transformer Embeddings for Image Search learn similarity for images of every kind.

Two photos are compared by the cosine similarity of their embeddings, and a vector index from 6 - Vector Search makes the search fast. The cost per image is one forward pass through the network, about 1.8 billion multiply-add operations for ResNet-18, which takes a few milliseconds on a GPU and well under a second on a laptop CPU.

Which layer, and which scale?

The embedding of the last stage is not the only possible choice, but the earlier stages are poor retrieval features. Averaged over the image, the 64 feature maps of the first stage give cosine similarities of 0.98 or more for the Matterhorn, the Taj Mahal, and the Federer photo alike: every photo contains edges and colors of all kinds. The last stage gives values between 0.45 and 0.51 for the same pairs. Also note that the network’s ReLU activations are never negative, so cosine similarities of CNN embeddings are never negative either, and their absolute values differ from model to model. Only the ranking and the differences between candidates carry information, and the same holds for the transformer embeddings of the next section.

Transfer Learning¶

ImageNet classes are rarely the classes a retrieval system needs. An archive of alpine photos may need “north face”, “glacier”, and “hut”; an online shop needs its own product categories. Training a network from scratch for each such task would need millions of labeled images. Transfer learning avoids this: it reuses the network trained on ImageNet, whose embeddings already separate visual concepts well, and adapts only a small part to the new task.

The cheapest form keeps the network frozen. We compute the embedding of every labeled image once and train a small classifier on top, typically a single linear layer with a softmax, p(c∣image)=softmax⁡(We+b)p(c \mid \text{image}) = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{e} + \mathbf{b})p(c∣image)=softmax(We+b). This is called a linear probe, because it tests how much of the new task the frozen embedding already captures with a linear decision. A few dozen to a few hundred labeled images per class are often enough, and training takes seconds. Fine-tuning goes further: it continues training some or all layers of the network on the new data with a small learning rate. It usually gives better results when the new images differ strongly from ImageNet photos, such as X-ray images or satellite images, but it needs more labeled data and computing power, and it can overwrite what the network learned before.

Example: A linear probe with six labeled photos

The new task is to decide whether a photo shows a mountain, a class that ImageNet does not have in this form. We label three mountain photos and three other photos of the collection at random, train a logistic regression on their ResNet-18 embeddings, and test it on the remaining 39 photos, 17 of which show a mountain. Always answering “no mountain” would be right for 22 of the 39 test photos, or 56%.

Over 50 random choices of the six training photos, the probe classifies 98.1% of the test photos correctly on average, and 92.3% in the worst case. The same procedure on the 125-bin color histograms reaches only 68.9% on average. Six labeled examples are enough because the frozen embedding already places mountain photos close together; the probe only has to find the direction that separates them from the rest.

For retrieval, transfer learning offers a second, even simpler path. No classifier is needed at all: the frozen embeddings of the collection go into a vector index, and a query image is answered by its nearest neighbors. Around 2014, several research groups showed that such “off-the-shelf” CNN embeddings beat the best hand-designed features on many retrieval and recognition benchmarks, often without any adaptation. They marked the point where learned features replaced histograms and SIFT as the default for image search. For tasks that ask for the same object, such as finding photos of the same landmark, later systems fine-tuned these networks with pairs of matching and non-matching photos, which is the metric learning described above.

Face Embeddings¶

Faces are a special case. A face search system does not need the category “face”, it needs the identity: is this the same person as in that photo? A classifier with one class per person does not work, because new persons appear every day. Face recognition systems therefore train the embedding itself so that photos of the same person lie close together and photos of different persons lie far apart. This is metric learning in its purest form, the image counterpart of a bi-encoder: one network encodes both photos, and the loss compares their embeddings. FaceNet, introduced by Google in 2015, trains with triplets of photos: an anchor a\mathbf{a}a, a positive p\mathbf{p}p of the same person, and a negative n\mathbf{n}n of a different person.

Training therefore concentrates on hard triplets, where a different person still looks similar.

With a trained face embedding, three tasks become vector operations. Verification (“is this the same person?”) compares the distance of two embeddings with a threshold. Identification (“who is this?”) searches the nearest neighbor in a gallery of known faces. Clustering groups the faces of a photo collection by person, which is how photo apps build their “people” albums. FaceNet’s 128-dimensional embedding reached 99.63% accuracy on the standard verification benchmark of its time; later loss functions such as ArcFace improved on it. The threshold for verification is chosen on an evaluation set, as in Scores, Thresholds, and ROC Curves.

Face embeddings are biometric data

A face embedding identifies a person, so it falls under special protection. The EU General Data Protection Regulation treats biometric data used to identify a person as a special category, and the EU AI Act prohibits building face recognition databases by untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or from surveillance cameras. Error rates of face recognition also differ between demographic groups, which turns a technical error into an unfair one. A system that indexes faces needs a legal basis, a clear purpose, and an evaluation per group, not only a vector index.

CNN embeddings trained for classification have two limits that the next section removes. Their distances were not trained to compare images, and they know only the concepts that the training labels taught them, with no connection to language. A user can search with an example image, but not with the words “a pyramid-shaped peak above a pine forest”.
