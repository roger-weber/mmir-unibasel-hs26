Edges, Texture, and Shape

The color histogram of the Matterhorn photo in Figure 7 is mostly sky blue, white, and a dark blue-gray. A white sailboat on a lake below a blue sky could have almost the same histogram. What makes the Matterhorn recognizable is something else: four ridges that meet in a sharp summit, dark rock bands between fields of snow, the grain of the forest below. All of this lies in how the intensity changes from one pixel to the next, and none of it shows up in a color distribution.

This section describes that arrangement in four steps. Edges mark where the intensity changes abruptly. Texture describes how such changes repeat over a region. Shape describes the outline of a region. Local keypoints mark distinctive spots that can be found again in another photo of the same scene. For each step, image processing has developed many features, and this section presents one or two representative methods: gradients, filter banks, histograms of local edge directions, and keypoint descriptors. They show the main ideas behind hand-designed features, and the same ideas reappear in the learned features of Learned Features: CNNs. Most of these features work on the brightness alone, so we use the lightness channel LLL of CIE Lab from the previous section, with values from 0 to 100.

Edges and Gradients¶

An edge is a place where the intensity changes sharply between neighboring pixels. Figure 1 shows that edges have four physical causes: a change of the surface orientation, such as the rim of a can; a change of depth, where an object ends and the background begins; a change of illumination, such as a cast shadow; and a change of the material or its color, such as the border of a printed label. Only the first two belong to the shape of an object. A shadow edge is the checker-shadow problem of the previous section in another form: it is clearly visible, but it is not a property of the object.

Edge detectors measure the gradient, the rate of change of the intensity in the horizontal and vertical direction. The Sobel operator estimates both with two small kernels. To apply a kernel at a pixel, we place it over the pixel and its eight neighbors, multiply each weight by the pixel value under it, and sum the products. Sliding the kernel over all pixels produces a new image:

The gradient image is the raw material for two simple texture features. The first one counts edges. A pixel is an edge pixel if its gradient magnitude reaches a threshold τ\tauτ, and the edge density of an image with N×MN \times MN×M pixels is the share of edge pixels:

It stays the same for all images of a collection, so that their edge densities can be compared. Smooth regions such as sky or water contain few edge pixels, while city scenes, crowds, and foliage contain many.

The second feature describes the edge pixels. A gradient histogram works like a color histogram, with reference gradients in place of reference colors. The gradient histograms on the right show how the edges are oriented. In the Taj Mahal photo, the vertical lines of the minarets and the horizontal lines of the walls and the pool produce gradients along the image axes: 61.8% of its edge pixels fall into the sectors at 0°, 90°, 180°, and −90°. The ridges and slopes of the Matterhorn are diagonal, so its edge pixels split almost evenly between the axis-aligned sectors (48.1%) and the diagonal ones (51.9%). The largest bar of the Federer photo, 24.0% at 90°, comes from a single long line: the edge between the dark border at the top of the photo and the brighter lawn below it. In all three photos, most edge pixels fall into the weakest magnitude range, between 68.4% and 79.0%.

The distances of Color Features apply to gradient histograms unchanged, including the quadratic distance, whose matrix A\mathbf{A}A now encodes how similar two reference gradients are. Directions are angles, so their differences wrap around, just like hues. Like color histograms, gradient histograms ignore where the edges lie; the HOG features below compute them per small cell to keep the layout. Gradient moments, the counterpart of color moments, are in Extended Image Features.

Texture with Gabor Filters¶

A texture is a pattern of intensity changes that repeats over a region: the vertical stripes of tree trunks, the ripples on a lake, the regular grid of windows on a facade. The Sobel kernels cannot describe such patterns well. They see only a 3 by 3 neighborhood and respond to a single scale of change. A texture feature needs filters that respond to one direction and one spatial frequency at a time, and many such filters to cover all directions and frequencies.

Texture analysis has three traditions: the structural approach searches for repeated basic elements (texels), the statistical approach summarizes the responses of local filters, and the Fourier approach analyzes the image in the frequency domain. This section follows the statistical approach; Extended Image Features shows the Fourier view and further texture features.

The Gabor filter is the classic choice. It is a sinusoidal wave inside a Gaussian window (Figure 4). The wave makes the filter respond to stripes of one wavelength and one orientation; the window limits the response to a local neighborhood. The filter is defined on rotated coordinates xˉ=xcos⁡θ+ysin⁡θ\bar{x} = x \cos\theta + y \sin\thetaxˉ=xcosθ+ysinθ and yˉ=−xsin⁡θ+ycos⁡θ\bar{y} = -x \sin\theta + y \cos\thetayˉ​=−xsinθ+ycosθ.

The first factor is a Gaussian window with width σ\sigmaσ and aspect ratio γ\gammaγ. The second factor is a complex wave with wavelength λ\lambdaλ, so 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ is its frequency, and phase ϕ\phiϕ. The angle θ\thetaθ rotates the filter. A filter responds strongly where the image contains stripes with its wavelength and orientation.

Gabor filters have a biological motivation. Neurons in the primary visual cortex, the first stage of visual processing in the brain, respond to oriented bars and edges in a small part of the visual field (The Human Visual System). Their response profiles are well described by Gabor functions. The same filters appear again in Learned Features: CNNs, learned by a neural network from data.

A filter bank combines Gabor filters for several orientations and scales. Typical banks use 3 to 7 orientations between 0 and 180° and 2 to 5 frequencies 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ between about 0.05 and 0.5 cycles per pixel. In the Fourier domain on the right of Figure 4, each filter covers one elliptical region of frequencies and directions, and together they tile the frequency plane. Figure 5 applies four such filters to a photo of a windsurfer. The mast of the sail forms a strong straight edge against the white foam, which appears as a spike in the frequency domain, perpendicular to the mast. The three filters with the orientation of the spike show the mast at three scales, from a coarse blob to a fine line; the filter with a different orientation does not show it. The appendix explains this Fourier view step by step.

In practice, we convolve the image with each filter of the bank directly in the spatial domain; image libraries such as OpenCV and scikit-image provide the kernels. Each filter produces a complex response image, and its absolute value measures how strongly the filter’s pattern is present at each pixel. To turn the response images into a feature vector, we compute the four moments of Color Features for each response image, and optionally the covariances between the response images.

Example: Size of a Gabor moment vector

A bank with 5 orientations and 3 scales has 15 filters and produces 15 response images. Four moments per response image give 15⋅4=6015 \cdot 4 = 6015⋅4=60 values. The covariances between all pairs of response images add 15⋅14/2=10515 \cdot 14 / 2 = 10515⋅14/2=105 values, for a total of 165. With so many filters, moments are preferred over histograms: a joint histogram over 15 responses would need an impractical number of bins.

Shape¶

Shape features describe the outline or the area of a region, which must be found first. Edge detectors such as the Canny detector locate boundaries, contour tracing connects boundary pixels into closed curves, and segmentation methods such as region growing group neighboring pixels with similar values. Once a region is known, a few descriptors characterize it:

  • Area: the share of the image covered by the region.

  • Centroid: the mean xxx and yyy position of the region’s pixels.

  • Axis of least inertia: the line through the centroid around which the region’s pixels have the smallest sum of squared distances. It gives the main direction of the region and allows us to rotate regions into a standard orientation before comparing them.

  • Eccentricity: the ratio of the length to the width of the region, measured along the axis of least inertia and perpendicular to it.

  • Circularity: how close the region is to a circle, for example 4πA/P24\pi A / P^24πA/P2 for a region with area AAA and perimeter PPP, which is 1 for a circle and smaller for any other shape.

Shape descriptors work well when the object is easy to separate from the background: product photos on a white background, trademarks, technical drawings, or cells in a microscope image. In natural photos, the segmentation step is the weak link. The Matterhorn has no clean outline: its lower slopes merge with the neighboring ridges, and clouds cut through its silhouette. The next two features describe shape without segmenting the image first.

Histograms of Oriented Gradients¶

The histogram of oriented gradients (HOG) describes local shape by the distribution of edge directions in small cells. Dalal and Triggs made it popular in 2005 for detecting pedestrians, and it became one of the most widely used hand-designed features. It works in four steps:

  1. Gradients. Compute gmagg_{\text{mag}}gmag​ and gdirg_{\text{dir}}gdir​ for every pixel, for example with the Sobel kernels. HOG uses unsigned directions between 0 and 180°: a dark-to-bright edge and a bright-to-dark edge with the same orientation count the same, because the clothing of a person can be darker or brighter than the background.

  2. Cell histograms. Divide the image into cells of 8 by 8 pixels. For each cell, build a histogram with 9 direction bins of 20° each, where each pixel votes for its direction with a weight equal to its gradient magnitude (Figure 6). Strong edges dominate the histogram, and flat areas contribute almost nothing.

  3. Block normalization. Group 2 by 2 neighboring cells into a block of 16 by 16 pixels, concatenate their four histograms into 36 values, and normalize the block to unit length. This removes the effect of local lighting and contrast. The blocks overlap: they shift by one cell (8 pixels) at a time, so each cell appears in up to four blocks, each time normalized with different neighbors.

  4. Descriptor. Concatenate all block vectors. For the detection window of Dalal and Triggs, 64 by 128 pixels, this gives 7⋅15=1057 \cdot 15 = 1057⋅15=105 blocks of 36 values, a vector of 3,780 values.

A classifier, originally a linear support vector machine, learns from the HOG vectors of many windows which ones contain a person. For retrieval, HOG vectors can be compared directly with the distances of Distance and Similarity Measures, or the cell histograms can be kept as a collection of local features. HOG tolerates small shifts and deformations, because a pixel that moves by a few positions usually stays in the same cell, and it tolerates lighting changes because of the block normalization. It is not invariant to rotation or scale: a person lying down or seen from far away produces a different descriptor. HOG is also worth remembering for what comes next. Oriented filters, pooling over a small cell, and normalization are exactly the operations that the layers of a convolutional network learn on their own (Learned Features: CNNs).

SIFT: Finding the Same Object Again¶

Two photos of the Matterhorn taken from different spots differ in almost every global feature: the sky covers a different share, the light has changed, the mountain appears larger or smaller and slightly rotated. Yet some spots appear in both photos: the summit, the end of a rock band, the corner of a snow field. The Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT), introduced by David Lowe in 1999, finds such spots and describes each of them so that the same spot can be recognized in another image.

SIFT first detects keypoints: small blob-like structures, such as a dark spot on a bright background, each at the scale at which it is most pronounced. A keypoint therefore comes with a position, a size, and a dominant gradient direction. SIFT then describes the neighborhood of each keypoint, rotated to the dominant direction and scaled to the keypoint’s size. It divides the neighborhood into a 4 by 4 grid of subregions and builds an 8-bin histogram of gradient directions in each, giving 4⋅4⋅8=1284 \cdot 4 \cdot 8 = 1284⋅4⋅8=128 values per keypoint. Because the neighborhood is rotated and scaled before the description, the descriptor is the same whether the spot appears small or large, upright or tilted. Because it is built from gradient directions and normalized, it also tolerates changes in brightness and moderate changes of the viewpoint. The dropdown below describes the four steps of the algorithm.

The four steps of SIFT (optional reading)

Step 1: Scale space. SIFT blurs the image with Gaussian filters of increasing width σ\sigmaσ and subtracts each blurred image from the next (Figure 7). Each difference of Gaussians (DoG) keeps the structures of one size range, much like a band-pass filter. After each doubling of σ\sigmaσ, the image is downsampled to half its width and height, and the next group of blurred images, called an octave, starts from there. The pyramid covers sizes from a few pixels to a large part of the image.

Step 2: Extrema. A pixel of a DoG image becomes a keypoint candidate if it is larger or smaller than all 26 neighbors: 8 in its own DoG image and 9 each in the DoG images directly above and below in scale. With five blurred images per octave, as in the figure, there are four DoG images, and candidates are searched in the two middle ones. Lowe used six blurred images per octave, which gives three levels for the search. Candidates with low contrast are discarded, because noise can easily move them. Candidates on straight edges are discarded too: along an edge, the position is poorly defined, so only points with strong curvature in two directions survive, such as corners and blobs.

Step 3: Orientation. In the neighborhood of each keypoint, whose size depends on the keypoint’s scale, SIFT builds a histogram of gradient directions with 36 bins of 10° each, weighted by the gradient magnitudes. The highest peak gives the dominant direction. Every other peak that reaches at least 80% of the highest creates an additional keypoint at the same position with that direction, so ambiguous spots are described twice. The descriptor of step 4 is computed relative to this direction, which makes it invariant to rotation.

Step 4: Descriptor. SIFT takes a 16 by 16 neighborhood around the keypoint, scaled to the keypoint’s size and rotated to its dominant direction, and divides it into 4 by 4 subregions of 4 by 4 samples (Figure 8). In each subregion, it builds a histogram of 8 gradient directions, weighted by the magnitudes and by a Gaussian that gives the samples near the keypoint more influence. The 16 histograms of 8 bins form the 128-dimensional descriptor. The descriptor is normalized to unit length, values above 0.2 are clipped to reduce the influence of a few very strong edges, and the vector is normalized again.

To find the same object in two images, SIFT compares descriptors. For each descriptor of the first image, we find its nearest and second-nearest descriptor in the second image by Euclidean distance. A match is accepted only if the nearest neighbor is clearly closer than the second-nearest one. This ratio test rejects ambiguous matches, which are frequent on repetitive structures such as windows or tree tops, where many keypoints look alike.

Key Formula: Ratio Test for Keypoint Matching
accept match (d,d1′)if∥d−d1′∥∥d−d2′∥<0.8\text{accept match } (\mathbf{d}, \mathbf{d}'_1) \quad \text{if} \quad \frac{\|\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}'_1\|}{\|\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d}'_2\|} < 0.8accept match (d,d1′​)if∥d−d2′​∥∥d−d1′​∥​<0.8
(7)

d\mathbf{d}d is a descriptor of the first image, d1′\mathbf{d}'_1d1′​ and d2′\mathbf{d}'_2d2′​ are its nearest and second-nearest descriptors in the second image. A correct match is usually much closer than any other candidate; an accidental match has competitors at a similar distance. Lowe found that the threshold 0.8 removes 90% of the false matches and less than 5% of the correct ones.

Figure 9 shows matches on four image pairs. Most lines connect corresponding points, but some cross the image and are wrong, especially on repetitive structures. A final geometric check removes them: the correct matches must agree on one transformation between the two images, such as a rotation, scaling, and shift, and an algorithm such as RANSAC finds the largest group of matches that agrees on one transformation. The number of matches that survive this check is a reliable score for “the same object or scene”.

To test SIFT, and the features of the following sections, on more than a handful of photos, we use a collection of 45 photos (Figure 10). Eight show the Matterhorn from other positions, in other seasons, and in other light. Twelve show other snow-covered peaks, such as the Eiger, the Weisshorn, Mount Fuji, and Aoraki / Mount Cook. Nine contain no mountain but share the palette of the query, with pale blue sky, white clouds, and dark green: sailboats on a lake, chapels below forested hills, a lighthouse on a meadow. The remaining 16 show unrelated subjects, from a city at night to a pizza, and include the Taj Mahal and Federer photos. All photos come from Wikimedia Commons under free licenses; the photo credits are listed at the end of the chapter summary.

Example: Matching the Matterhorn

The Matterhorn photo has 5,333 SIFT keypoints. Figure 11 matches them against three other photos. The white lines are the matches that pass the ratio test, and the pink lines are the matches among them that agree on one rotation, scaling, and shift.

The top photo shows the east and north faces from Zermatt as well, but under a cloudless, deep blue sky. The ratio test accepts 180 matches, and 100 of them agree on one transformation: the mountain appears 1.24 times larger and rotated by 0.9°. The pink lines run in parallel and connect the rock bands and forested slopes below the summit, which look alike in both photos. The snow-covered summit contributes few of them, because its snow and light differ between the two photos.

The middle photo shows the same faces from the Riffelberg, higher up above Zermatt. The ratio test still accepts 83 matches, but they point in all directions. Only 6 of them count as consistent, and all 6 lead to the same point in the other photo: the transformation that explains them shrinks the whole query onto that point. The photo of Hong Kong at night at the bottom reaches 10 such matches in the same way. A practical system therefore also rejects transformations with an implausible scaling.

Among the 45 photos of the collection, the photo from nearly the same spot is the only one with more than 10 consistent matches. The other seven photos of the Matterhorn, taken from other positions, in other seasons, or in other light, are indistinguishable from unrelated photos.

Comparing every descriptor of a query with every descriptor of millions of images is far too slow. Sivic and Zisserman borrowed the solution from text retrieval in 2003. They cluster a large sample of descriptors with k-means into a vocabulary of visual words, typically tens of thousands of cluster centers or more, and replace each descriptor by the ID of its nearest center. An image becomes a bag of visual words, which is weighted with tf-idf and stored in an inverted file, exactly like the documents of 1 - Classical Text Retrieval. Only the short list of images that share many rare visual words with the query needs the expensive geometric check.

SIFT and its faster successors became the standard tools for tasks that require the same object or scene: stitching photos into a panorama, finding near-duplicate and copied images, recognizing landmarks and products, and reconstructing 3D models from photo collections. Implementations exist in OpenCV, where SIFT is freely available since its patent expired in 2020. Their limitation is the flip side of their strength. SIFT matches this particular scene, not mountains in general: a photo of the Eiger shares no more consistent keypoints with the Matterhorn than a city at night does. For a scene as changeable as a mountain, SIFT does not even reliably find the same mountain, because snow, shadows, and perspective change the local patterns that its descriptors record. It works best for rigid objects with stable texture, such as buildings, paintings, products, and printed pages. Recognizing categories requires features that generalize over the appearance of many instances, and those are what neural networks learn.
