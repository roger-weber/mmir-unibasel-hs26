Speech Recognition with HMMs

Where this is used

Transcription summarizes the classical speech recognizer in one paragraph, with MFCC features, phoneme models, a pronunciation dictionary, and a language model, before it turns to Whisper. This section describes how these components work together. It builds on the hidden Markov models of Hidden Markov Models, uses the k-means clustering and the Gaussian mixtures of Clustering and the decision trees of Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting, and ends with the neural networks of Recurrent Networks and the encoder-decoder transformers of Model Families and Pretraining.

For three decades, from the 1980s to the 2010s, speech recognizers were built from hidden Markov models. Their components are still visible in today’s systems: the MFCC or mel features, the 10 ms frames, the phonemes as units, the language model, and the search for the most probable transcript. We first state the problem, then build the recognizer from the phoneme upward, look at how it copes with speaking rate, speakers, and microphones, and finally follow its replacement by neural networks.

The Recognition Problem¶

A recognizer receives a sequence of feature vectors O=o1,…,oTO = o_1, \dots, o_TO=o1​,…,oT​, one every 10 ms, and looks for the word sequence WWW that is most probable given these observations. Bayes’ rule splits this probability into two models that can be trained separately.

The acoustic model p(O∣W)p(O ∣ W)p(O∣W) says how well a word sequence explains the sound. The language model P(W)P(W)P(W) says how likely the word sequence is in the language. p(O)p(O)p(O) is the same for all WWW and drops out.

The acoustic model is trained on transcribed speech, the language model on text alone, of which far more exists. The language model resolves what the sound leaves open: “recognize speech” and “wreck a nice beach” sound almost the same, and the language model prefers the first.

The observations are the MFCC vectors of Perceptual Features: 13 coefficients from frames of 25 ms with a hop of 10 ms, extended by their first and second differences over time to 39 values. MFCCs describe the spectral envelope, the resonances of the vocal tract that distinguish one sound from another, and discard the fine structure of the harmonics. The features therefore depend little on the pitch of the speaker, which carries intonation but not the phoneme in most languages. They do not remove noise, and they do not compensate for the speaking rate; the model has to handle both.

Phoneme Models¶

The acoustic model is assembled from phonemes, the smallest sound units that distinguish words in a language, such as /p/ and /b/ in “pin” and “bin”. English has about 40 of them. Each phoneme has its own HMM with a left-to-right topology, shown in Figure 1.

The three emitting states stand for the beginning, the middle, and the end of the sound, which differ: the transition from the previous sound, the steady part, and the transition to the next. Each state either stays, with the self-loop probability aiia_{ii}aii​, or moves on to the next state; it never goes back, because speech moves forward in time. The self-loops absorb the variation of the duration: a slowly spoken vowel stays longer in each state, a fast one passes in a few frames. The entry state s0s_0s0​ emits nothing and connects the model to the end of the previous phoneme.

Each emitting state needs an emission probability bj(ot)b_j(o_t)bj​(ot​) for a 39-dimensional vector. Early systems used a discrete HMM: vector quantization with k-means (Clustering) clusters the MFCC vectors of the training data into a codebook of, for example, 256 centroids, and each frame is replaced by the index of its nearest centroid. The clusters become the observation symbols of the HMM, not its states, and the emission probabilities are tables over the 256 symbols. Quantization discards the information within a cluster, so later systems modeled the vectors directly with a Gaussian mixture per state, trained with the expectation maximization of Clustering.

The sound of a phoneme depends on its neighbors: the /k/ in “key” is formed further forward in the mouth than the /k/ in “coo”. Recognizers therefore use triphones, phoneme models that depend on the previous and the next phoneme. With 40 phonemes, there are up to 403=64,00040^3 = 64{,}000403=64,000 triphones, most of which occur rarely or never in the training data. State tying groups similar states and trains them together: a decision tree (Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting) asks questions about the neighbors, such as “is the left neighbor a nasal?”, and each leaf becomes one shared state. Large systems typically end up with a few thousand tied states.

From Phonemes to Words¶

A pronunciation dictionary lists the phonemes of every word: “speech” is /s p iː tʃ/. Concatenating the phoneme HMMs of a word gives a word HMM, and connecting the end of every word with the start of every other word gives one large HMM for all utterances. The language model contributes the transitions between words. An n-gram model estimates the probability of a word from its n−1n - 1n−1 predecessors, counted in a large text corpus and smoothed for unseen combinations (Naive Bayes):

(2)

for a trigram model. Decoding finds the most probable path through the composite HMM with the Viterbi algorithm of Hidden Markov Models, which yields the phonemes, the words, and their time stamps at once. The composite HMM has millions of states, so decoders keep only the paths whose score lies within a beam of the best one at each frame, which makes the search fast but no longer guarantees the best path. In practice, the language model score is also raised to a power, a scale factor tuned on held-out data, because the acoustic probabilities of 100 frames per second overwhelm the few word probabilities otherwise.

Training needs no time alignment. Each training utterance comes with its transcript, the dictionary turns the transcript into a chain of phoneme HMMs, and the Baum-Welch algorithm of Hidden Markov Models estimates where each phoneme starts and ends while it estimates the parameters. The dictionary also sets the limit of the vocabulary: a word that is not in it cannot be recognized at all, which hits names, new terms, and words from other languages, exactly the words that searches often contain.

Speaking Rate¶

People speak at different speeds, and the same speaker stretches some words and swallows others. A left-to-right HMM handles this with its self-loops, but the self-loops imply a particular distribution of durations. A state with the self-loop probability aaa stays for ddd frames if it loops d−1d - 1d−1 times and then leaves.

Their total duration is at least 3 frames, has a mean of 15 frames (150 ms), and is most likely 10 or 11 frames, each with a probability of 0.060. The sum of three geometric durations has a peak away from its minimum, which is closer to how long phonemes actually last.

This is one reason why phoneme models have three states rather than one. Recognizers combine three measures against variation in the speaking rate:

  • Self-loops and several states per phoneme, as above, let a phoneme cover a variable number of frames. The topology is fixed; the training estimates the self-loop probabilities.

  • Explicit duration models replace the geometric distribution with a distribution estimated from data, in hidden semi-Markov models. They describe durations better, at the price of a more expensive decoding, since the Viterbi recursion must consider every possible duration of a state.

  • Training data with varied rates lets the models learn the variation. A common augmentation adds copies of the training recordings played about 10 percent slower and faster, so the same words appear at three rates.

Speakers and Microphones¶

Two further sources of variation do not concern the rate but the voice and the recording, and two normalizations remove much of them.

A microphone, a telephone line, or a room acts as a filter: it multiplies the spectrum of the speech S(f)S(f)S(f) with a fixed transfer function H(f)H(f)H(f). The logarithm of the mel energies turns this product into a sum, and the discrete cosine transform of the MFCC computation keeps it a sum, so every cepstral vector becomes ct=st+hc_t = s_t + hct​=st​+h, the speech plus a constant vector of the channel. Cepstral mean normalization subtracts the mean over the utterance.

The normalization also removes the long-term average of the speech itself, which carries little information about the phonemes. It cannot remove additive noise, which does not become a constant in the cepstrum.

Speakers differ in the length of their vocal tract. A shorter vocal tract, as in children and in most women, shifts the resonances that distinguish vowels, the formants, to higher frequencies. Vocal tract length normalization compensates for this by warping the frequency axis before the mel filter bank, f′=αff' = \alpha ff′=αf, with a warping factor α\alphaα per speaker. The factor is chosen from a small grid, typically between 0.88 and 1.12, as the one under which the speaker’s speech is most likely for the acoustic model. Neither normalization concerns the speaking rate; both make the features of different speakers and recordings comparable.

From HMMs to Neural Networks¶

The Gaussian mixtures were the first component to be replaced. In a hybrid HMM-DNN recognizer, a deep network receives a window of several frames and outputs a probability for each tied state, P(s∣ot)P(s ∣ o_t)P(s∣ot​). The HMM needs the likelihood p(ot∣s)p(o_t ∣ s)p(ot​∣s) instead, and Bayes’ rule provides it up to a constant: p(ot∣s)∝P(s∣ot)/P(s)p(o_t ∣ s) \propto P(s ∣ o_t) / P(s)p(ot​∣s)∝P(s∣ot​)/P(s), where P(s)P(s)P(s) is the frequency of the state in the training alignment. The rest of the recognizer, the HMM topology, the dictionary, the language model, and the decoder, stays as it was. Hinton et al. (2012) summarized the results of four research groups, in which such networks outperformed Gaussian mixtures on a variety of benchmarks, sometimes by a large margin. Within a few years, hybrid systems had replaced Gaussian mixtures in most recognizers.

The next step removed the HMM and the alignment. Connectionist temporal classification (CTC, Graves et al., 2006) trains a recurrent network (Recurrent Networks) to output one symbol per frame, letters or phonemes, plus a blank symbol for “nothing new”. A frame sequence maps to a label sequence by merging repeated symbols and then deleting the blanks: “h h _ e l l _ l o” becomes “hello”, where the blank between the two l’s keeps them apart. The training objective sums the probabilities of all frame sequences that map to the transcript, with a forward-backward recursion like that of Hidden Markov Models. No phoneme boundaries are needed, and a network that outputs letters needs no pronunciation dictionary.

Encoder-decoder transformers (Model Families and Pretraining) finally map the whole spectrogram to a sequence of text tokens. Whisper, described in Transcription, is the prominent example. It produces subword tokens, so it can write words that no dictionary listed, although it may misspell a rare name. The elements of the classical pipeline remain inside: log mel features with 10 ms hops, and a decoder that acts as a language model.

Searching Spoken Content¶

Once speech is text, it enters the inverted index of Inverted Files like any document, with time stamps that let a search jump to the moment a word was spoken. Recognition errors become retrieval errors, however: a word that was misrecognized cannot be found, and a word that was inserted produces false matches. Systems mitigate this by indexing alternatives, such as the several best transcripts or a lattice of competing words with their probabilities, so that a query can match a word that was not the top choice.

Phonetic search skips the words. It indexes the recognized phoneme sequence with overlapping phoneme n-grams, like the word n-grams of Phrases and Compounds, translates the query into phonemes with the pronunciation dictionary or a grapheme-to-phoneme model, and ranks passages by the n-grams they share with the query. This finds words that the vocabulary did not contain, and it tolerates partial errors. A query for “Basel” /b ɑː z ə l/ and a transcript that heard “basil” /b æ z ə l/ share one of three phoneme trigrams and two of four bigrams, while the two words share nothing.
