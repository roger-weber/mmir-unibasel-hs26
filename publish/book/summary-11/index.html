Summary

Key Takeaways¶

  1. A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs and passes it through a nonlinear activation function. A single layer of perceptrons separates classes only with hyperplanes and cannot compute XOR; a hidden layer computes a new representation in which the classes become separable.

  2. Neural network research came in three waves: perceptrons from 1958, backpropagation from 1986, and deep learning from 2006, which won with large datasets, graphics processors, and the training techniques of C.3.

  3. Training minimizes a loss with stochastic gradient descent on mini-batches, usually with momentum. Backpropagation computes all partial derivatives in one backward pass: the delta of a neuron is the derivative of the loss with respect to its sum, hidden deltas come from the deltas of the next layer, and each weight’s gradient is its input times the delta it feeds.

  4. Sigmoid derivatives of at most 1/4 make gradients vanish in deep networks, and large weights make them explode. ReLU-type activations, He initialization, normalization layers, and residual connections keep signals and gradients at a stable scale.

  5. Regularization fights overfitting: more data through augmentation and self-supervised pretraining, early stopping, weight decay, and dropout. Adam adapts the step size of each parameter to the size of its gradients.

  6. Convolutional layers connect each neuron to a local receptive field and share the kernel across all positions. Stride and padding set the output size, pooling summarizes neighborhoods, 1×11 \times 11×1 convolutions reduce the number of features, and global average pooling turns feature maps into an embedding.

  7. The architectures from LeNet to ResNet made convolutional networks deeper and cheaper: small kernels, inception modules, residual connections, and dense connections. The Vision Transformer replaces convolutions with attention over image patches.

  8. Recurrent networks process sequences with a hidden state and are trained with backpropagation through time, where gradients vanish over long distances. LSTM and GRU cells keep information with gated additive updates; attention, and finally the transformer, removed the need for recurrence.

Further Reading¶

Textbooks¶

  • Goodfellow, I., Bengio, Y., & Courville, A. (2016). Deep learning. MIT Press. Free online edition. Chapters 6 to 10 cover feed-forward networks, regularization, optimization, convolutional networks, and recurrent networks in depth.

  • Nielsen, M. A. (2015). Neural networks and deep learning. Determination Press. Free online book. A gentle introduction that derives backpropagation step by step, explains weight decay and the vanishing gradient problem, and gives a visual proof of the universal approximation theorem of Cybenko (1989) and Hornik (1991).

From Perceptrons to Multilayer Networks¶

  • Rosenblatt, F. (1958). The perceptron: A probabilistic model for information storage and organization in the brain. Psychological Review, 65(6), 386-408. PDF. The original perceptron and its learning rule.

  • Minsky, M., & Papert, S. A. (1969). Perceptrons: An introduction to computational geometry. MIT Press; expanded edition 1988. MIT Press. The analysis of what single-layer perceptrons can and cannot compute, including XOR.

  • Hinton, G. E., Osindero, S., & Teh, Y. W. (2006). A fast learning algorithm for deep belief nets. Neural Computation, 18(7), 1527-1554. PDF. The layer-by-layer training that started the third wave of neural network research.

  • Le, Q. V., et al. (2012). Building high-level features using large scale unsupervised learning. Proceedings of the International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). arXiv. The Google Brain network trained on 16,000 processor cores that learned to detect cats without labels.

Backpropagation and Gradient Descent¶

  • Rumelhart, D. E., Hinton, G. E., & Williams, R. J. (1986). Learning representations by back-propagating errors. Nature, 323, 533-536. PDF. The paper that made backpropagation the standard training method for multilayer networks.

  • Sutskever, I., Martens, J., Dahl, G., & Hinton, G. (2013). On the importance of initialization and momentum in deep learning. Proceedings of the 30th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR 28(3), 1139-1147. PMLR. Shows how momentum schedules and a careful initialization make plain SGD train deep networks.

Training Deep Networks¶

  • Glorot, X., & Bengio, Y. (2010). Understanding the difficulty of training deep feedforward neural networks. Proceedings of the 13th International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Statistics (AISTATS), PMLR 9, 249-256. PMLR. Analyzes vanishing activations and gradients and proposes the Glorot initialization.

  • Nair, V., & Hinton, G. E. (2010). Rectified linear units improve restricted Boltzmann machines. Proceedings of the 27th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). PDF. Introduces the rectified linear unit to neural networks.

  • He, K., Zhang, X., Ren, S., & Sun, J. (2015). Delving deep into rectifiers: Surpassing human-level performance on ImageNet classification. Proceedings of the IEEE International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV). arXiv. The parametric ReLU and the He initialization for ReLU networks.

  • Clevert, D.-A., Unterthiner, T., & Hochreiter, S. (2016). Fast and accurate deep network learning by exponential linear units (ELUs). International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). arXiv. The exponential linear unit.

  • Hendrycks, D., & Gimpel, K. (2016). Gaussian error linear units (GELUs). arXiv. The activation function of BERT and GPT.

  • Ioffe, S., & Szegedy, C. (2015). Batch normalization: Accelerating deep network training by reducing internal covariate shift. Proceedings of the 32nd International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). arXiv. Normalization over the mini-batch with learned scale and shift.

  • Ba, J. L., Kiros, J. R., & Hinton, G. E. (2016). Layer normalization. arXiv. Normalization over the features of each example, the variant used in transformers and recurrent networks.

  • Wu, Y., & He, K. (2018). Group normalization. Proceedings of the European Conference on Computer Vision (ECCV). arXiv. Normalization over groups of features, for convolutional networks trained with small batches.

  • Srivastava, N., Hinton, G., Krizhevsky, A., Sutskever, I., & Salakhutdinov, R. (2014). Dropout: A simple way to prevent neural networks from overfitting. Journal of Machine Learning Research, 15, 1929-1958. JMLR. Dropout and its interpretation as an average over many thinned networks.

  • Kingma, D. P., & Ba, J. (2015). Adam: A method for stochastic optimization. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). arXiv. The Adam optimizer with its bias correction.

  • Loshchilov, I., & Hutter, F. (2019). Decoupled weight decay regularization. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). arXiv. AdamW, which separates weight decay from the adaptive step.

  • Hoffmann, J., et al. (2022). Training compute-optimal large language models. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 35. arXiv. The Chinchilla study behind the rule of about 20 training tokens per parameter.

CNN Architectures¶

  • Rowley, H. A., Baluja, S., & Kanade, T. (1998). Neural network-based face detection. IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 20(1), 23-38. PDF. The upright detector with hand-designed receptive fields, preprocessing, and bootstrapped nonface examples.

  • Rowley, H. A., Baluja, S., & Kanade, T. (1998). Rotation invariant neural network-based face detection. Proceedings of the IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), 38-44. PDF. Adds the router network that estimates the rotation of a face before detection.

  • LeCun, Y., Bottou, L., Bengio, Y., & Haffner, P. (1998). Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition. Proceedings of the IEEE, 86(11), 2278-2324. IEEE Xplore. The full description of LeNet-5 and its comparison with other classifiers on MNIST.

  • Krizhevsky, A., Sutskever, I., & Hinton, G. E. (2012). ImageNet classification with deep convolutional neural networks. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 25. NeurIPS proceedings. AlexNet: ReLU, dropout, data augmentation, and training on two GPUs.

  • Simonyan, K., & Zisserman, A. (2015). Very deep convolutional networks for large-scale image recognition. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). arXiv. The VGG networks and the argument for stacks of 3×33 \times 33×3 convolutions.

  • Szegedy, C., Liu, W., Jia, Y., Sermanet, P., Reed, S., Anguelov, D., Erhan, D., Vanhoucke, V., & Rabinovich, A. (2015). Going deeper with convolutions. Proceedings of the IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), 1-9. arXiv. GoogLeNet, the inception module, and auxiliary classifiers.

  • He, K., Zhang, X., Ren, S., & Sun, J. (2016). Deep residual learning for image recognition. Proceedings of the IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), 770-778. arXiv. The degradation problem, residual blocks, and the bottleneck design up to 152 layers.

  • Huang, G., Liu, Z., van der Maaten, L., & Weinberger, K. Q. (2017). Densely connected convolutional networks. Proceedings of the IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), 2261-2269. arXiv. Dense blocks, growth rate, and transition layers.

  • Dosovitskiy, A., Beyer, L., Kolesnikov, A., Weissenborn, D., Zhai, X., Unterthiner, T., Dehghani, M., Minderer, M., Heigold, G., Gelly, S., Uszkoreit, J., & Houlsby, N. (2021). An image is worth 16x16 words: Transformers for image recognition at scale. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). arXiv. The Vision Transformer and the role of pretraining data size.

  • Zhang, A., Lipton, Z. C., Li, M., & Smola, A. J. (2023). Dive into deep learning. Cambridge University Press. Chapter “Modern Convolutional Neural Networks”. Free online textbook with runnable implementations of AlexNet, VGG, GoogLeNet, ResNet, and DenseNet; the source of several figures in C.5.

Recurrent Networks¶

  • Hochreiter, S., & Schmidhuber, J. (1997). Long short-term memory. Neural Computation, 9(8), 1735-1780. PDF. The LSTM cell with its gated cell state.

  • Cho, K., van Merriënboer, B., Gulcehre, C., Bahdanau, D., Bougares, F., Schwenk, H., & Bengio, Y. (2014). Learning phrase representations using RNN encoder-decoder for statistical machine translation. Proceedings of the Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing (EMNLP). arXiv. The encoder-decoder architecture and the gated recurrent unit.

  • Bahdanau, D., Cho, K., & Bengio, Y. (2015). Neural machine translation by jointly learning to align and translate. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). arXiv. The attention mechanism that lets the decoder look back at all encoder states.

  • Olah, C. (2015). Understanding LSTM networks. Blog post. colah.github.io. A visual explanation of recurrent networks and LSTM gates, with diagrams in the style of the unrolled network in C.6.

  • Gu, A., & Dao, T. (2023). Mamba: Linear-time sequence modeling with selective state spaces. arXiv. A state-space model that revives the idea of a compact recurrent state for very long sequences.
