6 - Vector Search

The previous chapter turned text into embeddings: dense vectors whose geometry captures meaning, so that “heart disease” and “cardiology treatment” land close together even though they share no words. Images, audio, and video can be encoded the same way. Once every document and every query is a point in the same space, retrieval becomes a geometric question: which stored points lie closest to the query point? Figure 1 makes this concrete, showing a 2D projection of a word-embedding space in which related terms fall into the same neighborhood, so that proximity stands in for similarity in meaning.

That question is easy to state and expensive to answer. A collection may hold millions or billions of vectors, each with hundreds or thousands of dimensions. Comparing the query against every stored vector is correct but slow, and the classical spatial indexes that accelerate low-dimensional search collapse as the number of dimensions grows. That leaves a gap between the only method guaranteed to be correct, the full scan, and the speed a real system needs. This chapter is about closing it: how to measure closeness, why exact search becomes hopeless in high dimensions, and how approximate methods recover speed by giving up a little accuracy that the application never needed.

The vectors themselves arrive from more than one source. We already have embeddings from learned models, built in the previous chapter; other applications extract vectors straight from the media, such as a color histogram of an image. Whatever their origin, the question this chapter asks is how to compare them, and there are two ways to do it. A similarity score, most often the dot product or cosine, rewards a close match with a larger value. A distance, such as Euclidean distance, marks a close match with a smaller value. The two are two sides of the same coin: a simple normalization turns one into the other, so the engineering problem, finding the closest points quickly, is the same whichever we use.

What you’ll learn

  • Explain how dot product, cosine similarity, and distance measures express closeness, and convert between them.

  • Describe how classical spatial indexes (Voronoi diagrams, grid files, R-trees) answer nearest-neighbor queries.

  • Explain the curse of dimensionality and why it forces exact search back to a full scan.

  • Compare approximate nearest-neighbor methods (IVF, HNSW, LSH, product quantization) and their recall-speed trade-off.

  • Choose a vector-search framework and combine similarity search with metadata filters.

Prerequisites

  • Semantic Search: how models place meaning in a vector space. See Word Embeddings and Building a Semantic Search System.

  • Performance Evaluation: recall, which reappears here as the quality axis of approximate search.

  • Basic linear algebra: vectors, dot products, norms, and eigenvectors.
