Key Takeaways¶

Human Hearing and Sound Physics¶

  1. Sound is a pressure wave that needs a medium. Its speed is about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, 1,482 m/s in water, and higher in solids, and λ⋅f=v\lambda \cdot f = vλ⋅f=v gives wavelengths from 17.1 m at 20 Hz to 17.1 mm at 20 kHz.

  2. Pitch, loudness, duration, timbre, sonic texture, and spatial location are perceptions with physical correlates, not measurements: the ear hears a missing fundamental, fills short gaps, and finds tones near 3 kHz louder than others of the same level.

  3. The ear canal resonates near 3.4 kHz and boosts it by about 10 to 20 dB. The middle ear passes the sound from the air into the fluid of the cochlea, where a direct transition would transmit only about 0.1 percent of the energy.

  4. The basilar membrane analyzes frequencies by place, high frequencies at the base and low ones at the apex. The Greenwood map grows with log⁡(1+f/146 Hz)\log(1 + f / 146\,\text{Hz})log(1+f/146Hz), nearly linear at low and logarithmic at high frequencies, the same form as the mel scale with its knee at 700 Hz.

  5. Outer hair cells amplify quiet sounds more than loud ones, inner hair cells transduce with a saturating response, and the nerve fibers adapt to constant sounds. Compression and adaptation are why audio features take logarithms of energies and why the onset strength counts only increases.

  6. The primary auditory pathway runs from the cochlear nuclei through the superior olive, which compares the two ears, the inferior colliculus, and the thalamus to the tonotopic auditory cortex. Time differences of at most about 0.66 ms locate low frequencies, level differences caused by the head shadow locate high ones.

Extended Audio and Music Features¶

  1. The entropy of energy splits a frame into sub-frames and computes the entropy of their normalized energies. It is close to log⁡2L\log_2 Llog2​L for steady sounds and low for abrupt events.

  2. A spectrum bin maps to its pitch class with ⌊9.5+12log⁡2(ω(k)/440)⌋ mod 12⌊ 9.5 + 12 \log_2(\omega(k)/440) ⌋ \text{ mod } 12⌊9.5+12log2​(ω(k)/440)⌋ mod 12, which rounds the note number, and to its octave with an offset of 4. A bin spacing Δf\Delta fΔf separates semitones only above about 16.8 Δf16.8 \, \Delta f16.8Δf.

  3. The harmonic sum estimates the fundamental as the candidate whose harmonics collect the largest weighted magnitude. Without weights, half the true fundamental collects the same peaks; the weights of Klapuri (2006) prevent this octave error.

  4. Several simultaneous pitches follow by iteration: estimate the strongest fundamental, subtract its harmonics, and repeat. Shared partials are the difficulty.

  5. The autocorrelation estimates the fundamental as fs/m0f_s / m_0fs​/m0​ for the lag m0m_0m0​ with the highest correlation; its resolution is limited by integer lags.

  6. The autocorrelation of the onset strength has peaks at the beat period and its multiples. A log-Gaussian tempo prior around 0.5 s (120 bpm) with a width of 1.4 octaves selects the period a listener would most likely tap. The tempogram computes the autocorrelation in a sliding window and shows tempo changes.

  7. Ellis’s beat tracker maximizes the onset strength at the beats plus α\alphaα times a log-ratio penalty on the intervals, by dynamic programming in linear time. Too small an α\alphaα follows accents and off-beats; a larger one keeps the beats regular.

Speech Recognition with HMMs¶

  1. A classical recognizer chooses the word sequence that maximizes p(O∣W)⋅P(W)p(O ∣ W) \cdot P(W)p(O∣W)⋅P(W): an acoustic model trained on transcribed speech times a language model trained on text.

  2. Each phoneme has a left-to-right HMM with three emitting states. Emissions were first discrete symbols from a k-means codebook, then Gaussian mixtures; triphones with decision-tree state tying model the influence of the neighbors.

  3. A pronunciation dictionary and an n-gram language model connect the phoneme models into one large HMM, which Viterbi decoding with a beam searches. Training needs only transcripts, no time alignment, but the dictionary limits the vocabulary.

  4. A self-loop gives a geometric duration whose most likely value is one frame. Several states per phoneme, explicit duration models, and training data at varied rates handle the speaking rate.

  5. Cepstral mean normalization removes the constant effect of a microphone or a line, and vocal tract length normalization warps the frequency axis per speaker. Neither addresses the speaking rate.

  6. Hybrid HMM-DNN systems replaced the Gaussian mixtures with networks whose state posteriors, divided by the state priors, act as likelihoods. CTC removed the alignment, and encoder-decoder transformers such as Whisper replaced the whole chain.

  7. Spoken content becomes searchable through its transcript or through phoneme n-grams; the latter finds words outside the vocabulary and tolerates partial recognition errors.

Further Reading¶

Human Hearing and Sound Physics¶

  • Chittka, L., & Brockmann, A. (2005). Perception space: The final frontier. PLoS Biology, 3(4), e137. PMC. An essay on how the sensory worlds of animals differ from ours; the source of the ear diagram in Figure 1.

  • Greenwood, D. D. (1990). A cochlear frequency-position function for several species: 29 years later. Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 87(6), 2592-2605. PubMed. The frequency map of the cochlea used in F.1, with constants for humans and other species.

  • Heffner, H. E., & Heffner, R. S. (2007). Hearing ranges of laboratory animals. Journal of the American Association for Laboratory Animal Science, 46(1), 20-22. PubMed. How the audible range differs between species.

Extended Audio and Music Features¶

  • Giannakopoulos, T. (2015). pyAudioAnalysis: An open-source Python library for audio signal analysis. PLoS One, 10(12), e0144610. PMC. A library with the entropy of energy, the spectral entropy, and the other short-term features of Ch 11.

  • Klapuri, A. (2006). Multiple fundamental frequency estimation by summing harmonic amplitudes. Proceedings of the 7th International Conference on Music Information Retrieval (ISMIR). The weighted harmonic sum and the iterative estimation of several pitches.

  • Ellis, D. P. W. (2007). Beat tracking by dynamic programming. Journal of New Music Research, 36(1), 51-60. PDF. The onset strength, the tempo prior, and the beat-tracking objective of F.2, with Figure 1.

  • McFee, B., et al. (2015). librosa: Audio and music signal analysis in Python. Proceedings of the 14th Python in Science Conference (SciPy). Paper. The library that implements the onset strength, the tempogram, the tempo prior, and Ellis’s beat tracker.

  • Müller, M. (2021). Fundamentals of music processing (2nd ed.). Springer. FMP notebooks. A textbook on chroma, tempo, and beat tracking, with free Python notebooks for every chapter.

Speech Recognition with HMMs¶

  • Rabiner (1989), the classic tutorial on the forward, Viterbi, and Baum-Welch algorithms, is listed with the other HMM references in Further Reading of B.

  • Gales, M., & Young, S. (2008). The application of hidden Markov models in speech recognition. Foundations and Trends in Signal Processing, 1(3), 195-304. PDF. The complete classical recognizer: features, triphones and state tying, language models, decoding, and normalization and adaptation.

  • Hinton, G., et al. (2012). Deep neural networks for acoustic modeling in speech recognition: The shared views of four research groups. IEEE Signal Processing Magazine, 29(6), 82-97. PDF. The hybrid HMM-DNN recognizer.

  • Graves, A., Fernández, S., Gomez, F., & Schmidhuber, J. (2006). Connectionist temporal classification: Labelling unsegmented sequence data with recurrent neural networks. Proceedings of the 23rd International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). PDF. Training without an alignment, with the blank symbol.

  • Jurafsky, D., & Martin, J. H. Speech and language processing (3rd ed. draft), Chapter 16: Automatic speech recognition and text-to-speech. PDF. A modern treatment from features to encoder-decoder models and CTC.

  • Radford, A., et al. (2023). Robust speech recognition via large-scale weak supervision. Proceedings of the 40th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML). arXiv. Whisper, the encoder-decoder transformer of Transcription.
