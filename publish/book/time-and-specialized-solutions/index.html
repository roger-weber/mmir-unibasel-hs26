Time-Based Media and Specialized Solutions

A sports editor needs the goal from last night’s match for the evening news. The archive holds the full recording: 90 minutes of play from several cameras, the stadium sound, and the live commentary. A search that returns “the match” is useless; the editor wants the 20 seconds around the goal. A music fan holds a phone towards the loudspeaker in a noisy bar and wants to know which song is playing. A search that returns “songs that sound similar” is useless too; the fan wants the one recording that is playing.

Both requests break assumptions of the previous sections. The first one adds time: the content unfolds over minutes or hours, and the user wants a moment, not a file. The second one asks for exact identification under heavy distortion, a task that general-purpose features and embeddings do not solve. This section covers both.

Time as a New Dimension¶

Images are static: the whole content is present at once. Audio and video unfold over time. They have a structure of transitions and segments, and a single file may contain hundreds of different moments. A user who searches a video collection usually wants a specific segment, just as a user of a document collection wants the relevant passage rather than a book of 500 pages. Time-based media therefore need the same step that Chunking Strategies introduced for long texts: split the content into retrieval units, index the units, and return the matching units with their position in the file.

Video has a natural hierarchy of such units:

  • A frame is a single still image. At 25 to 60 frames per second, a video contains far too many frames to index them all individually, and neighboring frames are nearly identical.

  • A shot is a continuous sequence of frames recorded by one camera without interruption. Shots are separated by cuts, where one frame switches abruptly to a different view, or by gradual transitions such as fades, dissolves, and wipes. The shot is the basic building block for indexing: a few representative keyframes per shot can be analyzed with the image techniques of the previous sections.

  • A scene groups consecutive shots that share a location, a time, or a continuous action. A dialogue filmed with alternating views of the two speakers consists of many shots but forms one scene.

  • An episode is a complete film or one part of a series, usually stored as a separate file with its own metadata.

Detecting shot boundaries is a perceptual task: the content of the frames changes abruptly or gradually. Detecting scenes is harder, because shots of the same scene can look very different. It requires semantic understanding of who is present, where, and what happens.

Audio needs units too, but it has no natural boundaries like cuts. Instead, the signal is split into overlapping frames of fixed length, for example 40 ms with a hop of 20 ms, and each frame yields a feature vector. Frames are then grouped into longer segments, for example 4 seconds with a hop of 100 ms, and statistics over the frame features describe each segment. A 4-second segment of 40 ms frames with a 20 ms hop contains (4000−40)/20+1=199(4000 - 40) / 20 + 1 = 199(4000−40)/20+1=199 frames. The overlap plays the same role as the overlap of text chunks: an event that falls on the boundary of one unit lies in the middle of the next. Time also brings features that do not exist in a single image. Spatio-temporal features describe motion and activity: optical flow measures how pixels move between consecutive frames and reveals both object motion and camera motion. Three-dimensional convolutional networks extend convolutions from the image plane into time and learn features of short video volumes. Such features answer queries that no single frame can answer, such as clips of people running, car chases, or unusual movement in surveillance footage.

The modalities of a video are aligned on a common time line, and this alignment is a powerful retrieval tool. A transcript produced by speech recognition carries timestamps, so a text hit in the transcript leads directly to the moment in the video. The stadium sound and the commentary of the sports match locate the goal: the crowd noise rises sharply in energy, and the commentator speaks faster and at a higher pitch. A system that detects these audio events finds candidate highlights before it analyzes a single frame. Cross-modal transformers fuse frames, sound, and text over time and can answer queries such as “the moment when the reporter says X while standing in front of Y”. Very long videos remain a challenge: the sequences are long, the computational cost is high, and one noisy modality, such as unrelated background music, can mislead the fusion.

Specialized Solutions¶

The general approaches of this chapter describe content in a way that serves many queries. Some tasks have requirements that these descriptions cannot meet. Identifying a song from a phone recording is the classic example.

The query is a 10-second recording from a bar, with conversation, clinking glasses, and a phone microphone that compresses and distorts the sound. The catalogue holds millions of studio recordings. The answer must be exactly one recording: not a similar song, not another version by the same artist. Comparing waveforms fails because noise changes every sample. Comparing spectral envelopes fails for the same reason. An embedding of musical similarity, in the spirit of Multimodal Transformers, would happily return other songs of the same genre and mood. The task needs features that ignore heavy distortion and are at the same time specific enough to distinguish millions of recordings, and it needs a lookup in the full catalogue that answers within a second.

Audio fingerprinting, the technique behind Shazam, meets these requirements. It extracts a compact fingerprint from the parts of the signal that survive noise and compression, precomputes the fingerprints of all catalogued recordings, and looks up the fingerprint of a query in a hash index. Figure 2 (left) names the stages of the pipeline; the chapter on audio features explains how each stage works and why it is robust.

The specialization has a price. Fingerprinting matches recordings, not songs. When users sing a song themselves, their pitch, timing, and timbre differ from the recording, and the fingerprint no longer matches. Live performances with different instruments, tempo, and audience noise often fail for the same reason. Query by humming needs a different solution that compares melodies rather than recordings. A specialized solution solves its task very well and little else.

Music streaming services show the opposite strategy: they combine many signals. Figure 2 (right) lists the machine learning applications of Spotify, from personalized recommendations to content classification. To recommend a track, the service combines acoustic features such as tempo, key, timbre, and energy, higher-level attributes such as genre, mood, and instrumentation, text such as artist information, user tags, and lyrics, and the listening behavior of millions of users. A listener may like a song for the timbre of the voice, the rhythm, the lyrics, or its cultural associations, so the system must model several kinds of similarity at once.

Other specialized solutions follow the same pattern. Face recognition identifies persons across an archive with dedicated face embeddings. Perceptual hashing finds near-duplicate images, such as resized or recompressed copies of a photo. Copyright detection on video platforms matches uploads against fingerprints of protected content. In each case, a precisely defined task with strict requirements on robustness, exactness, or speed justifies a dedicated method.

When to look for a specialized solution

General features and embeddings answer “what is similar to this?” A specialized method is worth considering when the task asks “which exact item is this?”, when the query is heavily distorted, or when a very large catalogue must answer within a second.
