8 - Web Search

Type “uni basel” into a search engine. Even two short words are a strong filter: of the hundreds of billions of pages in the index, almost all contain neither term and drop out. But a tiny fraction of the web is still thousands to millions of pages: the university’s home page, every course and news page, student blogs, a marathon result list with a runner from Basel. The ranking functions of the earlier chapters score these pages by how well their words match the query, and for a two-word query most of them match about equally well. Yet the user expects the home page at rank 1, and expects it within a fraction of a second. The words of the query cannot decide this ranking on their own.

Four problems make the web the hardest setting for retrieval in this book:

  • Too many equal matches. A short query discards almost the whole web but still leaves far more matching pages than anyone can read, with nearly equal term-based scores.

  • Different intents, one result list. Queries range from a site name such as “instagram”, typed instead of a URL, to a full question such as “Who was Albert Einstein?”. Each intent expects a different kind of result (Intent Routing and Classification). With two or three words and no context, the engine cannot know what one particular user wants, so it serves what most users want. For “apple”, that is the home page of Apple, not an article about the fruit.

  • No editor. The collections of earlier chapters were curated: nobody adds hundreds of useless books to a university library catalogue to promote their own. The web has no such gatekeeper. Its content ranges from careful reference works to outdated or false information, clickbait, and scams, and much of it exists in many copies, such as mirrored manuals, the documentation of popular frameworks, and copied blog posts. The first web search engines learned the hard way that term matching ranks such pages high.

  • Adversarial ranking. A position on the first result page is worth money, because results on the second page are almost never clicked. Search engine optimization (SEO) is an industry that tunes pages to rank higher, from legitimate improvements to plain manipulation. Unlike in a library, no central authority decides which document deserves the top position, and every signal that rewards a page invites someone to fake it.

If the keywords cannot decide, the engine needs evidence from elsewhere. The web offers more of it than any curated collection:

  • The query intent. The engine detects the type of query and applies a strategy for it. A navigational query must lead to the official website every time; for frequent queries, this can come from a curated list, much like the entries of an FAQ service.

  • The page and what others say about it. Term positions, the HTML structure, and the anchor texts of the links that point to a page (Ranking Signals Beyond Term Counts).

  • The link graph. Popular pages receive more links, so the link structure shows what people in general consider important (Link Analysis: PageRank and HITS). The goal is not to optimize every single query, but to make the great majority of the queries people actually type work very well.

  • User behaviour. Clicks, the time users spend on a result, and reformulated queries show which results satisfy. Learning to rank combines hundreds of such signals into one model that keeps adapting as behaviour changes (Learning to Rank).

The intent also decides what the result page looks like. Instead of one list of links, the engine sends the query to specialized searches and composes the page for it: a highlighted passage or a generated answer for a question, facts from a curated knowledge base for a known entity, a map for a place, a product list for a purchase, and related searches for the next step. All of this rests on a crawler that does more than copy the web: by following its links, it collects the evidence about each page that the page itself does not provide (The Web Search Landscape).

The same machinery now works without a query at all. Social media feeds and video platforms decide, for every user and every scroll, which post to show next. They retrieve and rank candidates just like a search engine, but the user’s history plays the role of the query, and the reactions of communities and individual users train a ranking that is personalized for every user (Ranking Without a Query: Feeds).

What you’ll learn

  • Explain why term-based ranking breaks down on the web (equal matches, diverse intents, uncurated quality, SEO), how the query intent shapes the result page, and what a crawler contributes beyond collecting pages.

  • Compute web-specific content signals: term proximity, BM25F field weighting, and per-domain anchor-text scores.

  • Compute PageRank by power iteration and HITS hub and authority values, and contrast global with query-specific link analysis.

  • Compare pointwise, pairwise, and listwise learning to rank, and explain how click data must be debiased before training.

  • Explain how a feed ranks content without a query, and why optimizing engagement differs from optimizing value.

Prerequisites

  • Classical Text Retrieval: the vector space model and BM25, which web signals extend (see BM25).

  • Performance Evaluation: graded relevance and nDCG, the target measure of learning to rank (see Evaluating Graded Relevance).

  • Index for Text Retrieval: inverted files and postings lists, which proximity scoring extends with term positions.

  • Semantic Search and Vector Search: embeddings, cross-encoders, and approximate nearest-neighbour search, reused for neural reranking and feed retrieval (see Semantic Similarity with Transformers).

  • Retrieval Augmented Generation: generated answers, which modern result pages show above the links.
