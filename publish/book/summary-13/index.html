Summary

Key Takeaways¶

The Human Visual System¶

  1. The light that reaches the eye is the product of illumination and reflectance, f=l⋅rf = l \cdot rf=l⋅r. Illumination varies by a factor of 100,000 and more, reflectance by about one hundred, and the visual system estimates reflectance rather than the product (lightness constancy).

  2. Three cone types with peaks near 420, 534, and 564 nm provide color vision in daylight; one rod type with a peak near 498 nm provides night vision without color. The L cone peaks in the yellow-green, not in the red.

  3. A retina holds on average 4.6 million cones and 92 million rods. S cones are rare (about 7 percent near the fovea), and the L:M ratio varies widely between people with normal color vision.

  4. Normal acuity of 20/20 means resolving details of 1 arcminute, or 30 cycles per degree; healthy young adults see better, and the limit of the eye is about 60 cycles per degree. Eagles resolve about twice as much detail; cats, dogs, rodents, and insects resolve much less.

  5. Retinal ganglion cells have center-surround receptive fields that respond to local contrast, not to brightness. Their computation is a convolution with a kernel whose weights sum to zero.

  6. V1 contains simple cells, which detect oriented edges at a position, and complex cells, which tolerate shifts. This hierarchy inspired the Neocognitron and, through it, convolutional networks with convolution and pooling layers.

  7. Beyond V1, the ventral stream recognizes objects and the dorsal stream processes motion and location; cortical magnification devotes most of the cortex to the fovea.

Color Systems¶

  1. Each cone type integrates the spectrum weighted by its sensitivity, so color reduces a continuous spectrum to three numbers. Different spectra with the same three numbers look identical (metamers), which is also what lets three primaries reproduce colors.

  2. CIE XYZ integrates the spectrum with the color matching functions of the 1931 standard observer. YYY is the luminance, all visible colors have non-negative coordinates, and the XYZ primaries are imaginary.

  3. The chromaticity (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) normalizes XYZ by its sum and removes brightness. It does not change when the light gets brighter or darker, which makes it useful for shadow removal.

  4. sRGB uses the Rec. 709 primaries, the D65 white point, and a nonlinear transfer function with an approximate gamma of 2.2. The encoding subtracts 0.055, and matrices convert only between XYZ and linear RGB, never sRGB values directly.

  5. CIE Lab compresses the ratios to the white point with a cube root to make Euclidean distances approximately perceptual. The four-box example shows equal sRGB steps of 100 as Lab distances of 42.7, 59.8, and 27.7.

  6. HSL and HSV are cylindrical versions of the RGB cube. They separate hue from brightness, but they are not perceptually uniform, and their saturation values differ for the same color.

Extended Image Features¶

  1. Raw sums of the first four powers and of the channel products yield all moments and covariances in a single pass. The formulas cancel large terms, so they need 64-bit accumulators, shifted data, or update formulas that keep central moments.

  2. The quadratic distance becomes a squared Euclidean distance after the transformation Λ1/2U⊤\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}\mathbf{U}^\topΛ1/2U⊤, provided that A\mathbf{A}A is positive semidefinite. Dropping axes with small eigenvalues gives a lower bound for a filter step.

  3. The matrix ai,j=1−di,j/max⁡da_{i,j} = 1 - d_{i,j}/\max dai,j​=1−di,j​/maxd is not positive semidefinite in general. For normalized histograms, centering the matrix fixes this without changing any distance; a Gaussian kernel is positive definite by construction.

  4. Gradient moments summarize magnitude and direction with nine values, but the direction is an angle and needs circular treatment.

  5. Laws’ texture energy derives 16 kernels from four prototype vectors and reduces them to 9 energy maps. Symmetric pairs are combined by averaging their energy maps, not their kernels. The maps give 512-bin histograms or 72 moments.

  6. In Fourier space, a Gabor filter is a Gaussian blob at distance 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ from the center in direction θ\thetaθ; a filter bank covers a half circle of directions and a few rings of scales.

  7. FFTs handle any image size and are fastest for sizes with small prime factors. Padding avoids the wrap-around of circular convolution. Libraries choose between direct and FFT-based convolution automatically.

Further Reading¶

The Human Visual System¶

  • Hubel, D. H., & Wiesel, T. N. (1962). Receptive fields, binocular interaction and functional architecture in the cat’s visual cortex. The Journal of Physiology, 160(1), 106-154. PubMed Central. The classic description of simple and complex cells in V1, the starting point of the hierarchy that convolutional networks imitate.

  • Fukushima, K. (1980). Neocognitron: A self-organizing neural network model for a mechanism of pattern recognition unaffected by shift in position. Biological Cybernetics, 36(4), 193-202. PDF. Turns the simple and complex cells into a layered network of S-cells and C-cells, the direct ancestor of convolution and pooling layers.

  • Curcio, C. A., Sloan, K. R., Kalina, R. E., & Hendrickson, A. E. (1990). Human photoreceptor topography. Journal of Comparative Neurology, 292(4), 497-523. PubMed. The source of the rod and cone counts and the foveal cone density used in E.1.

  • Curcio, C. A., Allen, K. A., Sloan, K. R., Lerea, C. L., Hurley, J. B., Klock, I. B., & Milam, A. H. (1991). Distribution and morphology of human cone photoreceptors stained with anti-blue opsin. Journal of Comparative Neurology, 312, 610-624. PubMed. Shows that S cones make up about 7 percent of the cones and are missing at the very center of the fovea.

  • Curcio, C. A., & Allen, K. A. (1990). Topography of ganglion cells in human retina. Journal of Comparative Neurology, 300(1), 5-25. PubMed. Counts 0.7 to 1.5 million ganglion cells per retina and links cortical magnification to ganglion cell density.

  • Hofer, H., Carroll, J., Neitz, J., Neitz, M., & Williams, D. R. (2005). Organization of the human trichromatic cone mosaic. The Journal of Neuroscience, 25(42), 9669-9679. PubMed Central. Images of the cone mosaic in living eyes, with L:M ratios from 1.1:1 to 16.5:1 among people with normal color vision.

  • Goodale, M. A., & Milner, A. D. (1992). Separate visual pathways for perception and action. Trends in Neurosciences, 15(1), 20-25. PubMed. The proposal of the ventral stream for recognition and the dorsal stream for visually guided action.

  • Caves, E. M., Brandley, N. C., & Johnsen, S. (2018). Visual acuity and the evolution of signals. Trends in Ecology & Evolution, 33(5), 358-372. DOI. A survey of acuity across animals in cycles per degree, showing that most species see far less detail than humans.

  • Reymond, L. (1985). Spatial visual acuity of the eagle Aquila audax: A behavioural, optical and anatomical investigation. Vision Research, 25(10), 1477-1491. PubMed. The measurement behind the eagle’s acuity of about 140 cycles per degree, twice the human limit.

  • Allison, W. T., Haimberger, T. J., Hawryshyn, C. W., & Temple, S. E. (2004). Visual pigment composition in zebrafish: Evidence for a rhodopsin-porphyropsin interchange system. Visual Neuroscience, 21(6), 945-952. PubMed. Microspectrophotometry of zebrafish photoreceptors, the source of the four measured cone peaks from 361 to 565 nm.

  • Kolb, H., et al. Webvision: The organization of the retina and visual system. Online textbook. webvision.pitt.edu. A free, detailed reference on the cell types and circuits of the retina, including bipolar, ganglion, horizontal, and amacrine cells.

Color Systems¶

  • Smith, T., & Guild, J. (1931). The C.I.E. colorimetric standards and their use. Transactions of the Optical Society, 33(3), 73-134. IOP Science. The original description of the 1931 standard observer and of the XYZ system that every later CIE space builds on.

  • CIE (2018). Colorimetry (4th ed., CIE 015:2018). International Commission on Illumination. CIE. The current standard for XYZ, xyY, and Lab, including the official tables; not free of charge.

  • Colour & Vision Research Laboratory. CVRL database: Colour matching functions. University College London. cvrl.ucl.ac.uk. Free tables of the CIE 1931 color matching functions in 1 nm steps, the data behind the metamer example in E.2.

  • Stokes, M., Anderson, M., Chandrasekar, S., & Motta, R. (1996). A standard default color space for the Internet: sRGB (Version 1.10). W3C. w3.org. The original proposal by HP and Microsoft that became IEC 61966-2-1, with the primaries and the transfer function.

  • ITU-R (2015). Recommendation BT.2020-2: Parameter values for ultra-high definition television systems for production and international programme exchange. International Telecommunication Union. ITU. Defines the wide-gamut Rec. 2020 primaries used in E.2.

  • Smith, A. R. (1978). Color gamut transform pairs. Computer Graphics (SIGGRAPH '78 Proceedings), 12(3), 12-19. PDF. Introduces the HSV hexcone model and the fast conversion between RGB and HSV.

  • Sharma, G., Wu, W., & Dalal, E. N. (2005). The CIEDE2000 color-difference formula: Implementation notes, supplementary test data, and mathematical observations. Color Research & Application, 30(1), 21-30. PDF. The reference implementation of CIEDE2000, the corrected successor of the Euclidean distance in Lab.

Extended Image Features¶

  • Laws, K. I. (1980). Rapid texture identification. Proceedings of SPIE, 238, 376-381. SPIE Digital Library. Describes texture energy measures computed with small convolution masks, the basis of the method in E.3.

  • Stricker, M. A., & Orengo, M. (1995). Similarity of color images. Storage and Retrieval for Image and Video Databases III, Proceedings of SPIE, 2420. SPIE Digital Library. Introduces color moments as a compact alternative to color histograms, the features whose one-pass computation E.3 derives.

  • Hafner, J., Sawhney, H. S., Equitz, W., Flickner, M., & Niblack, W. (1995). Efficient color histogram indexing for quadratic form distance functions. IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 17(7), 729-736. IEEE Xplore. Shows how cheap lower bounds of the quadratic histogram distance prefilter a large image collection before the exact distance is computed.

  • Manjunath, B. S., & Ma, W. Y. (1996). Texture features for browsing and retrieval of image data. IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, 18(8), 837-842. IEEE Xplore. The standard reference for Gabor texture features in image retrieval, with a comparison against other multiresolution texture features.

  • Frigo, M., & Johnson, S. G. (2005). The design and implementation of FFTW3. Proceedings of the IEEE, 93(2), 216-231. PDF. Explains how a modern FFT library computes transforms of any size, including primes, in O(nlog⁡n)O(n \log n)O(nlogn) time.

  • Pébay, P. P. (2008). Formulas for robust, one-pass parallel computation of covariances and arbitrary-order statistical moments (Report SAND2008-6212). Sandia National Laboratories. OSTI. Update formulas for central moments that avoid the cancellation of the raw-sum approach and merge results computed in parallel.
