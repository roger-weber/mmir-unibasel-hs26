Decision Trees and Gradient Boosting

Where this is used

Classifying Audio separates speech from music with the decision tree of Castan (2010), learned with the C4.5 algorithm described here. The learning-to-rank models of Learning to Rank, with LambdaMART as the best-known example, are ensembles of gradient-boosted trees. Intent Routing and Classification notes that production query classifiers have moved from Naive Bayes to gradient-boosted trees.

A decision tree classifies an example with a sequence of simple tests, such as “is the age below 25?”, and the answers lead to a label. Trees are easy to read for humans and fast to evaluate for machines, they handle discrete and continuous features without normalization, and they can be written down as rules. A single tree is rarely the best classifier, but ensembles of trees are among the most accurate methods for tabular data today. This section shows how a tree is learned from data with the ID3 and C4.5 algorithms, how pruning keeps it from overfitting, and how random forests and gradient boosting combine many trees.

Classification Trees¶

Classification trees are older than computers. Figure 1 shows a classification scheme from the botanical writings of Carl Linnaeus, who organized plants and animals in such hierarchies from 1735 on. Each bracket is a test on one characteristic, each branch a possible outcome, and the leaves are the classes. The tree is not balanced: some leaves are reached after two tests, others after five.

A node of a decision tree usually tests a single feature. Figure 2 shows the common kinds of tests. A binary test checks a condition and has the branches true and false. A test on a discrete feature has one branch per value or per set of values. A test on a continuous feature splits its domain into ranges. Multi-dimensional features are usually treated as several separate features, one per dimension. Many algorithms use binary trees only, but any number of branches is possible.

Figure 3 shows a small tree that decides whether a customer is credit-worthy. A new example follows the tests from the root until it reaches a leaf, and the label of the leaf is the prediction. A customer aged 30 is credit-worthy after one test; a customer aged 70 needs a second test on the credit score. Learning a tree means finding tests that lead from the features of the training examples to their labels with as few tests as possible, a preference for simple trees that follows Occam’s razor (Over- and Underfitting).

Every path from the root to a leaf is a rule, the conjunction of the tests along the path. The tree in Figure 3 gives five rules, for example “if age<25age < 25age<25 and the customer is a student, then yes” and “if age≥65age \ge 65age≥65 and score<75score < 75score<75, then no”. The rules are mutually exclusive, since every example follows exactly one path. A rule compiler can turn them into plain program code, for example a function with five if statements, which runs faster than traversing a tree data structure.

Learning a Tree with ID3¶

Ross Quinlan’s ID3 algorithm (1986) builds a tree from the top down. It chooses the feature whose test best separates the classes, splits the training examples by the values of that feature, and repeats the procedure for each subset until a subset contains a single class or no features are left. Each feature is used at most once on a path, so the depth of the tree is limited by the number of features.

To measure how well a feature separates the classes, ID3 uses the entropy of the labels, the uncertainty about the class of a randomly drawn example, which Information Theory explains in detail. Since ID3 chooses the feature with the maximum gain, the base of the logarithm does not matter: another base scales all gains by the same factor. If a value vvv does not occur in a subset, the branch for it receives the most common label of its parent node.

Continuous Features and the Gain Ratio¶

Quinlan’s C4.5 algorithm (1993) extends ID3 in several ways and became one of the most widely used classifiers; a survey of data mining algorithms in 2008 ranked it first. Two extensions matter most.

Continuous features are split with a threshold. C4.5 sorts the training examples by the feature value and considers a threshold between each pair of neighbors with different labels, typically at the midpoint. Each candidate is scored with the information gain of the resulting binary split. Unlike a discrete feature, a continuous feature may be tested again further down the tree, with a different threshold.

Example: Splitting a continuous temperature

Six days have the temperatures 40, 48, 60, 72, 80, 90 and the labels No, No, Yes, Yes, Yes, No, with an entropy of 1 bit. The label changes between 48 and 60 and between 80 and 90, so the candidate thresholds are 54 and 85. The split at 54 separates two “No” days from four days with three “Yes” and gains 0.459 bits; the split at 85 separates five days with three “Yes” from one “No” day and gains 0.191. C4.5 chooses the threshold 54.

The gain ratio corrects a bias of the information gain toward features with many values. A feature such as a date has a different value on every day, splits the training set into subsets of one example each, and reaches the maximum gain, although it is useless for new days. C4.5 divides the gain by the split information, the entropy of the subset sizes, which is large for features that split into many small subsets:

Overfitting and Pruning¶

A tree that grows until every leaf is pure fits the training data perfectly, including its noise and its exceptions, and generalizes poorly. Two kinds of pruning counter this. Pre-pruning stops the growth early: a node becomes a leaf if it holds too few examples or if the best split gains too little. Post-pruning grows the full tree first and then simplifies it with a validation set, as described in Training and Model Selection. It visits the inner nodes, tentatively replaces the subtree below a node by a leaf with the most common label, and keeps the replacement if the accuracy on the validation set does not drop. It repeats this until no replacement helps.

C4.5 can also prune the rules instead of the tree. It removes conditions from each rule as long as the accuracy improves. The rules are then no longer mutually exclusive, so C4.5 sorts them by their accuracy and applies the first rule that matches.

Real training data also needs preparation before a tree is grown. Examples without a label are removed, outliers eliminated, and missing feature values predicted or treated as a value of their own. Identical feature vectors with conflicting labels are collapsed into one entry with the most frequent label, keeping their count so that the entropies remain correct. Features that cannot help, such as a date or an identifier, are removed. Most of this work is specific to the domain: a good tree depends more on a small set of informative, weakly correlated features than on the learning algorithm.

Random Forests and Gradient Boosting¶

A single tree is unstable. A small change in the training data can change the feature chosen at the root and thereby the whole tree, so trees have a high variance in the sense of Over- and Underfitting. Ensembles average many trees to reduce this variance.

A random forest (Breiman 2001) trains hundreds of trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the training set, drawn with replacement, and each split considers only a random subset of the features. The randomness makes the trees differ, and their majority vote is far more accurate and stable than any single tree.

The learning rate keeps each tree from correcting too much at once, and the number of trees and their depth are hyperparameters chosen on validation data. With other losses, the trees fit other gradients: the logistic loss for classification, or the pairwise ranking gradients of LambdaMART for the learning-to-rank models of Learning to Rank. Libraries such as XGBoost and LightGBM implement gradient boosting efficiently, and on tabular data with mixed feature types, gradient-boosted trees often outperform neural networks.
